1. Executive legal position – read this before applying the 28% rule

The law has moved through two distinct stages. From 1 October 2023, GST amendments specifically brought online money gaming, other online gaming and casino actionable claims into a revised statutory framework, with a 28% rate for specified actionable claims and special valuation rules. From 1 May 2026, the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 prohibits offering, facilitating, advertising and participating in online money games.

Period / activityLegal position to examine
Up to 30 September 2023Pre-amendment GST classification and valuation disputes. Do not automatically apply the post-October 2023 rules without considering the applicable judicial position and limitation.
1 October 2023 to 30 April 20262023 GST amendments, specified actionable claim provisions, 28% rate framework, Rules 31B/31C and the Supreme Court's 2026 interpretation are relevant.
From 1 May 2026 – online money gamesOnline money games and online money gaming services are prohibited under the PROG Act, 2025. Do not treat the 28% GST framework as permission to operate such a business.
From 1 May 2026 – physical casinosThe online money gaming prohibition is not, by itself, a blanket prohibition of physical casino operations. GST on casino actionable claims continues to require analysis under the CGST Act, rate notification and Rule 31C, alongside applicable State laws/licences.
Online social games / e-sportsNot automatically prohibited. Classification depends on whether the game meets the statutory definition of an online money game and the 2026 Rules' determination framework. Normal GST treatment of platform services, entry fees, sponsorship and related supplies must be analysed separately.
Critical compliance warning: The fact that a transaction is covered by a GST definition or rate entry does not legalise an activity prohibited under another law. Conversely, the later prohibition does not automatically extinguish GST, interest, penalty or assessment exposure for earlier tax periods. Period-wise analysis is essential.

2. What is online gaming and online money gaming?

The CGST Act, as amended in 2023, inserted definitions relevant to online gaming. “Online gaming” broadly refers to offering a game on the internet or an electronic network and includes online money gaming. “Online money gaming” focuses on payment or deposit of money or money's worth, including virtual digital assets, with an expectation of winning money or money's worth, irrespective of whether the outcome is based on skill, chance or both.

Online money gaming

Player pays/deposits money or money's worth and expects money or money's worth in return. The 2023 GST definition expressly did not make skill-versus-chance the decisive GST test.

Online social / non-money games

Games without a qualifying money or money's-worth stake and prize structure may fall outside the online-money-game definition. Their platform fee, subscription, advertising or other services still require ordinary GST classification.

Specified actionable claim

Section 2(102A) includes actionable claims involved in betting, casinos, gambling, horse racing, lottery and online money gaming.

Platform operator

The amended supplier definition can deem a person organising or arranging specified actionable claims, including a platform owner/operator/manager, to be the supplier for GST purposes.

Examples historically considered in the GST framework included fantasy sports with pooled entry stakes, poker, rummy or other online formats where a player deposits money or money's worth to participate for a monetary prize. Whether a particular game meets the definition depends on its actual mechanics, payment flows, prize entitlement and contractual terms, not merely its marketing label.

4. GST rate – why 28% and what exactly is taxed?

Notification No. 11/2023-Central Tax (Rate), dated 29 September 2023, amended the rate notification to insert “specified actionable claim” in Schedule IV, attracting 28% central tax (with corresponding State/UT tax for intra-State supplies or IGST for inter-State supplies as applicable). The rate applies to the specified actionable claim category; it should not be mechanically applied to every service supplied by a gaming company.

Supply typeGST analysis
Specified actionable claim – betting, casino, gambling, horse racing, lottery, online money gaming28% rate framework under the relevant rate notification, subject to place of supply, tax type and current legal status of the activity.
Platform technology / SaaS / hosting / software licence supplied separatelyAnalyse the actual service, contractual bundle, recipient, place of supply and applicable SAC/rate. Do not assume all platform revenue is automatically part of actionable-claim value.
Advertising / sponsorship / brand placementSeparate taxable service analysis may apply. Identify whether the operator supplies advertising or receives sponsorship and the correct recipient/supplier.
Food, beverage, accommodation, transport and event services at a casinoAnalyse separately under their respective GST classifications and rate conditions; avoid combining unrelated supplies into casino actionable-claim value without legal basis.
Rate computation: For a taxable value of ₹1,00,000 at 28%, GST is ₹28,000. For an intra-State supply this is ordinarily ₹14,000 CGST + ₹14,000 SGST; for an inter-State supply, ₹28,000 IGST, subject to the correct place-of-supply result.

5. Rule 31B – valuation of online gaming including online money gaming

Rule 31B provides that the value of supply of online gaming, including actionable claims involved in online money gaming, is the total amount paid or payable to or deposited with the supplier by or on behalf of the player by way of money or money's worth, including virtual digital assets.

The rule also states that an amount returned or refunded to a player is not deductible from the value merely because the player did not use the amount to participate in an event. Its explanation clarifies that winnings used by a player for a further event without withdrawing are not treated as a fresh amount paid or deposited for valuation under the rule.

TransactionRule 31B treatment
Player deposits ₹1,000 into gaming walletIncluded in the statutory deposit measure where deposited with the supplier for the covered online gaming supply.
Player uses only ₹300 in a game and leaves ₹700 unusedRule 31B's measure is not simply the amount staked in one round; the total qualifying deposit is the statutory reference.
Unused amount of ₹700 is refundedThe rule says refund is not deductible from the value. Refund treatment must be assessed under the express rule and relevant facts.
Player wins ₹500 and reuses winnings in another round without withdrawalThe explanation prevents the same recycled winnings from being treated as a fresh deposit for Rule 31B valuation.
Player withdraws winnings and later makes a new depositAssess the later deposit as a new payment/deposit under the rule; maintain a complete wallet ledger to distinguish recycled balances and fresh deposits.
Do not use “gross gaming revenue” or platform commission as a substitute for Rule 31B value for a period and transaction governed by the statutory measure. The Supreme Court's 2026 decision discussed the distinction between the deposit measure and the operator's retained platform fee.

6. Rule 31C – valuation of actionable claims in casinos

Rule 31C prescribes the value of casino actionable claims by reference to the total amount paid or payable by or on behalf of the player for:

  1. Purchase of tokens, chips, coins or tickets, by whatever name called, for use in the casino; or
  2. Participation in a casino event where such tokens, chips, coins or tickets are not required.

The rule says an amount returned or refunded by the casino, including on return of chips/tokens or otherwise, is not deductible from the value. Its explanation clarifies that winnings reused in another event without withdrawal are not treated as a fresh deposit for this purpose.

Casino transactionValuation point
Player purchases chips worth ₹20,000₹20,000 is the starting statutory value under Rule 31C.
Player returns unused chips worth ₹5,000The express proviso says the returned/refunded amount is not deductible from value.
Player wins ₹8,000 and reuses winnings in another game without withdrawalDo not count the same recycled winnings as a fresh amount paid for another event under the explanation.
Player pays entry/participation amount where chips are not requiredAmount paid for participation is included under Rule 31C(ii).
Player pays separately for hotel, restaurant, ferry or entertainment serviceAnalyse the separate supply and its own GST treatment; maintain separate billing and revenue ledgers.
Casino operator control: Maintain chip issue and redemption records, player-wise wallet/chip ledgers, complimentary chip registers, event-entry records, refunds, winnings, redeemed balances and reconciliation with cash/card/UPI receipts.

8. Supreme Court – DG GST Intelligence v. Gameskraft Technologies (27 May 2026)

The Supreme Court's reported judgment in Directorate General of Goods and Services Tax Intelligence (HQS) & Ors. v. Gameskraft Technologies Pvt. Ltd. & Ors., dated 27 May 2026, is a major development for legacy online gaming and casino GST disputes.

Key principles reported in the decision

  • The Court examined whether organised gaming arrangements involving stakes placed on uncertain outcomes amount to actionable-claim supplies for GST purposes.
  • It held that the existence of skill in a game does not, by itself, take a stake-based transaction outside the GST framework where money or money's worth is staked on uncertain outcomes.
  • For online gaming transactions governed by Rule 31B, the statutory valuation measure is linked to the amount deposited with the supplier, not merely the platform fee or commission retained.
  • Winnings or prize pools are not generally deductible from the statutory measure merely because they are paid out, subject to the specific explanation for winnings reused without withdrawal.
  • The 2023 amendments, including Rules 31B and 31C, were treated as clarificatory/explanatory and retrospective in the manner stated in the judgment.
  • For casino transactions, the Court upheld the use of valuation methodologies under the earlier framework where records were inadequate, but required final computation to be aligned with Rule 31C principles; factual objections to quantification remained open.
Practical impact for legacy notices A taxpayer cannot rely only on the argument that a game involved skill or that only the operator's platform fee was revenue. The exact period, game mechanics, deposits, recycled winnings, refund flows, statutory provisions and factual quantification must be tested against the judgment.

The judgment does not authorise online money gaming after the separate statutory prohibition came into force on 1 May 2026. The GST judgment concerns the tax framework and disputes; the PROG Act separately governs whether the activity may lawfully be offered.

9. Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 – prohibition from 1 May 2026

The Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 (Act No. 32 of 2025) was enacted on 22 August 2025. The Central Government appointed 1 May 2026 as its commencement date. The Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Rules, 2026 also came into force on 1 May 2026.

ProvisionCore effect
Section 5No person shall offer, aid, abet, induce or otherwise engage in offering an online money game or online money gaming service.
Section 6Prohibits advertisements directly or indirectly promoting or inducing a person to play an online money game.
Section 7Prohibits transfer of funds in relation to online money games, including facilitation through banks/payment systems as prescribed by the Act.
Section 8Provides for establishment of the Online Gaming Authority.
Sections 9–12Provide offences, penalties and related liability for contraventions.
Section 14Provides for blocking access to online money gaming services.

The Act applies across India and also addresses online money gaming services offered within India or operated from outside India. The prohibition is not limited to games of chance: the Act's definition and prohibition are designed to cover online money games regardless of whether the game involves skill, chance or a combination, where the statutory conditions are met.

Do not continue or facilitate an online money game based on an old GST registration or GST rate entry. The business must obtain specialist advice on winding down, user balances, refunds, contractual obligations, tax returns, employee/vendor settlements, records and regulatory reporting. This article is not a legal opinion on any individual platform's classification.

11. Casinos, horse racing and offline betting – continuing GST questions

The PROG Act is directed at online games and online money gaming services. It should not be read as a blanket repeal of GST provisions relating to physical casinos, horse racing, lotteries or other specified actionable claims. These activities continue to require analysis under GST law and the relevant State/sector regulatory framework.

Physical casino accounting and GST areas

  • Chip/token sale and redemption controls under Rule 31C.
  • Amounts paid for participation where chips or tokens are not required.
  • Winnings reused in further play without withdrawal.
  • Complimentary chips, promotional credits and loyalty rewards – analyse the facts and whether consideration is paid or payable.
  • Casino hotel, food, beverages, transport, entertainment and event services.
  • Inter-State place of supply, branch transfers and distinct-person transactions.
  • Records required to support the valuation base and reconcile gaming operations with books.

Horse racing and betting

The 2023 GST framework includes horse racing and betting in the specified actionable-claim category. However, the precise valuation provision depends on the activity and location. Rule 31A contains provisions relevant to betting/gambling/horse racing at a race club, while Rules 31B and 31C address online gaming and casino supplies respectively. Do not apply Rule 31B or Rule 31C indiscriminately to every betting transaction.

12. Online social games and e-sports – do not confuse them with online money games

The 2025 Act recognises and promotes e-sports and online social games while prohibiting online money games. The 2026 Rules create a process for determining whether a game is a prohibited online money game or a permissible online social game/e-sport and prescribe registration in specified circumstances.

Revenue streamGST review

In-app purchase of cosmetic items with no cash-out or monetary prize entitlementReview whether the item is a digital service, electronically supplied service or other supply; examine redeemability and game mechanics.
Advertising revenueAnalyse advertising service, recipient location, export/intermediary issues and place of supply.
E-sports tournament organiser feeExamine event participation, sponsorship, broadcasting and other bundled services separately.
Prize money to playersConsider the applicable income-tax treatment and the regulatory classification of the competition; do not assume that every prize makes a game an online money game without applying the Act and Rules.
Classification file: Preserve game rules, user journey screenshots, payment terms, wallet functionality, prize mechanics, redemption policy, app-store descriptions, contracts and any determination/registration order. A product label such as “social game” or “e-sports” is not conclusive.

13. Offshore online gaming suppliers – GST registration and IGST

The 2023 IGST amendments created a specific framework for online money gaming supplied by a person located outside India to a person in India. Section 14A of the IGST Act addresses liability of the overseas supplier and registration/payment compliance. The CGST Act also contains a mandatory registration category for such suppliers.

  • Identify the actual supplier and whether the supplier is located outside India.
  • Identify whether the recipient is in India and whether the service/activity falls within the statutory definition.
  • Review the special simplified registration mechanism, authorised representative requirements and return/payment obligations applicable to the relevant period.
  • Review blocking provisions and enforcement exposure for non-compliance.
  • For the period from 1 May 2026, separately apply the PROG Act prohibition and rules; do not assume that GST registration permits continued online money gaming.

For ordinary cross-border digital services that are not prohibited online money gaming, apply the normal IGST place-of-supply and OIDAR/intermediary rules as appropriate to the actual supply. The special online-money-gaming provisions should not be extended to unrelated software or cloud services merely because the customer is a gaming company.

14. GST registration, returns, invoicing and record-keeping

Historical online money gaming compliance (up to 30 April 2026)

  • Determine the supplier and GSTIN responsible for the actionable-claim supply.
  • Review registration, including the special overseas supplier provisions where applicable.
  • Maintain player-wise deposit, withdrawal, refund and winnings ledgers.
  • Separate actionable-claim value from platform fees and unrelated service revenue.
  • Reconcile GST output liability with payment gateway, bank, wallet and platform reports.
  • Report supplies and discharge liability through the applicable GST returns for each tax period.

Casino compliance

  • Maintain chip issue/return, player entry, gaming table, cash counter and event records.
  • Reconcile opening chips + chips sold/issued – chips returned/redeemed = closing chips.
  • Maintain records of player winnings reused without withdrawal and avoid duplicate valuation under the explanation to Rule 31C.
  • Separate food, beverage, hotel, transport, event and other service revenue.
  • Maintain State licences, contracts and location-wise GST analysis.

After the online money gaming prohibition

For a business ceasing prohibited operations, complete tax-period returns, pay tax and interest where due, preserve records, reconcile user balances and refunds, and document the date and manner of cessation. Do not cancel GST registration until all continuing taxable activities, assets, liabilities and statutory requirements have been reviewed.

16. Income-tax TDS on winnings – 194BA and the 2025 Act transition

GST and income-tax operate independently. For historical periods under the Income-tax Act, 1961, Section 194BA provided TDS on net winnings from online games at 30%, with deduction on withdrawal and at year-end in the prescribed circumstances. From 1 April 2026, the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act for the new tax year. The corresponding provision is Section 393 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, read with Rule 135 of the Income-tax Rules, 2026 and the applicable rate schedule.

PeriodProvision / rate reference
Up to 31 March 2026Income-tax Act, 1961 – Sections 115BBJ and 194BA; 30% tax/TDS on net winnings, subject to the applicable computation and rates.
From 1 April 2026Income-tax Act, 2025 – Section 393 (table entry for online-game winnings) read with Rule 135 of the Income-tax Rules, 2026; 30% rate in the applicable rate schedule.

Net winnings formula under the 2026 Rules

Rule 135 uses the prescribed formula:

Net winnings = (A + D) − (B + C)

A = aggregate withdrawals during the tax year; B = aggregate non-taxable deposits made during the tax year; C = opening user-account balance; D = closing user-account balance.

Section 393 also addresses timing of deduction on withdrawals and at the end of the tax year, and winnings wholly or partly in kind where tax must be paid before release. Confirm the precise statutory notes and rate schedule applicable to the transaction date. Do not continue citing Section 194BA as the current provision for deductions made on or after 1 April 2026.

Separate tax questions: The fact that online money games became prohibited from 1 May 2026 does not erase income-tax consequences for historical winnings or necessarily settle tax treatment of other lawful prize competitions. Determine the applicable period, nature of winnings and current law.

17. Legacy GST notices, assessments and disputes – practical response framework

Businesses that operated online gaming or casinos before 1 May 2026 may continue to face scrutiny for earlier periods. The Supreme Court's 2026 decision is relevant, but each demand still requires a transaction-level and period-wise reconciliation.

Documents to collect

  1. Show-cause notice, relied-upon documents, audit report and statement of demand.
  2. GST registrations, returns, tax payment challans and electronic liability/cash/credit ledgers.
  3. Platform terms, game rules, player wallet architecture and transaction flow diagrams.
  4. Player-wise deposits, withdrawals, refunds, winnings and recycled-winnings data.
  5. Payment gateway, bank, wallet and settlement reports.
  6. Reconciliation of gross deposits, stakes, prize payouts, platform fees and other revenue.
  7. Invoices, contracts and accounting ledgers for separate services.
  8. Relevant versions of the CGST/IGST Acts, Rules and rate notifications applicable to each period.

Issues to test in the reply

  • Correct legal entity, supplier, GSTIN and place of supply.
  • Period-wise applicability of pre-1 October 2023 and post-1 October 2023 provisions.
  • Whether amounts in the data represent fresh deposits, transfers, recycled winnings, refunds or unrelated receipts.
  • Whether the department has counted the same recycled winnings more than once.
  • Whether unrelated platform, advertising, software or support services have been incorrectly included in actionable-claim value.
  • Whether the rate, tax head, interest, penalty and limitation have been correctly computed.
  • Whether the demand quantification follows the Supreme Court judgment and leaves genuine factual objections open.
Do not file a standardised reply. The legal position may be adverse on the central valuation issue while the taxpayer may still have valid disputes on data integrity, duplication, period, quantification, limitation, tax head, interest and penalty. A properly reconciled annexure is essential.

18. Practical audit checklist and frequently asked questions

Compliance / audit checklist

  1. Identify whether the activity is online money gaming, online social gaming, e-sports, physical casino, horse racing or another supply.
  2. Split transactions before and after 1 October 2023.
  3. For online money gaming, separately identify activity before and after 1 May 2026.
  4. Review game rules, payment mechanics, prize entitlement and redemption options.
  5. Reconcile deposits, withdrawals, refunds and winnings at player level.
  6. Apply Rule 31B or Rule 31C only to the relevant activity and period.
  7. Reconcile 28% output GST with GSTR-1, GSTR-3B and ledgers.
  8. Separate actionable-claim value from software, platform, advertising and event services.
  9. Review offshore supplier registration and IGST compliance for historical supplies.
  10. Review ITC attribution, blocked credits and reversals.
  11. For 2026 onwards, verify PROG Act classification, determination and registration requirements.
  12. Apply the correct Income-tax Act and TDS rule for the payment date.

1. Is GST 28% applicable to online games of skill?

Under the 2023 GST framework, the relevant online money gaming definition did not distinguish skill from chance. The tax framework applied to qualifying online money gaming irrespective of skill/chance. However, online money games are prohibited under the PROG Act from 1 May 2026.

2. Can an online money gaming company continue business by paying 28% GST?

No. GST registration or payment does not override the prohibition under the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025, effective 1 May 2026.

3. Does the 2026 prohibition apply to every online game?

No. The Act distinguishes prohibited online money games from permissible online social games and e-sports. The statutory definition and 2026 Rules' determination criteria must be applied to the actual game.

4. Are physical casinos also prohibited by the PROG Act?

The Act is directed at online games and online money gaming services. It is not, by itself, a blanket prohibition of physical casino operations. State laws, licences and GST provisions continue to require separate review.

5. What is Rule 31B?

Rule 31B provides the valuation measure for online gaming, including online money gaming, based on the total amount paid/payable or deposited with the supplier by or on behalf of the player, subject to its express explanation and proviso.

6. What is Rule 31C?

Rule 31C values casino actionable claims by reference to amounts paid for chips, tokens, coins or tickets, or participation in an event where such instruments are not required.

7. Can refunded player deposits be reduced from Rule 31B value?

The proviso to Rule 31B states that amounts returned or refunded are not deductible from the value of supply. Apply the text to the exact transaction and tax period.

8. Are reused winnings taxed again?

The explanations to Rules 31B and 31C state that winnings used for further play without withdrawal are not treated as a fresh amount paid/deposited for the next event. Maintain a traceable player ledger.

9. What did the Supreme Court decide in Gameskraft in May 2026?

The reported decision upheld the statutory GST framework for organised stake-based gaming and applied the Rule 31B deposit measure to online gaming. It also addressed retrospective operation of the 2023 amendments and casino valuation under Rule 31C. The exact findings and period must be checked against the judgment.

10. Does the 2026 Act remove GST liability for periods before 1 May 2026?

No automatic extinguishment follows from the prohibition. Historical tax, interest, penalty, return and assessment issues must be examined under the law applicable to those periods.

11. Is Section 194BA still the current online-game TDS section?

For payments/deductions from 1 April 2026, refer to Section 393 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 and Rule 135 of the Income-tax Rules, 2026. Section 194BA remains relevant to the earlier law and historical periods.

12. Does the 30% TDS rate mean GST is also 30%?

No. Income-tax TDS on net winnings and GST on a taxable supply are different levies with different tax bases, persons liable and timing rules.

13. Is casino restaurant revenue taxed at 28%?

Not merely because it is earned by a casino. Identify and classify restaurant, accommodation, transport, event and other supplies separately under the applicable GST provisions.

14. Can an e-sports event charge entry fees?

Possibly, subject to the PROG Act's e-sports recognition/registration framework and applicable GST treatment of the actual entry, sponsorship, broadcasting or event services. A prize or entry fee alone does not replace the statutory classification exercise.

15. What is the most important audit evidence?

Player-level deposit and withdrawal ledgers, chip/token movement records, game mechanics, refunds, recycled winnings, payment settlements, contracts, GST returns and a period-wise tax reconciliation.

This article is an educational guide updated as at 23 November 2026. It distinguishes the GST framework from the separate online gaming regulatory prohibition and income-tax treatment. The legal status of a specific game, casino, supplier, transaction or notice depends on its facts, period, applicable Central/State law, notifications and judicial orders. Obtain transaction-specific legal/tax advice before operating, paying tax, claiming ITC or responding to a demand.

Practical takeaway

The 28% GST regime and the 2026 prohibition must be read together, not in isolation. Use Rule 31B for the relevant historical online gaming valuation, Rule 31C for casino actionable claims, and the correct period-specific law for legacy assessments. From 1 May 2026, do not offer or facilitate online money gaming on the assumption that GST payment makes it lawful. Separate permissible social games, e-sports, physical casino supplies and ancillary services, and maintain evidence supporting every classification and valuation.
