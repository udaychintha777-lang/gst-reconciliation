CGST Section 117 – Appeal to High Court

Section 117 explains when an aggrieved person can take a GST Appellate Tribunal order to the High Court, the 180-day filing period, the requirement of a substantial question of law, and the powers of the High Court while deciding the appeal.

Bare Act

117 Appeal to High Court

(1) Any person aggrieved by any order passed by the State Benches of the Appellate Tribunal may file an appeal to the High Court and the High Court may admit such appeal, if it is satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law.

(2) An appeal under sub-section (1) shall be filed within a period of one hundred and eighty days from the date on which the order appealed against is received by the aggrieved person and it shall be in such form, verified in such manner as may be prescribed:

Provided that the High Court may entertain an appeal after the expiry of the said period if it is satisfied that there was sufficient cause for not filing it within such period.

(3) Where the High Court is satisfied that a substantial question of law is involved in any case, it shall formulate that question and the appeal shall be heard only on the question so formulated, and the respondents shall, at the hearing of the appeal, be allowed to argue that the case does not involve such question:

Provided that nothing in this sub-section shall be deemed to take away or abridge the power of the court to hear, for reasons to be recorded, the appeal on any other substantial question of law not formulated by it, if it is satisfied that the case involves such question.

(4) The High Court shall decide the question of law so formulated and deliver such judgment thereon containing the grounds on which such decision is founded and may award such cost as it deems fit.

(5) The High Court may determine any issue which—

(a) has not been determined by the State Benches; or

(b) has been wrongly determined by the State Benches, by reason of a decision on such question of law as herein referred to in sub-section (3).

(6) Where an appeal has been filed before the High Court, it shall be heard by a Bench of not less than two Judges of the High Court, and shall be decided in accordance with the opinion of such Judges or of the majority, if any, of such Judges.

(7) Where there is no such majority, the Judges shall state the point of law upon which they differ and the case shall then be heard upon that point only by one or more of the other Judges of the High Court. The point shall be decided according to the opinion of the majority of the Judges who have heard the case, including those who first heard it.

(8) Where the High Court delivers a judgment in an appeal filed before it under this section, effect shall be given to such judgment by either side on the basis of a certified copy of the judgment.

(9) Save as otherwise provided in the CGST Act, the provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, relating to appeals to the High Court shall, as far as may be, apply in the case of appeals under this section.

Current-law point: The CBIC GST Acts database records that the references to the Appellate Tribunal’s “State Benches” in Section 117 were substituted with effect from 1 August 2023 by section 152 of the Finance Act, 2023, brought into force through Notification No. 28/2023–CT dated 31 July 2023.
Plain-language explanation

What does Section 117 actually do?

Section 117 is the GST appeal route from the specified order of the Appellate Tribunal to the High Court. It is not a general second opportunity to re-argue every factual dispute that was already considered by the Tribunal.

In simple terms: If a person is aggrieved by an order of a State Bench of the GST Appellate Tribunal, the person can approach the High Court where the statutory requirements are satisfied. The central gateway is whether the case involves a substantial question of law.

The section therefore creates an important distinction between an ordinary disagreement with the factual outcome and a legal issue that meets the threshold for High Court consideration.

Step 1 Tribunal order The starting point is an order passed by the relevant State Bench of the Appellate Tribunal.
Step 2 Aggrieved person The person challenging the order must have a legal grievance arising from that order.
Step 3 Substantial question of law The High Court must be satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law before admitting the appeal.
Step 4 High Court decision The Court decides the formulated question of law and may deal with connected issues within the limits provided by Section 117.
Core legal concept

What is a “substantial question of law”?

Section 117 itself uses the expression but does not provide a one-line definition. Whether a particular issue amounts to a substantial question of law depends on the facts, the statutory provision involved, the Tribunal’s order and the legal issue raised before the High Court.

For a GST professional, the practical distinction can be understood like this:

Issue raisedTypical characterWhy it matters
Pure dispute about whether a particular document was actually produced before the Tribunal Primarily factual The High Court route under Section 117 is not designed simply to re-try every factual dispute.
Dispute about interpretation of a GST statutory provision Legal issue May require High Court consideration if it satisfies the substantial-question threshold.
Tribunal applies a statutory provision in a manner alleged to be legally erroneous Question of law The legal proposition and its significance must be framed carefully in the appeal.
Two competing interpretations of an important statutory expression Potential legal question The appeal should identify the exact statutory provision and the legal interpretation in dispute.
Do not label every disagreement as a substantial question of law. A Section 117 appeal should clearly identify the legal question arising from the Tribunal’s order and explain why the question satisfies the statutory threshold. The final determination belongs to the High Court.

How a tax team can frame the issue

A useful internal drafting approach is to write the issue as a question of law rather than as a general complaint.

Weak internal description:
“The Tribunal’s order is wrong and the demand should be deleted.”
More useful legal framing:
“Whether, on the facts and statutory provisions identified in the Tribunal’s order, the Tribunal was legally correct in interpreting [specific CGST provision] in the manner adopted in the impugned order?”

The exact wording should be settled by the advocate handling the High Court matter after reviewing the complete Tribunal order and record.

Limitation

180-day filing period

Section 117(2) provides that the appeal is to be filed within 180 days from the date on which the order appealed against is received by the aggrieved person.

Trigger Record the actual date on which the Tribunal order is received by the aggrieved person.
Statutory period 180 days from receipt of the order.
Late filing The High Court may entertain the appeal after 180 days if it is satisfied that sufficient cause existed for the delay.
Practical control Do not wait until the end of the limitation period to start legal review and drafting.

Example – limitation tracking

Assume a company receives a Tribunal order on 10 September. The internal tax team should immediately record the receipt date, identify the last date under the applicable limitation computation, obtain legal advice and begin preparing the appeal. The exact filing date should be calculated by the legal team under the applicable procedural rules and court practice.

Evidence of receipt matters. Maintain the order, communication through which it was received, portal records where applicable, email records and an internal limitation sheet. Do not rely only on an employee’s memory of when the order was seen.

Internal limitation checklist

  • Save the complete signed/certified Tribunal order.
  • Record the date of receipt in the litigation tracker.
  • Identify the statutory 180-day period.
  • Obtain counsel’s preliminary view on whether a substantial question of law arises.
  • Prepare the proposed questions of law and supporting grounds.
  • Keep sufficient time for approval, document collection, drafting and filing.
  • Track the actual High Court filing and case number separately from the internal approval date.
High Court scope

What can the High Court decide?

1Formulate the substantial question of law

Under Section 117(3), once satisfied that a substantial question of law is involved, the High Court formulates the question. The appeal is ordinarily heard on that formulated question.

2Consider another substantial question of law

The proviso to Section 117(3) preserves the Court’s ability to hear another substantial question of law not initially formulated, where the statutory conditions are satisfied and reasons are recorded.

3Decide the formulated legal question

Section 117(4) requires the High Court to decide the question of law and give a judgment containing the grounds for its decision. The Court may also award costs as it deems fit.

4Deal with an issue not determined by the Tribunal

Section 117(5)(a) allows the High Court to determine an issue which has not been determined by the State Benches, within the framework of the provision.

5Deal with an issue wrongly determined because of the legal question

Section 117(5)(b) allows the High Court to determine an issue wrongly determined by the State Benches by reason of a decision on the relevant question of law.

6Bench of at least two Judges

Section 117(6) provides that the appeal is heard by a Bench of not less than two Judges and decided according to the opinion of the Judges or the majority, where there is one.

7Certified copy for giving effect to the judgment

Section 117(8) states that effect is to be given to the High Court judgment by either side on the basis of a certified copy of the judgment.

8Code of Civil Procedure

Section 117(9) provides that, subject to the CGST Act, the provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 relating to appeals to the High Court apply, as far as may be, to appeals under this section.

Practical procedure

How to prepare a Section 117 High Court appeal

Tribunal order→ Receipt date→ Legal review→ Questions of law→ APL-08→ High Court filing

FORM GST APL-08

Rule 117 of the CGST Rules provides that an appeal to the High Court under Section 117(1) is to be filed in FORM GST APL-08. The grounds of appeal and verification in APL-08 are to be signed in the manner specified in the relevant registration/signing rule.

Core document Complete copy of the impugned Appellate Tribunal order.
Legal grounds Clearly drafted substantial questions of law and grounds explaining the alleged legal error.
Record Relevant pleadings, notices, replies, evidence and earlier appellate orders as required for the matter.
Limitation evidence Documents establishing when the impugned order was received.
Important: APL-08 is the GST Rules form reference. High Court filing can also involve court-specific filing systems, fees, formatting, affidavits, certified copies and procedural requirements. Those requirements should be checked with the concerned High Court and the advocate handling the matter.

Practical examples

Section 117 examples for GST professionals

Example 1 – Tribunal order involving interpretation of a GST provision

A company receives an Appellate Tribunal order interpreting a statutory provision in a way that the company believes conflicts with the language of the Act. The company does not merely state that the result is commercially unfavorable. Its counsel identifies the exact provision, the Tribunal’s interpretation and the legal proposition requiring examination.

Section 117 relevance: The proposed appeal must address the substantial-question-of-law requirement and the 180-day period.

Example 2 – Pure factual disagreement

Suppose the Tribunal considers purchase records, invoices, payment records and other evidence and reaches a factual conclusion. A party simply stating “the Tribunal should have believed our evidence” does not, by itself, explain a substantial question of law.

The appeal strategy would need to identify the legal issue, if any, arising from the Tribunal’s treatment of the evidence or the statutory test applied.

Example 3 – Tribunal did not decide an issue

Suppose an issue connected with the legal dispute was not determined by the State Bench. Section 117(5)(a) specifically gives the High Court power to determine an issue which has not been determined by the State Bench, subject to the statutory framework.

Example 4 – Legal error affects another issue

Suppose the Tribunal’s decision on a statutory interpretation question led it to decide another issue in a particular way. Section 117(5)(b) addresses an issue wrongly determined by the State Bench by reason of its decision on the relevant question of law.

Example 5 – Delay beyond 180 days

A taxpayer does not file within the statutory 180-day period. Section 117(2) permits the High Court to entertain the appeal after the period if the Court is satisfied that sufficient cause existed for the delay. The existence and adequacy of the cause must be established before the Court.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions on Section 117

1. Is every GST Tribunal order automatically appealable to the High Court?

No. Section 117 requires the High Court to be satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law. The section therefore does not create an unrestricted appeal on every factual or legal disagreement.

2. What is the time limit under Section 117?

The statutory period is 180 days from the date on which the impugned order is received by the aggrieved person. The High Court may entertain a delayed appeal where it is satisfied that there was sufficient cause for the delay.

3. Which form is prescribed for an appeal to the High Court?

Rule 117 of the CGST Rules provides for FORM GST APL-08 for an appeal to the High Court under Section 117(1).

4. Can the High Court consider another substantial question of law?

Yes. The proviso to Section 117(3) preserves the Court’s power to hear another substantial question of law not initially formulated, where the statutory conditions are met and reasons are recorded.

5. Can the High Court decide an issue that the Tribunal did not decide?

Section 117(5)(a) expressly permits the High Court to determine an issue which has not been determined by the State Bench, within the scope of the section.

6. Can the High Court deal with an issue wrongly determined by the Tribunal?

Section 117(5)(b) permits the High Court to determine an issue wrongly determined by the State Bench by reason of a decision on the relevant question of law.

7. How many judges hear a Section 117 appeal?

Section 117(6) provides for a Bench of not less than two Judges of the High Court.

8. Does filing a High Court appeal automatically mean the GST demand is not payable?

Section 119 specifically deals with sums due notwithstanding an appeal to the High Court or Supreme Court. Filing an appeal should therefore not be treated as an automatic stay of every consequence of the underlying order. Separate legal and procedural steps may be relevant.

9. Does Section 117 itself prescribe the court fee?

Section 117 sets the statutory appeal framework, but High Court filing can involve court-specific procedural and fee requirements. These should be verified for the concerned High Court and case.

10. Can a CA represent the company in the High Court merely because Section 116 permits authorised representation?

Section 116 concerns appearance before GST officers, the Appellate Authority and the Appellate Tribunal. Representation before a High Court is governed by the applicable court and legal-practice rules. Section 116 should not be read as a general High Court representation provision.
