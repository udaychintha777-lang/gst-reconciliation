Section 158 of the CGST Act, 2017

Disclosure of information by a public servant. Understand GST taxpayer-information confidentiality, the court-related protection in Section 158(2), the statutory disclosure exceptions in Section 158(3), and the relationship with Section 158A.

1. Section 158 at a Glance

Basic ruleInformation contained in GST statements, returns, accounts, documents, evidence and proceedings is generally protected from disclosure, subject to statutory exceptions.
Protection for officersSub-section (2) restricts a court from requiring a covered officer to produce or give evidence about protected particulars, except where sub-section (3) permits disclosure.
ExceptionsSub-section (3) lists situations where disclosure is permitted, including prosecution, GST administration, recovery, audit, inquiries, other tax laws, system operation and public-interest class-level publication.
In simple language: Section 158 is a confidentiality provision. GST records are not meant to become freely available public information merely because they are in the possession of a GST officer. Disclosure is restricted, but the law itself identifies important situations in which information may be disclosed.

3. Statutory Text of Section 158

158. Disclosure of information by a public servant.—

(1) All particulars contained in any statement made, return furnished or accounts or documents produced in accordance with this Act, or in any record of evidence given in the course of any proceedings under this Act (other than proceedings before a criminal court), or in any record of any proceedings under this Act shall, save as provided in sub-section (3), not be disclosed.

(2) Notwithstanding anything contained in the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, no court shall, save as otherwise provided in sub-section (3), require any officer appointed or authorised under this Act to produce before it or to give evidence before it in respect of particulars referred to in sub-section (1).

(3) Nothing contained in this section shall apply to the disclosure of,—
(a) any particulars in respect of any statement, return, accounts, documents, evidence, affidavit or deposition, for the purpose of any prosecution under the Indian Penal Code or the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, or any other law for the time being in force; or
(b) any particulars to the Central Government or the State Government or to any person acting in the implementation of this Act, for the purposes of carrying out the objects of this Act; or
(c) any particulars when such disclosure is occasioned by the lawful exercise under this Act of any process for the service of any notice or recovery of any demand; or
(d) any particulars to a civil court in any suit or proceedings, to which the Government or any authority under this Act is a party, which relates to any matter arising out of any proceedings under this Act or under any other law for the time being in force authorising any such authority to exercise any powers thereunder; or
(e) any particulars to any officer appointed for the purpose of audit of tax receipts or refunds of the tax imposed by this Act; or
(f) any particulars where such particulars are relevant for the purposes of any inquiry into the conduct of any officer appointed or authorised under this Act, to any person or persons appointed as an inquiry officer under any law for the time being in force; or
(g) any such particulars to an officer of the Central Government or of any State Government, as may be necessary for the purpose of enabling that Government to levy or realise any tax or duty; or
(h) any particulars when such disclosure is occasioned by the lawful exercise by a public servant or any other statutory authority, of his or its powers under any law for the time being in force; or
(i) any particulars relevant to any inquiry into a charge of misconduct in connection with any proceedings under this Act against a practising advocate, a tax practitioner, a practising cost accountant, a practising chartered accountant, a practising company secretary to the authority empowered to take disciplinary action against the members practising the profession of a legal practitioner, a cost accountant, a chartered accountant or a company secretary, as the case may be; or
(j) any particulars to any agency appointed for the purposes of data entry on any automated system or for the purpose of operating, upgrading or maintaining any automated system where such agency is contractually bound not to use or disclose such particulars except for the aforesaid purposes; or
(k) any particulars to an officer of the Government as may be necessary for the purposes of any other law for the time being in force; or
(l) any information relating to any class of taxable persons or class of transactions for publication, if, in the opinion of the Commissioner, it is desirable in the public interest, to publish such information.
Current-law note: The Central CGST Act text contains Section 158(1)–(3). Section 158A was inserted separately after Section 158 and became effective from 1 October 2023. Section 158A is therefore a separate consent-based information-sharing provision and should not be confused with Section 158 itself.

4. Section 158 in Simple Language

Section 158 starts with a confidentiality rule: specified particulars held by the GST administration are not to be disclosed except where the law permits disclosure.

The provision covers information found in statements, returns, accounts, documents, records of evidence and records of proceedings. This means the protection is wider than just a taxpayer's GST return.

Why does GST law need confidentiality?

  • GST authorities receive commercially sensitive financial information.
  • Returns can contain turnover, tax, customer, supplier and transaction details.
  • Investigations can contain statements and evidentiary material.
  • Businesses may submit contracts, ledgers and other confidential records during proceedings.
  • Unrestricted disclosure could expose information beyond the statutory purpose for which it was obtained.
Core idea: Section 158 does not say “never disclose”. It says “do not disclose unless a statutory exception applies.”

The phrase “particulars” is important. Section 158 is concerned with the information contained in these records, not simply the physical possession of a document.

6. Section 158(2) – Protection Before a Court

Section 158(2) provides that, notwithstanding the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, a court shall not, except as otherwise provided in Section 158(3), require an officer appointed or authorised under the CGST Act to produce or give evidence before it concerning the particulars referred to in Section 158(1).

What does this mean?

The provision gives a statutory evidentiary protection to covered officers in relation to protected particulars. But the protection is expressly subject to the exceptions in sub-section (3).

Important: Section 158(2) should not be interpreted as saying that GST officers can never produce records before a court. The exceptions in Section 158(3) are part of the same statutory scheme.

7. Exceptions Under Section 158(3)

Sub-section (3) is the most practically important part after the general confidentiality rule. It lists situations in which the confidentiality restriction does not apply.

ClausePermitted disclosure – simplified
(a)For prosecution under the IPC, Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, or any other law in force.
(b)To Central/State Government or persons implementing the CGST Act, for carrying out the Act's objects.
(c)Where disclosure occurs through lawful service of a notice or recovery of a demand under the Act.
(d)To a civil court in specified suits/proceedings where Government or a GST authority is a party and the matter arises from relevant proceedings.
(e)To an officer appointed to audit tax receipts or refunds.
(f)For inquiry into the conduct of a GST officer, to the appointed inquiry officer.
(g)To Central/State Government officers where necessary to levy or realise tax or duty.
(h)Where disclosure results from lawful exercise of statutory powers under another law.
(i)For specified professional misconduct inquiries involving advocates, tax practitioners, cost accountants, chartered accountants or company secretaries.
(j)To contracted agencies operating, maintaining or upgrading automated systems, subject to contractual non-disclosure restrictions.
(k)To a Government officer where necessary for purposes of another law in force.
(l)Class-level information about taxable persons or transactions where the Commissioner considers publication desirable in the public interest.

8. Disclosure for Prosecution

Clause 158(3)(a) permits disclosure of specified particulars for the purpose of prosecution under the IPC, the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, or any other law for the time being in force.

This exception recognises that confidentiality cannot prevent lawful use of relevant evidence in criminal or other statutory prosecution where the provision permits it.

Example: During a GST investigation, records relevant to an offence under another applicable law are identified. Where the statutory conditions of clause (a) are satisfied, the confidentiality restriction does not prevent the permitted disclosure for prosecution.

9. Disclosure for GST Administration

Clause 158(3)(b) allows disclosure to the Central Government, State Government or persons acting in implementation of the CGST Act for carrying out the objects of the Act.

This allows the GST system to function as an administrative framework rather than treating every internal exchange of information between authorised functionaries as prohibited.

SituationWhy the exception matters
GST administrationRelevant information may need to move between authorised functionaries.
Implementation of the ActOperational work requires access to information for lawful statutory purposes.
Inter-government coordinationTax administration may involve Central and State authorities.

10. Notice and Recovery

Clause 158(3)(c) permits disclosure where the disclosure is occasioned by lawful exercise under the CGST Act of a process for service of a notice or recovery of a demand.

For example, information may need to be used in the lawful process of serving a notice or recovering an amount due under GST law.

Practical point: The exception is tied to the lawful exercise of statutory powers. It should not be read as permission for unrelated or informal disclosure.

11. Audit, Inquiry and Other Tax Purposes

Several clauses specifically address oversight and statutory coordination.

  • Audit: Clause (e) permits disclosure to an officer appointed to audit tax receipts or refunds.
  • Officer conduct inquiry: Clause (f) permits relevant disclosure to an inquiry officer.
  • Other tax/duty collection: Clause (g) permits disclosure to Central/State Government officers where necessary to levy or realise tax or duty.
  • Other statutory powers: Clause (h) recognises disclosure arising from lawful exercise of powers by a public servant or statutory authority under another law.
  • Professional disciplinary inquiry: Clause (i) covers specified misconduct inquiries involving regulated tax/legal/accounting professionals.

12. Automated Systems and Service Providers

Clause 158(3)(j) is particularly relevant in a technology-driven tax administration. It permits disclosure to an agency appointed for data entry or for operating, upgrading or maintaining an automated system, provided the agency is contractually bound not to use or disclose the particulars except for those purposes.

Why the contractual condition matters

  • The service provider is not given unrestricted freedom to use the data.
  • The contractual restriction is specifically mentioned in the statute.
  • System access should be limited to the authorised purpose.
  • Businesses handling GST data should also maintain appropriate access controls and confidentiality arrangements.
Example: A technology agency is engaged to maintain an automated GST information system. Access to taxpayer particulars is necessary for system maintenance, but the contractual confidentiality restriction limits use or disclosure to the specified purpose.

13. Public-Interest Class-Level Publication

Clause 158(3)(l) allows publication of information relating to a class of taxable persons or class of transactions where the Commissioner considers publication desirable in the public interest.

This is materially different from casually publishing an identifiable taxpayer's confidential return. The statutory wording refers to class-level information.

ConceptPractical distinction
Individual identifiable informationGenerally protected unless a statutory exception applies.
Class of taxable personsMay be published under clause (l) if the Commissioner considers it desirable in the public interest.
Class of transactionsMay similarly be published under the statutory public-interest exception.
Do not confuse Section 158(3)(l) with Section 159. Section 159 separately deals with publication of a person's name and particulars in specified cases.

14. Section 158 and Section 158A

Section 158A is a separate provision inserted after Section 158 by the Finance Act, 2023 and made effective from 1 October 2023. It deals with consent-based sharing of information furnished by a taxable person.

Section 158A allows specified details furnished by a registered person to be shared by the common portal with notified systems, subject to the statutory conditions and consent requirements in that provision.

ProvisionMain subject
Section 158Confidentiality and permitted disclosure of information held under the GST Act.
Section 158AConsent-based sharing of specified information furnished by a registered person with notified systems.
Important: Section 158A does not replace Section 158. It operates as a separate statutory framework and expressly refers to Sections 133, 152 and 158.

15. Detailed Practical Examples

Example 1 – GST return confidentiality

A taxpayer's return contains turnover and tax information. A GST officer cannot simply disclose those particulars publicly because they are in the GST system. Section 158 starts from confidentiality, subject to exceptions.

Example 2 – Investigation statement

A statement is recorded during a GST proceeding. Its particulars are covered by the confidentiality rule, subject to the statutory exceptions.

Example 3 – Recovery proceedings

Information is used in the lawful process of serving a GST demand/recovery notice. Clause 158(3)(c) can permit the relevant disclosure.

Example 4 – Tax audit

An officer appointed for audit of tax receipts or refunds needs relevant particulars. Clause (e) specifically permits disclosure for that purpose.

Example 5 – Officer misconduct inquiry

Relevant information is required by a properly appointed inquiry officer examining the conduct of a GST officer. Clause (f) addresses this situation.

Example 6 – Other tax collection

A Government officer requires particulars necessary to levy or realise another tax or duty. Clause (g) may permit disclosure where its conditions are satisfied.

Example 7 – Software maintenance

A contracted technology agency needs limited access to GST information to maintain an automated system. Clause (j) permits disclosure where the agency is contractually bound not to use or disclose the information except for the stated purposes.

Example 8 – Class-level statistics

The Commissioner considers it desirable in the public interest to publish information about a class of taxable persons or transactions. Clause (l) provides a statutory route for such publication.

Example 9 – Civil court involving Government

A civil proceeding involves Government or a GST authority and relates to relevant GST proceedings. Clause (d) may permit disclosure to the civil court.

Example 10 – Professional misconduct inquiry

Information relevant to misconduct proceedings against a practising CA, cost accountant, company secretary, advocate or tax practitioner may be disclosed to the authority empowered to take disciplinary action, subject to clause (i).

Example 11 – Taxpayer asks for another taxpayer's return

A person cannot assume that another taxpayer's GST return becomes available merely because it is held by the department. The confidentiality framework must be considered.

Example 12 – Data used for unrelated publicity

An employee informally shares taxpayer information outside an authorised statutory purpose. Section 158 should not be treated as permission for such disclosure.

16. Business Confidentiality Controls

Section 158 is directed primarily at public-servant disclosure, but businesses can use its principles as a useful compliance-control reference when interacting with GST authorities and handling notices.

ControlRecommended practice
Document classificationMark sensitive GST submissions, investigation records and financial data appropriately.
Access controlLimit access to GST litigation and investigation files to authorised personnel.
Notice registerRecord what information was submitted, when and against which proceeding.
Third-party sharingVerify legal authority before sending taxpayer-sensitive information outside the core team.
Cloud/system accessUse controlled accounts and contractual confidentiality obligations for service providers.
Evidence trailMaintain an audit trail for documents shared with consultants, lawyers or authorities.

18. Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 – “GST information can never be disclosed.”

Section 158 itself contains multiple statutory exceptions.

Mistake 2 – “Any government employee can access everything.”

Disclosure is linked to the statutory framework and specified purposes. Section 158 should not be read as unrestricted access.

Mistake 3 – Confusing Section 158 with Section 152

They deal with related but distinct confidentiality/disclosure contexts.

Mistake 4 – Confusing Section 158 with Section 159

Section 159 specifically addresses publication of a person's name and particulars in specified circumstances.

Mistake 5 – Treating class-level publication as individual publication

Clause 158(3)(l) refers to a class of taxable persons or a class of transactions.

Mistake 6 – Ignoring Section 158A

Section 158A is a separate provision on consent-based sharing and became effective from 1 October 2023.

Mistake 7 – Assuming a confidentiality rule blocks lawful recovery

Section 158(3)(c) expressly addresses lawful service of notices and recovery of demands.

Mistake 8 – Uploading sensitive records to uncontrolled third-party systems

Businesses should maintain appropriate contractual, access and confidentiality controls when using technology vendors or consultants.

20. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 158 of the CGST Act?

It governs disclosure of particulars contained in statements, returns, accounts, documents, evidence and proceedings under the Act, subject to statutory exceptions.

2. Is all GST information confidential?

Section 158 establishes a general non-disclosure rule for the specified particulars, but sub-section (3) lists circumstances where disclosure is permitted.

3. What does Section 158(2) provide?

It restricts a court, subject to Section 158(3), from requiring a covered GST officer to produce or give evidence concerning the particulars referred to in sub-section (1).

4. Can information be disclosed for prosecution?

Yes. Section 158(3)(a) expressly permits disclosure for prosecution under the laws specified there.

5. Can information be shared for GST administration?

Yes. Clause (b) permits specified disclosure for carrying out the objects of the CGST Act.

6. Can information be used for recovery?

Yes. Clause (c) addresses disclosure occasioned by lawful exercise of a process for service of notice or recovery of a demand.

7. Can information be given to a civil court?

Yes, clause (d) provides an exception for specified civil suits or proceedings involving Government or an authority under the Act.

8. Can GST information be disclosed for tax audit?

Yes. Clause (e) permits disclosure to an officer appointed for audit of tax receipts or refunds.

9. Can information be disclosed in an inquiry against a GST officer?

Yes. Clause (f) provides for relevant disclosure to an inquiry officer.

10. Can information be given to another Government tax officer?

Yes, where the statutory conditions in clauses (g), (h) or (k), as applicable, are satisfied.

11. Can a software agency receive GST information?

Clause (j) permits disclosure to specified automated-system agencies where the agency is contractually bound not to use or disclose the particulars except for the stated purposes.

12. Can class-level GST information be published?

Yes, clause (l) permits publication of information relating to a class of taxable persons or class of transactions where the Commissioner considers publication desirable in the public interest.

13. Is Section 158A the same as Section 158?

No. Section 158A is a separate consent-based information-sharing provision.

14. When did Section 158A become effective?

Section 158A was inserted by the Finance Act, 2023 and was made effective from 1 October 2023.

15. Does Section 158 protect a taxpayer from every disclosure?

No. The provision regulates disclosure by the statutory framework but expressly allows disclosure in the situations listed in sub-section (3).

16. Does Section 158 prevent a taxpayer from using an appeal remedy?

No. It is a confidentiality/disclosure provision and does not itself remove the statutory remedies available for GST orders.

17. Is Section 158 relevant to digital GST records?

Yes. The section covers records and documents produced under the Act, while clause (j) specifically recognises automated-system agencies.

18. Is this page legal advice?

No. It is an educational explanation. Always verify the current Act, rules, notifications, circulars and judicial interpretation applicable to the relevant date and facts.

21. Quick Revision – Section 158

  • Section 158 = Disclosure of information by a public servant.
  • General rule: specified GST particulars should not be disclosed except as permitted by Section 158(3).
  • Protected material includes statements, returns, accounts, documents, evidence records and proceeding records.
  • Section 158(2) provides a court-related protection for covered GST officers, subject to the exceptions.
  • Section 158(3) contains clauses (a) to (l) permitting specified disclosures.
  • Exceptions cover prosecution, GST implementation, notice/recovery, civil court proceedings, audit, officer inquiries, tax collection, other statutory powers, professional disciplinary inquiries, automated systems, other laws and public-interest class-level publication.
  • Section 158A is a separate consent-based sharing provision, effective from 1 October 2023.
  • Section 159 separately deals with publication of information concerning persons in specified cases.
  • Confidentiality is not absolute; lawful statutory exceptions matter.
