GST ACT — SECTION 57

Section 57 CGST Act — Consumer Welfare Fund

Section 57 explains where certain GST refund amounts go when the law says they should not be paid directly to the claimant. This detailed guide explains the Consumer Welfare Fund, its connection with unjust enrichment and Section 54, how money is credited to the Fund, how consumer-welfare grants work under Rule 97, and the practical meaning for taxpayers and finance teams.

1. Why Section 57 matters

People searching “Section 57 CGST Act”, “Consumer Welfare Fund GST”, “GST refund credited to Consumer Welfare Fund”, “unjust enrichment GST”, “Rule 97 GST” and “what happens to GST refund after unjust enrichment” are all dealing with the same broad mechanism.

Section 57 is short, but it is important because it explains what happens to certain amounts that are required to be credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund instead of being paid directly to the taxpayer.

Simple idea: If GST law determines that the burden of tax has effectively been passed on to someone else, the refund is generally not meant to become a windfall for the claimant. The amount may instead be credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund, subject to the refund and unjust-enrichment provisions.

2. Section 57 in very simple language

Suppose a business pays GST and later asks for a refund.

The Government must ask an important question: “Who actually bore the GST cost?”

If the business itself bore the tax, a qualifying refund can generally go back to the business.

But if the business had already recovered that GST from its customer by including it in the selling price, giving the same GST back to the business could allow the business to benefit twice: once from the customer and again from the Government.

This principle is called unjust enrichment.

Where the law requires the refund amount to be credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund, Section 57 provides the statutory destination for that money.

3. Why was the Fund created?

The Consumer Welfare Fund is designed to ensure that amounts which cannot appropriately be returned directly to the claimant are ultimately used for consumer welfare.

It is therefore not simply a Government revenue account. The Fund has a specific statutory consumer-welfare purpose.

The Government's CWF guidelines explain that the Fund's overall objective includes consumer awareness and measures to promote and protect the welfare of consumers who bear the burden of GST. citeturn0search4

4. Statutory structure

Section 57 contains three basic sources of money for the Fund:

ClauseWhat it means
Section 57(a)Amount referred to in Section 54(5) is credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund.
Section 57(b)Income earned from investment of money already credited to the Fund is also credited to the Fund.
Section 57(c)Other monies received by the Government, as prescribed, can also be credited to the Fund.

The current statutory text in force confirms this three-part structure. citeturn0search0

5. Unjust enrichment connection

Unjust enrichment is the key concept behind many Consumer Welfare Fund situations.

In everyday language:

If you already recovered the GST cost from someone else, you should generally not receive the same GST amount back from the Government.

For example, a trader purchases goods and pays ₹18,000 GST. The trader then sells the goods and separately collects that ₹18,000 GST from the customer. If the trader later receives a refund of the same ₹18,000 without giving the customer the benefit, the trader could be enriched unfairly.

The refund provisions therefore contain safeguards designed to prevent such double recovery.

6. Section 54(5) connection

Section 54(5) is the immediate statutory bridge to Section 57.

Section 54 deals with refund claims. During the refund process, the proper officer determines whether the amount is payable to the applicant or whether it must be dealt with under the Consumer Welfare Fund mechanism.

The current refund rules provide that where the amount is not payable to the applicant under the applicable unjust-enrichment provision, the officer issues the prescribed refund order/payment advice for crediting the amount to the Consumer Welfare Fund. citeturn0search1

7. What money goes into the Fund?

The most important statutory source is the amount referred to in Section 54(5).

This connects the Fund directly with GST refund administration.

In addition, the Fund receives:

  • income from investments of amounts already credited to it; and
  • other monies received by the Government as prescribed.

The Fund therefore grows not only through refund-related credits but also through income and other prescribed receipts.

8. Investment income

Suppose ₹100 crore is already lying in the Consumer Welfare Fund and part of it is invested in accordance with the Government's framework.

The income earned from that investment does not become ordinary unrelated Government income under Section 57. Clause (b) specifically provides that such income is also credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund.

This keeps the Fund's resources connected to its statutory purpose.

9. Other monies

Section 57(c) allows other monies received by the Government to be credited to the Fund in the manner prescribed.

The important point is that this is not an unrestricted power to put any Government receipt into the Fund. The section links this category to the prescribed framework.

10. “In such manner as may be prescribed”

Section 57 does not itself describe every administrative step for operating the Fund.

Those details are provided through the CGST Rules, particularly Rule 97, together with the Government's administrative framework.

This is a common legislative structure in GST:

Act → establishes legal right/obligation → Rules → provide procedure → forms/guidelines → operational administration

11. What happens to a refund that cannot be paid directly?

The broad process is:

  1. Taxpayer files a refund claim under Section 54.
  2. Proper officer examines the claim.
  3. Refundable amount is determined.
  4. Officer considers the statutory conditions, including unjust enrichment where applicable.
  5. If the amount is payable to the claimant, it is refunded.
  6. If the amount is not payable to the claimant under the applicable provision, the prescribed amount is credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund.

The refund rules expressly provide for an RFD-06 order and RFD-05 advice where the amount is to be credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund. citeturn0search1

12. RFD-06 and RFD-05

In the refund process, RFD-06 is the order sanctioning or rejecting the refund, while RFD-05 is the payment advice used for the actual payment/credit process.

Where the amount is not payable to the applicant and is instead credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund, the refund rules provide for RFD-06 and RFD-05 for that purpose. citeturn0search1

For a taxpayer's records, these forms are important because they show what happened to the claimed amount and why the amount did not become a direct refund.

13. Can the claimant ultimately receive the money?

Section 57 should not be read to mean that money once credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund can never reach an individual claimant.

The refund rules contemplate that amounts credited to the Fund can subsequently be paid to a claimant where an order of the proper officer, Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court directs that payment. citeturn0search1

So the Fund is a statutory holding/destination mechanism, not necessarily an irreversible end point in every case.

14. Consumer benefit principle

The underlying policy is straightforward: if the taxpayer did not actually bear the tax burden, the refund should ultimately benefit consumers rather than create an unintended gain for the taxpayer.

Rule 97 therefore creates a framework through which money in the Fund can be used for consumer-welfare purposes.

15. Rule 97 explained

Rule 97 is the principal procedural rule dealing with the Consumer Welfare Fund.

AreaWhat Rule 97 does
CreditsProvides for credits to the Consumer Welfare Fund.

Standing CommitteeProvides for the committee structure that recommends proper utilisation.

MonitoringProvides powers relating to documents, inspection, audit, reports and recovery.

CBIC's refund rules describe these Rule 97 mechanisms in detail. citeturn0search1

16. Standing Committee

Rule 97 provides for a Standing Committee responsible for recommending proper utilisation of the money credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund.

The framework includes a Chairman, Vice-Chairman, Member Secretary and other members as prescribed.

The Committee meets as required and not less frequently than the minimum period prescribed by the Rules.

17. Grants from the Fund

Money in the Consumer Welfare Fund can be used for consumer-welfare activities through the prescribed grant mechanism.

Eligible organisations involved in consumer-welfare activities can apply for financial assistance, subject to the Rule 97 requirements.

The Government's CWF guidelines explain that the overall objective includes consumer awareness and measures to promote and protect consumer welfare. citeturn0search4

18. Committee powers

The Standing Committee has substantial oversight powers under Rule 97. These include requiring applicants to produce books, accounts and documents; inspection of premises; audit of accounts; requiring reports on utilisation; rejecting applications with material inconsistencies; recommending financial assistance; and recommending safeguards against misuse.

These controls are important because CWF money is intended for a specific public purpose.

19. Accounts and audit

Section 58, which follows Section 57, requires proper and separate accounts and relevant records for the Fund and an annual statement of accounts in the prescribed form in consultation with the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India.

Therefore, Section 57 should be read with Section 58 when considering the Fund's financial administration.

20. Consumer-welfare projects

Consumer-welfare expenditure can include activities that improve consumer awareness, understanding of GST rights and responsibilities, and broader consumer protection.

The Government's CWF guidelines specifically identify publicity and consumer awareness on GST as an overall objective. citeturn0search4

Applications are not automatic grants. The prescribed Committee evaluates the proposal and considers financial status, importance, utility and safeguards against misuse.

21. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — GST collected from customer

A trader pays ₹1 lakh GST on purchases and recovers ₹1 lakh GST from its customer. If a refund is later claimed and the tax burden has been passed on, unjust-enrichment provisions may prevent direct payment to the trader.

Example 2 — Tax burden actually borne by claimant

A qualifying claimant demonstrates that the tax burden was not passed on. Subject to all other refund conditions, the refund may be paid directly rather than credited to the Fund.

Example 3 — Refund rejected on unjust-enrichment grounds

The refund amount is otherwise determined but is not payable directly to the claimant because the statutory unjust-enrichment condition is not satisfied. The prescribed amount can be credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund.

Example 4 — Court later directs payment

An amount is credited to the Fund, but a later appellate or court order directs payment to the claimant. Rule 97 permits payment from the Fund pursuant to the appropriate order.

Example 5 — Investment income

The Fund earns income from investment of its balances. Section 57(b) provides that this income is also credited to the Fund.

Example 6 — Consumer welfare grant

An eligible consumer organisation proposes a consumer-awareness project. It may apply for financial assistance under the Rule 97 framework.

Example 7 — Material inconsistency

An applicant's grant proposal contains material inconsistencies. The Committee can reject the application under its Rule 97 powers.

Example 8 — Audit of grant

A grant recipient is required to demonstrate proper use of funds. The Committee can require accounts to be audited and reports to be submitted.

Example 9 — Incorrect refund assumption

A company assumes that every GST refund automatically belongs to it because it paid the GST. The company must first examine the relevant refund category and unjust-enrichment requirement.

Example 10 — Multiple claims

A business has several refund claims from different periods. Each claim should be separately analysed for eligibility, tax incidence, unjust enrichment and the treatment ordered by the proper officer.

22. Business and refund examples

Construction company

A contractor claims refund of excess tax. If the tax cost was incorporated into customer billing and recovered, the finance team must address unjust enrichment rather than assuming the refund is automatically receivable by the company.

Exporter

An exporter claiming a qualifying export refund generally needs to examine the specific refund category and applicable unjust-enrichment rules. Export-related categories can have their own statutory treatment.

Inverted-duty refund

An inverted-duty refund claim should be tested under Section 54(3) and the applicable Rules. The fact that ITC accumulated does not by itself eliminate the need to analyse the relevant refund conditions.

25. GST Council context

The Consumer Welfare Fund sits within the wider GST refund architecture. The legislative policy is closely connected with the principle that tax burden should not be refunded to an intermediary or business that has already recovered it from another person.

The detailed operational framework has been developed through the CGST Rules and Government notifications/guidelines rather than through a lengthy Section 57 itself.

For practical research, Section 57 should therefore be read with Sections 54 and 58 and Rule 97 rather than in isolation.

27. Common mistakes

  • Thinking every refund automatically goes to the taxpayer.
  • Ignoring unjust enrichment.
  • Reading Section 57 without Section 54.
  • Assuming money credited to the CWF can never be paid to a claimant.
  • Ignoring RFD-06/RFD-05 treatment.
  • Failing to document whether the tax burden was passed to customers.
  • Confusing the Consumer Welfare Fund with ordinary Government revenue.
  • Assuming a CWF grant is automatic for every consumer organisation.
  • Ignoring Rule 97 when analysing the Fund.
  • Ignoring Section 58 when analysing utilisation and accounts.

29. Section 57 GST FAQs

What is Section 57 of the CGST Act?

Section 57 creates the Consumer Welfare Fund and identifies amounts that are credited to it, including amounts referred to in Section 54(5), investment income and other prescribed receipts.

What is the Consumer Welfare Fund in GST?

It is a statutory fund intended for consumer-welfare purposes and used as the destination for certain refund amounts that are not payable directly to the claimant.

Why is a GST refund credited to the Consumer Welfare Fund?

One major reason is the unjust-enrichment principle: if the claimant has passed the tax burden to another person, the claimant should generally not receive the same amount as a direct refund.

What is unjust enrichment in GST?

In simple terms, it means a person should not receive a refund of tax that the person has already recovered from someone else.

Does every GST refund go to the Consumer Welfare Fund?

No. Qualifying refunds are paid to the applicant where the law permits. Only amounts required to be dealt with under the Consumer Welfare Fund mechanism are credited there.

Can money from the Consumer Welfare Fund later be paid to a claimant?

Yes. The refund rules provide for payment from the Fund where an order of the proper officer, Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court directs payment.

What is Rule 97 GST?

Rule 97 provides the procedural framework for the Consumer Welfare Fund, including credits, utilisation, Standing Committee functions and grants.

What is the difference between Section 57 and Section 58?

Section 57 deals with creation and credits to the Fund. Section 58 deals with utilisation of the Fund and its accounts and records.

What is Section 54(5) connection with Section 57?

Section 54(5) is the statutory source specifically referred to in Section 57(a), linking the refund process to the Consumer Welfare Fund.

Is Consumer Welfare Fund ordinary Government revenue?

No. It is a statutory fund with a defined consumer-welfare purpose and a prescribed administrative framework.

Can a consumer organisation get a grant from the Fund?

Eligible organisations can apply under Rule 97, but grants are subject to the prescribed conditions, scrutiny and Committee recommendations.

Who controls Consumer Welfare Fund grants?

The Rule 97 Standing Committee has specified powers and recommends proper utilisation of Fund money.

Why is customer pricing relevant to a refund claim?

Pricing and customer recovery can be evidence relevant to whether the tax burden was passed on and therefore to the unjust-enrichment analysis.

31. Key takeaways

  • Section 57 creates the Consumer Welfare Fund under the CGST framework.
  • Section 57(a) links the Fund directly to amounts referred to in Section 54(5).
  • Investment income from Fund money is also credited to the Fund.
  • Unjust enrichment is a major reason why some refund amounts are not paid directly to the claimant.
  • Money credited to the Fund is intended for consumer-welfare purposes.
  • Rule 97 provides the detailed operating framework.
  • Amounts credited to the Fund can, in appropriate cases, subsequently be paid to a claimant pursuant to an order.
  • Section 57 should always be read with Sections 54 and 58 and Rule 97.
  • For businesses, documenting tax incidence and customer recovery is important when analysing refund claims.
