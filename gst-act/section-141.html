Section 141 of the CGST Act, 2017

Transitional provisions relating to job work. A detailed, practical guide to inputs, semi-finished goods and finished goods that were sent for job work or manufacturing processes before GST and returned after the appointed day.

Section 141 at a Glance

PurposeProtect the pre-GST job-work treatment for specified goods that were already outside the principal's premises when GST commenced.
Main goods coveredInputs, semi-finished goods and finished goods moved for job work or specified manufacturing processes before 1 July 2017.
Core timelineSix months from the appointed day, with a possible Commissioner extension of up to two additional months where sufficient cause is shown.
In simple language: Imagine a manufacturer sent raw material to a job worker on 20 June 2017 under the old excise/VAT system. GST started on 1 July 2017 while the material was still with the job worker. Section 141 was created so that the material could come back after GST started without automatically creating a GST tax liability, provided the statutory transition conditions were met.

1. Statutory Text of Section 141

141. Transitional provisions relating to job work.—

(1) Where any inputs received at a place of business had been removed as such or removed after being partially processed to a job worker for further processing, testing, repair, reconditioning or any other purpose in accordance with the provisions of the existing law prior to the appointed day and such inputs are returned to the said place on or after the appointed day, no tax shall be payable if such inputs, after completion of the job work or otherwise, are returned to the said place within six months from the appointed day:

Provided that the period of six months may, on sufficient cause being shown, be extended by the Commissioner for a further period not exceeding two months:

Provided further that if such inputs are not returned within the period specified in this sub-section, the input tax credit shall be liable to be recovered in accordance with the provisions of clause (a) of sub-section (8) of section 142.

(2) Where any semi-finished goods had been removed from the place of business to any other premises for carrying out certain manufacturing processes in accordance with the provisions of the existing law prior to the appointed day and such goods are returned to the said place on or after the appointed day, no tax shall be payable if the said goods, after undergoing manufacturing processes or otherwise, are returned to the said place within six months from the appointed day:

Provided that the period of six months may, on sufficient cause being shown, be extended by the Commissioner for a further period not exceeding two months:

Provided further that if such goods are not returned within the period specified in this sub-section, the input tax credit shall be liable to be recovered in accordance with the provisions of clause (a) of sub-section (8) of section 142.

(3) Where any finished goods had been removed from the place of business for carrying out certain processes in accordance with the provisions of the existing law prior to the appointed day and such goods are returned to the said place on or after the appointed day, no tax shall be payable if such goods, after undergoing manufacturing processes or otherwise, are returned to the said place within six months from the appointed day:

Provided that the period of six months may, on sufficient cause being shown, be extended by the Commissioner for a further period not exceeding two months:

Provided further that if such goods are not returned within the period specified in this sub-section, the tax shall be payable by the manufacturer in accordance with the provisions of clause (a) of sub-section (8) of section 142.
Key distinction: Sub-sections (1) and (2) speak of recovery of input tax credit under Section 142(8)(a) if the goods are not returned in time. Sub-section (3), concerning finished goods, states that tax becomes payable by the manufacturer under Section 142(8)(a).

2. Section 141 Explained in Simple Language

GST began on 1 July 2017. But manufacturing supply chains do not stop at midnight before a new tax law. Raw materials, semi-finished products and finished goods can be physically located with another processor when the tax regime changes.

Before GST, the movement of goods for job work or manufacturing processes could take place under the existing Central Excise/VAT framework. Section 141 created a bridge for those goods.

The basic rule: If qualifying goods had already been sent out before the appointed day under the old law and were returned within the statutory transition period, the return itself would not attract GST merely because it crossed the GST commencement date.

Why was this necessary?

Without a transition rule, a manufacturer could face uncertainty over goods that had physically left its premises before GST but returned after GST. Section 141 essentially preserves the old-law treatment for the specified transition window, subject to the return deadline.

4. Section 141(1): Inputs Sent to a Job Worker

Section 141(1) covers inputs received at the principal's place of business and then removed, either as inputs or after partial processing, to a job worker before the appointed day.

What activities can be covered?

The provision expressly refers to further processing, testing, repair, reconditioning and other purposes under the existing law.

Step 1Input belongs to the principal's manufacturing/business process.
Step 2It was removed to the job worker before GST commenced.
Step 3It is returned after GST starts but within the statutory period.
Example – machining:

A manufacturer sent steel components to a machining job worker on 25 June 2017. The job worker completed machining and returned them on 20 September 2017. The return falls within six months from 1 July 2017, so Section 141(1)'s transition protection can apply, assuming its other conditions are satisfied.

5. Section 141(2): Semi-Finished Goods Sent for Manufacturing Processes

Sub-section (2) covers semi-finished goods that had been removed from the place of business to another premises before the appointed day for carrying out manufacturing processes under the existing law.

The important point is that the goods do not necessarily have to be with a “job worker” in the narrow commercial sense. The statutory wording refers to removal to any other premises for certain manufacturing processes.

Example – plating:

A manufacturer completed the first stage of production and sent semi-finished metal parts to another premises for plating on 28 June 2017. The parts were returned in October 2017. If the return is within the statutory six-month period and the other conditions are met, the transitional rule applies.

Accounting tip: Maintain a separate reconciliation of semi-finished goods physically outside the factory on 30 June 2017. Do not rely only on the general inventory ledger.

6. Section 141(3): Finished Goods Sent Out for Processes

Sub-section (3) addresses finished goods that had been removed from the place of business before GST for carrying out certain processes under the existing law.

These goods could be returned after GST commenced without tax becoming payable merely because the return occurred after 1 July 2017, provided they were returned within the statutory period.

Example – finishing process:

A manufacturer had completed production and sent finished goods outside its premises for a specified process on 29 June 2017. The goods returned on 10 November 2017. The business should compare the return date with the six-month statutory period from the appointed day and verify whether any valid extension was obtained.

Important: The consequence for finished goods is worded differently from the input and semi-finished-goods provisions. If the statutory period is missed, Section 141(3) links the consequence to tax payable by the manufacturer under Section 142(8)(a).

7. The Six-Month Timeline

The statutory transition period is measured from the appointed day, which for GST commencement was 1 July 2017.

The transition rule therefore required businesses to identify the exact population of goods lying outside the principal's premises on 30 June 2017 and monitor their return rather than waiting until the end of the period.

Practical control: Create a “30 June 2017 job-work opening list” with challan/reference number, date sent, description, quantity, value, job worker/premises, process, expected return and actual return date.

8. Commissioner Extension of Up to Two Months

Each relevant sub-section allows the six-month period to be extended by the Commissioner for a further period not exceeding two months, where sufficient cause is shown.

This is not the same as an automatic eight-month period. The statutory language requires sufficient cause and an extension by the Commissioner.

Do not assume: “The goods can stay outside for eight months automatically.” The additional period depends on a valid Commissioner extension based on sufficient cause.

Possible evidence for explaining delay

  • Processing records showing the goods remained under genuine manufacturing/job-work activity.
  • Technical or production records explaining why the process could not be completed on time.
  • Correspondence between principal and processor.
  • Transport or logistics records.
  • Stock statements and physical verification evidence.
  • Any official application/order relating to the extension.

9. What Happens if the Goods Are Not Returned in Time?

Missing the statutory return period is important because Section 141 does not provide an unlimited transition window.

GoodsSection 141 consequence
InputsITC becomes liable to recovery under Section 142(8)(a).
Semi-finished goodsITC becomes liable to recovery under Section 142(8)(a).
Finished goodsTax becomes payable by the manufacturer under Section 142(8)(a).

The transition provision therefore creates a strong compliance incentive to track physical goods, not merely accounting balances.

Example – missed deadline:

Inputs sent to a job worker before 1 July 2017 remain there beyond the permitted period and no valid extension was obtained. The principal should not continue treating the transaction as if the Section 141 protection were unlimited; the Section 142(8)(a) consequence needs to be examined.

10. Detailed Practical Examples

Example 1 – Raw material at job worker

100 tonnes of steel were sent to a fabricator on 25 June 2017. The material returned on 15 August 2017. The return is within the six-month period, so Section 141(1) can protect the return, subject to the statutory facts.

Example 2 – Repair of components

Machine components were sent for repair before GST and returned after GST. Repair is expressly among the purposes referred to in Section 141(1).

Example 3 – Testing

Inputs were sent to an outside testing facility before GST and returned after the appointed day. Testing is specifically mentioned in 141(1).

Example 4 – Reconditioning

Old equipment/input material was sent for reconditioning before GST. The return is within the statutory period. The business should retain evidence of the pre-GST removal and subsequent return.

Example 5 – Semi-finished plating

Semi-finished goods were moved to another premises for plating before 1 July 2017 and returned within six months. Section 141(2) is the relevant transition route.

Example 6 – Semi-finished goods returned late

The same goods return after the six-month period without a valid extension. The ITC recovery consequence under Section 142(8)(a) must be considered.

Example 7 – Finished goods polishing

Finished goods were sent outside before GST for polishing and returned within six months. Section 141(3) can apply if the statutory conditions are met.

Example 8 – Finished goods not returned

Finished goods remain outside beyond the permitted period. Section 141(3) links the tax consequence to Section 142(8)(a).

Example 9 – Extension application

A production breakdown prevents completion of the job work. The principal documents the cause and obtains a Commissioner extension. The extension cannot exceed two additional months.

Example 10 – Missing challan trail

The company says goods were sent before GST but cannot establish when or to whom. Even if the physical goods later return, weak documentation makes it difficult to demonstrate that the transition provision applies.

Example 11 – Quantity mismatch

1,000 units were sent but only 920 are returned. The company should reconcile the 80-unit difference separately rather than assuming the entire movement qualifies automatically.

Example 12 – Multiple job workers

A manufacturer had goods with 20 job workers on 30 June 2017. A centralised transition register should track each job worker separately because return dates and evidence may differ.

11. Connection With Section 142(8)(a)

Section 141 itself tells the reader what happens if the goods are not returned within the specified period. The relevant consequence is linked to Section 142(8)(a).

This means Section 141 should not be studied in isolation. For a disputed or delayed transition case, the analysis should cover:

  1. what goods were sent out;
  2. under which old-law provision they were removed;
  3. when they were removed;
  4. when they were returned;
  5. whether the six-month period was met;
  6. whether a valid Commissioner extension existed;
  7. what ITC had been availed or was otherwise involved; and
  8. what consequence Section 142(8)(a) produces for the particular category.
Why this matters: A page or working paper that says only “late job work return = GST payable” is incomplete. The exact statutory chain from Section 141 to Section 142(8)(a) should be checked.

Typical document trailOld-law removal records plus return records.GST delivery challan and job-work records.
Purpose of comparisonHistorical transition.Ongoing GST job-work compliance.

CBIC's current ITC rules explain that goods sent to job workers under Section 143 are covered by prescribed challan and reporting requirements.

Easy memory: Section 141 = old job-work movements crossing into GST. Section 143 = job work under GST itself.

14. Practical Compliance Workflow for a Finance or Tax Team

  1. Freeze the opening population: Identify all inputs, semi-finished and finished goods physically outside the premises immediately before 1 July 2017.
  2. Classify each movement: Mark whether it falls under 141(1), 141(2) or 141(3).
  3. Verify pre-GST removal: Match challan, gate register, stock ledger and job-worker acknowledgement.
  4. Set the statutory deadline: Calculate six months from the appointed day.
  5. Track actual return: Record quantity and date received back.
  6. Check extension: If returned after six months, verify whether a valid Commissioner extension exists and whether it stayed within the additional two-month limit.
  7. Investigate exceptions: Shortages, goods still outside, destruction, scrap, sale, transfer or unexplained differences should be separately analysed.
  8. Assess Section 142: For late/non-returned goods, determine the applicable consequence under Section 142(8)(a).
  9. Preserve evidence: Keep a permanent historical transition file because these matters can arise during audit, assessment or litigation years later.

16. Common Mistakes

“The six months starts from the date the goods were sent.”

No. Section 141 measures the transition period from the appointed day.

“The extra two months are automatic.”

No. The Commissioner may extend the period where sufficient cause is shown, and the further period cannot exceed two months.

“Only job workers are covered.”

Not entirely. Section 141(2) and 141(3) also address semi-finished and finished goods moved to another premises for manufacturing processes.

“Any goods returned after GST are covered.”

No. The goods must satisfy the historical movement and return conditions of the relevant sub-section.

“Late return has the same consequence for every category.”

No. The wording for inputs/semi-finished goods and finished goods differs.

“Section 141 is the same as current job-work rules.”

No. Section 141 is a transitional provision connected with the GST implementation date. Current job-work operations are primarily governed by Section 143 and related rules.

“A stock ledger is enough.”

Not necessarily. A complete historical trail may require challans, gate records, acknowledgements, transport evidence, job-work records and return documents.

“If goods physically returned, there is no further issue.”

The return date matters. If the statutory period was exceeded, the Section 142 consequence may need examination.

18. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 141 of the CGST Act?

It provides transitional rules for inputs, semi-finished goods and finished goods that had been removed under the existing law before GST commenced and were returned after the appointed day.

2. What is the appointed day for Section 141?

For GST implementation, the appointed day was 1 July 2017.

3. What is the basic time limit under Section 141?

The basic return period is six months from the appointed day.

4. Can the six-month period be extended?

Yes. On sufficient cause being shown, the Commissioner may extend it for a further period not exceeding two months.

5. Is the extra two months automatic?

No. A valid Commissioner extension based on sufficient cause is required.

6. What does Section 141(1) cover?

Inputs removed to a job worker before GST for further processing, testing, repair, reconditioning or another covered purpose.

7. What does Section 141(2) cover?

Semi-finished goods removed before GST to another premises for manufacturing processes.

8. What does Section 141(3) cover?

Finished goods removed before GST for carrying out certain processes and returned after GST commenced.

9. What if inputs are not returned in time?

The input tax credit becomes liable to recovery under Section 142(8)(a), subject to the statutory framework.

10. What if semi-finished goods are not returned in time?

The ITC recovery consequence under Section 142(8)(a) applies as specified by Section 141(2).

11. What if finished goods are not returned in time?

Section 141(3) provides that tax becomes payable by the manufacturer in accordance with Section 142(8)(a).

12. Does Section 141 apply to current job work?

It is a transitional provision for the GST implementation period. Current job work is governed principally by Section 143 and related rules.

13. Is a job-work challan important?

Yes. For historical cases, the evidence should establish the pre-GST removal, goods, quantity, processor and return.

14. What if only part of the goods were returned?

The returned and non-returned quantities should be reconciled separately and the Section 141/142 consequences assessed for the balance.

15. Does Section 141 cover goods sent for repair?

Section 141(1) expressly includes repair among the purposes for which inputs could have been sent to a job worker under the existing law.

16. Does Section 141 cover testing?

Yes. Testing is expressly mentioned in Section 141(1).

17. Why is Section 142 important?

Section 141 expressly links late/non-return situations to Section 142(8)(a), so the two provisions should be read together for exception cases.

19. Quick Revision – Section 141

  • Section 141 = transitional job-work provisions.
  • 141(1): inputs sent to job worker before GST.
  • 141(2): semi-finished goods sent to another premises for manufacturing processes.
  • 141(3): finished goods sent out for specified processes.
  • Base return period: six months from the appointed day.
  • Extension: Commissioner may extend for sufficient cause by up to two additional months.
  • Late inputs/semi-finished goods: ITC recovery consequence under Section 142(8)(a).
  • Late finished goods: tax payable by manufacturer under Section 142(8)(a).
  • Historical provision: do not confuse Section 141 with current Section 143 job-work compliance.
  • Best control: maintain a historical job-work register with dispatch, return, quantity and extension evidence.
