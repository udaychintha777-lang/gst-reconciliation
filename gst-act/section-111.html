1. Why Section 111 matters

Section 111 is the procedural foundation for proceedings before the GST Appellate Tribunal. It explains a very important principle: GSTAT is not required to mechanically follow every procedural requirement of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC).

Instead, the Tribunal is guided by the principles of natural justice, the CGST Act and the rules made under it, while having statutory power to regulate its own procedure.

At the same time, Section 111 gives the Tribunal several powers similar to those of a civil court for specified matters. This creates a practical balance:

FlexibilityThe Tribunal can regulate its procedure.
FairnessNatural justice must guide proceedings.
EvidenceSpecified civil-court powers are available.
EnforcementTribunal orders can be enforced like civil decrees.

2. Section 111 in simple language

Think of Section 111 as the Tribunal's procedural rulebook at the level of the Act.

ProvisionSimple meaningPractical effect
111(1)Not strictly bound by CPC; guided by natural justice.Procedure can be flexible, but fairness cannot be ignored.
111(2)Specified civil-court powers are available.Tribunal can summon people, require documents, receive affidavits, etc.
111(3)Orders can be enforced like civil decrees.A Tribunal order is not merely advisory.
111(4)Proceedings have judicial status and Tribunal is deemed a civil court for specified purposes.Parties and representatives must treat proceedings seriously and comply with lawful directions.
Important: “Not bound by CPC” does not mean “no rules”. The Tribunal remains subject to the CGST Act, applicable rules and natural justice.

3. Natural justice — the central principle

Section 111(1) expressly says that the Tribunal is guided by the principles of natural justice.

In practical GST litigation, this means the parties should receive a fair opportunity to present their case and respond to material adverse contentions before an order is made, subject to the statutory framework and facts of the proceeding.

What should a taxpayer's team expect?

  • Notice or communication of the relevant proceeding.
  • A reasonable opportunity to make submissions.
  • Opportunity to address relevant evidence and arguments.
  • A fair hearing before an adverse decision, where the law requires one.
  • An order that deals with the issues requiring determination.

Practical finance example

Suppose a ₹3 crore ITC dispute depends on whether 800 invoices relate to actual receipt of services. If the Tribunal relies on a departmental statement about those invoices, the taxpayer's representative should be able to address the relevant material and explain the underlying accounting and commercial evidence.

Do not confuse natural justice with unlimited adjournments or unlimited evidence. Fair procedure does not remove the Tribunal's ability to control proceedings and require parties to act diligently.

4. Tribunal can regulate its own procedure

Because GST litigation involves tax, accounting, technical and commercial questions, the Tribunal needs procedural flexibility. Section 111(1) therefore gives the Tribunal power to regulate its own procedure, subject to the CGST Act and applicable rules.

This means a taxpayer should not assume that every procedural question will be answered by simply copying a CPC provision into a GSTAT proceeding.

What should a professional do?

  1. Check the CGST Act first.
  2. Check the applicable GST rules and Tribunal procedure framework.
  3. Check the Tribunal's procedural directions/orders applicable to the case.
  4. Use CPC principles where relevant only after considering the GST statutory framework.
  5. Ensure the proposed request is consistent with natural justice.

5. Civil-court powers: what does that actually mean?

Section 111(2) gives the Tribunal the same powers as a civil court under the CPC for specified matters. This is a limited statutory adoption of civil-court powers, not a statement that GSTAT becomes a civil court for every purpose.

The listed powers cover important evidence and procedural functions.

PowerWhat it can mean in a GST case
Summoning attendanceRequire a person to appear before the Tribunal.
Examination on oathExamine a person formally on matters relevant to the proceeding.
Discovery/productionRequire relevant documents to be disclosed or produced.
Affidavit evidenceReceive evidence through affidavits where appropriate.
Public recordsRequisition specified public records/documents.
CommissionsIssue commissions for examination of witnesses or documents.
Default/ex parteDeal with non-appearance and decide matters ex parte where permitted.
Restoration/set-asideSet aside specified dismissal-for-default or ex parte orders subject to the applicable law.

6. Summoning and examination of persons

The Tribunal can summon and enforce the attendance of a person and examine that person on oath for the purposes specified in Section 111.

When could this matter in a business case?

  • A disputed transaction depends on the factual explanation of a person.
  • A technical issue requires evidence from a person with relevant knowledge.
  • A document's authenticity or commercial context needs clarification.
  • A witness's evidence is relevant to the dispute.
Practical control: If a key employee, project manager, procurement officer or technical expert may be required to explain facts, preserve their underlying records and prepare a clear factual chronology before the hearing.

7. Discovery and production of documents

Section 111(2) permits the Tribunal to exercise civil-court-like powers relating to discovery and production of documents.

This is especially important in GST because a dispute may depend on records spread across several systems:

  • ERP/Tally/SAP accounting data;
  • purchase registers;
  • sales registers;
  • GSTR-1, GSTR-3B and GSTR-2B data;
  • e-invoices and e-way bills;
  • contracts and work orders;
  • bank/payment records;
  • GRNs and measurement books;
  • subcontractor records; and
  • technical/product documentation.

8. Evidence on affidavit

Section 111(2) allows the Tribunal to receive evidence on affidavits.

For a business, an affidavit should not be treated as a replacement for proper source records. The strongest approach is usually to connect the person's statement to contemporaneous business evidence.

Good evidence chain: person with knowledge → statement/affidavit → underlying ERP record → invoice/contract → payment or operational evidence.

For example, if a finance manager explains how an ITC reconciliation was performed, the underlying reconciliation workbook, purchase register and GSTR-2B data should be preserved so the explanation can be tested against source information.

9. Public records and documents

Section 111(2) permits requisitioning specified public records or documents from an office, subject to the statutory limitations referred to in the provision.

This can be relevant where an issue depends on a record maintained by a public authority rather than the taxpayer.

Practical lesson

If your case depends on a government record, do not simply state that the record exists. Identify the record, explain its relevance, preserve any available copy, and coordinate with counsel on the appropriate procedural request.

10. Commissions for examination of witnesses or documents

The Tribunal has power to issue commissions for examination of witnesses or documents. This can be useful when evidence needs to be obtained or examined through a formal process contemplated by the Tribunal.

For businesses, this reinforces the need to identify critical witnesses and documents early rather than discovering during final hearings that an important factual issue cannot be properly supported.

11. Default and ex parte proceedings

Section 111(2) expressly recognises the Tribunal's power to dismiss a representation for default or decide a matter ex parte, subject to the statutory procedure.

Ex parte essentially means the matter may be decided without the absent party participating in that proceeding when the legal conditions for doing so are satisfied.

Practical warning: A Tribunal hearing should never be treated as “the lawyer will handle it”. The company must maintain a hearing calendar, responsible owner and escalation system so notices and hearing dates are not missed.

Minimum internal control

  • Record every Tribunal notice on the litigation tracker.
  • Set at least two reminders before each hearing.
  • Nominate a primary and backup responsible person.
  • Confirm counsel/authorised representative has received the notice.
  • Keep proof of filing and service.
  • Record the outcome immediately after the hearing.

12. Setting aside default or ex parte orders

Section 111(2) also recognises the Tribunal's power to set aside an order dismissing a representation for default or an order passed ex parte.

This does not mean that a taxpayer can automatically obtain restoration merely by asking. The taxpayer should establish the facts and follow the applicable procedure and requirements.

Practical example

A company misses a hearing because a Tribunal notice was sent to an outdated internal mailbox and no responsible person was assigned. The company should immediately collect the relevant notice, communication history, internal escalation records and reasons for non-appearance and seek appropriate relief through the prescribed procedure.

Better approach: Treat restoration as a remedy for an exceptional failure, not as a substitute for a litigation-control system.

13. Enforcement of Tribunal orders

Section 111(3) provides that an order made by the Appellate Tribunal may be enforced in the same manner as a decree made by a court in a suit pending before it.

The provision also allows the Tribunal to send its order for execution to the court within whose local limits the relevant person or company falls, with different tests for a company and other persons.

PartyJurisdiction mentioned in Section 111(3)
CompanyWhere the registered office is situated.
Other personWhere the person voluntarily resides, carries on business or personally works for gain.

This gives the Tribunal's orders practical enforceability. A party should therefore not treat the appellate outcome as merely an advisory tax opinion.

14. Judicial proceedings status

Section 111(4) states that proceedings before the Tribunal are deemed to be judicial proceedings for the statutory purposes specified there, and the Tribunal is deemed to be a civil court for specified purposes under the Code of Criminal Procedure.

The practical message is straightforward: statements, evidence, attendance and compliance with Tribunal directions should be handled with the seriousness appropriate to judicial proceedings.

Professional conduct: Finance employees who support a Tribunal matter should provide accurate records and avoid informal explanations that contradict the company's filed position or source documents.

17. Practical examples

Example 1 — Supplier invoice dispute

A taxpayer's ITC of ₹80 lakh is disputed because the department alleges that supporting supplier information does not establish eligibility.

Section 111 approach: the taxpayer should organise invoice-level evidence, purchase register, GSTR-2B, goods/service receipt evidence, accounting entries, payment records and the contractual basis for the transaction.

Example 2 — Ex parte risk

The tax head receives a Tribunal notice but does not forward it to external counsel until the hearing date has passed.

Control failure: the problem is not only legal; it is an internal governance failure. A central litigation mailbox, case owner and automated escalation should exist.

Example 3 — Government record

A classification dispute depends on a document maintained by a public authority.

Practical response: identify the document, explain its relevance and coordinate with counsel regarding a request for production/requisition under the applicable procedure.

Example 4 — Contract-heavy dispute

A works-contract dispute depends on whether the taxpayer supplied a composite service, materials, or separately identifiable components.

Evidence strategy: use the contract, BOQ, variation orders, invoices, measurement records, payment certificates and accounting entries together rather than relying on the invoice description alone.

19. ITC dispute example — from invoice to Tribunal

Suppose a business has 4,500 disputed invoices. A common mistake is to present only the total disputed ITC.

Better approach

ReconciliationCompare supplier GSTIN/invoice data with GSTR-2B.
Evidence taggingTag receipt, accounting and payment evidence.
Issue groupingGroup invoices by the actual statutory dispute.

This approach lets the legal team explain the issue while the finance team can independently prove the numbers.

21. Additional evidence — why earlier stages matter

Section 111 itself gives procedural powers to the Tribunal. However, finance teams should also understand the separate GST rules governing production of additional evidence before the Appellate Authority or Tribunal.

The GST appeal rules generally restrict an appellant from introducing evidence at the appellate stage that was not produced earlier, subject to specified exceptions such as refusal to admit proper evidence, sufficient cause preventing production, or inadequate opportunity at the earlier stage. The reasons for admitting additional evidence must be recorded, and the other side must receive a reasonable opportunity to respond.

Practical consequence: Do not deliberately hold back documents during adjudication expecting to introduce everything for the first time at GSTAT.

Finance-team rule

If a document is relevant to a disputed fact, preserve it and evaluate it during the earliest stage of the dispute. If it could not be produced earlier, record the reason contemporaneously.

22. Common mistakes

  1. Thinking GSTAT follows no procedure. It is not bound by CPC in the same way as an ordinary civil court, but it remains governed by natural justice, the CGST Act and applicable rules.
  2. Sending an unindexed document dump. Important evidence should be mapped to issues.
  3. Ignoring hearing notices. Section 111 allows default and ex parte handling.
  4. Assuming every document can be introduced later. Additional-evidence rules can restrict late evidence.
  5. Not preserving ERP evidence. Source data may be necessary to prove accounting and transaction facts.
  6. Mixing tax and legal positions. Finance should reconcile the numbers independently of legal arguments.
  7. Using inconsistent figures. The appeal amount, ledger, returns and reconciliation should agree or the difference should be explained.
  8. Assuming a Tribunal order is merely advisory. Section 111 provides an enforcement mechanism.
  9. Failing to document reasons for non-production. If evidence was unavailable earlier, the reason should be traceable.

26. Frequently Asked Questions

What is Section 111 of the CGST Act?

Section 111 deals with the procedure before the GST Appellate Tribunal.

Is GSTAT bound by the Code of Civil Procedure?

No. Section 111(1) says the Tribunal is not bound by the CPC procedure while disposing of proceedings or appeals. It is guided by natural justice and the CGST Act and applicable rules, and it can regulate its own procedure.

Does “not bound by CPC” mean GSTAT can ignore procedural fairness?

No. Natural justice is expressly built into Section 111. The Tribunal's procedural flexibility does not remove the requirement of a fair process.

Does GSTAT have civil-court powers?

Yes, Section 111(2) gives the Tribunal civil-court-like powers for specified matters, including summons, attendance, examination on oath, discovery and production of documents, affidavits, public records, commissions and default/ex parte matters.

Can GSTAT summon a person?

Section 111(2) provides power to summon and enforce attendance and examine a person on oath for the Tribunal's statutory functions.

Can GSTAT require documents?

Yes. Section 111(2) includes discovery and production of documents among the specified civil-court-like powers.

Can GSTAT decide a case ex parte?

Section 111(2) permits dismissal for default or an ex parte decision within the statutory framework. Parties should therefore take every hearing notice seriously.

Can an ex parte order be set aside?

Section 111(2) recognises the Tribunal's power to set aside specified dismissal-for-default and ex parte orders, subject to the applicable law and procedure.

How are Tribunal orders enforced?

Section 111(3) provides that an order of the Tribunal may be enforced in the same manner as a decree made by a court in a suit, with execution sent to the appropriate court based on the jurisdictional test specified in the section.

Why should finance teams care about Section 111?

Because invoices, ledgers, contracts, reconciliations, ERP records, payment records and project documents can become important evidence in GST litigation. A strong procedural strategy requires strong source records.

Can new evidence always be submitted before GSTAT?

No. The applicable GST rules contain restrictions on additional evidence and provide specified exceptions. The taxpayer should preserve and present important evidence at the earliest appropriate stage.

Is Section 111 the same as Section 112?

No. Section 111 deals with Tribunal procedure, while Section 112 deals with appeals to the Appellate Tribunal.

28. Key takeaways

  • Section 111 provides the core procedural framework for proceedings before the GST Appellate Tribunal.
  • GSTAT is not bound by the CPC in the same manner as an ordinary civil court.
  • The Tribunal is guided by natural justice, the CGST Act and applicable rules.
  • The Tribunal has specified civil-court-like powers for summons, attendance, documents, affidavits, public records, commissions and default/ex parte matters.
  • Tribunal orders can be enforced in the manner specified in Section 111(3).
  • Proceedings have judicial status for the purposes stated in Section 111(4).
  • Missing a hearing can create serious consequences because the Tribunal can deal with default and ex parte proceedings.
  • Finance teams should preserve ERP data, invoices, contracts, reconciliations, payment records and operational evidence from the earliest stage of a dispute.
  • Additional evidence should not be held back for the Tribunal; the applicable GST rules impose conditions on late evidence.
  • A well-indexed evidence file is often much more useful than a large unstructured document collection.
