Section 93 CGST Act — GST Liability After Death, HUF Partition & Firm Dissolution

Does GST liability disappear when a proprietor dies? What happens to old GST dues after death? Who is responsible when an HUF or AOP is partitioned, or when a partnership firm is dissolved?

Section 93 answers these difficult situations. This detailed guide explains the legal representative rule, continuation of business after death, HUF/AOP partition, firm dissolution and termination of guardianship or trust, in practical language.

1. Why Section 93 matters

GST liability does not automatically disappear because the legal or constitutional status of a taxable person changes.

Death, HUF partition, AOP partition, dissolution of a firm and termination of a guardianship or trust can all create a gap between the date on which a liability arose and the date on which the department determines it.

Section 93 is designed to prevent that gap from wiping out GST liability. It identifies who remains responsible and, importantly, expressly covers tax, interest or penalty that is determined after the death, partition, dissolution or termination event.

Core idea: A later assessment does not necessarily mean a later liability. The GST law can attach the liability to the earlier period and apply the special successor/representative rules in Section 93.

3. Section 93 in simple language

Suppose a sole proprietor runs a taxable business and dies on 30 June. The department later discovers that GST of ₹8 lakh was underpaid for an earlier period.

The fact that the proprietor died before the demand was finally determined does not automatically erase the liability.

If the business continues, the legal representative or other person continuing it can be liable for the tax, interest and penalty due from the deceased. If the business is discontinued, the legal representative's liability is tied to the estate of the deceased and the estate's capacity to meet the charge.

Event changes the person/business structure → GST liability for the earlier period can still survive

4. Complete statutory structure

PartCore rule
93(1)(a)If the business continues after death, the legal representative or other person continuing it is liable for tax, interest or penalty due from the deceased.
93(1)(b)If the business is discontinued, the legal representative is liable out of the deceased's estate, to the extent the estate can meet the charge.
93(2)On partition of an HUF or AOP property, each member or group of members is jointly and severally liable for dues up to partition.
93(3)On dissolution of a firm, every former partner is jointly and severally liable for firm dues up to dissolution.
93(4)When guardianship or a trust ends in the specified circumstances, the ward or beneficiary becomes liable for dues up to termination.

Each limb expressly contemplates tax, interest or penalty determined either before the event and remaining unpaid or determined after the event.

5. Death of a taxable person

Death is the first and most important situation covered by Section 93.

The analysis then splits into two paths:

Business continues

The legal representative or other person continuing the business can be liable for the deceased person's GST tax, interest and penalty.

Business discontinued

The legal representative is liable out of the deceased's estate, to the extent the estate is capable of meeting the charge.

6. Business continues after death

If the business continues after death, Section 93(1)(a) specifically addresses the continuing operation.

The person continuing the business does not get a GST-free fresh start merely because the proprietor has died. The earlier GST obligations of the deceased business must be identified and managed.

Practical example

A sole proprietor dies on 31 March. His daughter, as legal representative, continues the business from 1 April. A GST short-payment relating to January–March is detected during a later scrutiny. The continuation of the business means the Section 93(1)(a) rule must be considered.

7. Business is discontinued

If the business is discontinued, Section 93(1)(b) takes a different approach.

The legal representative is liable to pay the deceased person's GST tax, interest or penalty out of the estate of the deceased, and only to the extent to which the estate is capable of meeting the charge.

Important distinction: “Business discontinued” does not mean “no GST liability”. It changes the statutory manner in which the liability is dealt with.

8. Liability limited to the estate in a discontinued business

The words “out of the estate” are important.

Where the business is discontinued, the legal representative's Section 93(1)(b) liability is linked to the estate of the deceased and its ability to meet the charge.

This should not be confused with saying that every legal heir automatically becomes personally liable from personal assets for every GST amount. The statutory wording and the actual circumstances must be examined.

9. What if the GST demand is determined after death?

This is one of the most useful features of Section 93.

The provision expressly covers amounts that were:

  • determined before death but remained unpaid; or
  • determined after death.

Therefore, the date of the assessment order is not the only date that matters. The tax period and the underlying liability must also be identified.

Example: Proprietor dies in 2026. A departmental investigation in 2028 determines GST short-payment for FY 2025-26. The death does not by itself extinguish the earlier liability; Section 93 must be applied to determine the responsible person and extent of liability.

10. Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code exception

Section 93 repeatedly begins its special rules with an important qualification: “Save as otherwise provided in the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016”.

This means that where insolvency proceedings and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code create a specific treatment, the IBC framework must also be considered.

Professional caution: Do not apply Section 93 in isolation when the business or estate is subject to a formal insolvency process. Review the applicable IBC provisions and the GST position together.

11. HUF partition

Section 93(2) applies where a taxable person is a Hindu Undivided Family and its property is partitioned among members or groups of members.

Each member or group of members becomes jointly and severally liable for the tax, interest or penalty due from the taxable person up to the time of partition.

The liability can therefore relate to a period before the partition even if the department determines it later.

12. AOP partition

The same subsection applies to an association of persons where its property is partitioned among members or groups of members.

The practical focus should be on:

✓ Date of partition
✓ Property/business involved
✓ Tax periods before partition
✓ Outstanding GST dues
✓ Members/groups receiving property
✓ Assessment status

13. Firm dissolution

Section 93(3) applies when a taxable person is a firm and the firm is dissolved.

Every person who was a partner is jointly and severally liable to pay the tax, interest or penalty due from the firm up to the time of dissolution.

Again, the demand can be determined after dissolution. Therefore, closing the firm's books or dissolving the partnership does not automatically erase historical GST exposure.

Connection with Section 90: Section 90 deals with partners while the firm exists, including the special retirement rule. Section 93(3) specifically deals with liability when the firm is dissolved.

14. Guardianship termination

Section 93(4) applies where the taxable person is the guardian of a ward on whose behalf the business is carried on.

If the guardianship is terminated, the ward becomes liable for tax, interest or penalty due from the taxable person up to the time of termination, including amounts determined after termination.

This provision should be read with Section 91, which deals with liability where a guardian carries on business for a minor or incapacitated person.

15. Trust termination

Section 93(4) also applies where the taxable person is a trustee carrying on business under a trust for a beneficiary.

When the trust is terminated, the beneficiary becomes liable for tax, interest or penalty due from the taxable person up to the time of termination, whether the amount was determined before termination but remained unpaid or determined later.

16. Joint and several liability

The HUF/AOP partition and firm dissolution provisions use the expression jointly and severally liable.

In practical terms, this means liability is not automatically divided merely according to a person's percentage interest or profit share. The statutory recovery position must be examined under the applicable provision.

SituationSection 93 consequence
HUF partitionEach member/group jointly and severally liable for dues up to partition.
AOP partitionEach member/group jointly and severally liable for dues up to partition.
Firm dissolutionEvery former partner jointly and severally liable for dues up to dissolution.

17. GST registration after death

Section 93 should not be confused with the GST registration provisions.

Where a sole proprietor dies and the business is transferred or continued by a successor, the successor's registration and the deceased proprietor's registration have to be handled under the registration framework.

CBIC has specifically clarified that where a business is continued after the death of a sole proprietor, the successor/transferee can have registration consequences and the transfer of unutilised ITC can be considered under the business-transfer provisions.

Practical point: Death, transfer of business, cancellation of the deceased proprietor's GST registration and transfer of ITC are related but separate legal steps.

18. ITC and transfer of business after death

If the business is continued by a successor, do not treat the deceased proprietor's electronic credit ledger as automatically transferable.

The transfer of unutilised ITC is governed by the specific business-transfer provisions and prescribed procedure. CBIC Circular 96/15/2019-GST explains that transfer/change in ownership can include transfer arising from death of a sole proprietor and refers to registration, cancellation and transfer of unutilised ITC where the business continues.

Therefore, the compliance file should separately address:

✓ Death certificate
✓ Legal heir/succession documents
✓ Business transfer documents
✓ New GST registration where required
✓ Old GST cancellation
✓ ITC transfer procedure
✓ Outstanding tax liabilities
✓ Return filing

21. Assessment and recovery

Section 93 determines who bears the special liability after the triggering event. It does not itself replace the ordinary GST determination and recovery provisions.

When a demand is received, identify:

  • Tax period
  • Underlying taxable person
  • Date of death/partition/dissolution/termination
  • Whether business continued
  • Amount of tax
  • Interest
  • Penalty
  • Date demand was determined
  • Payments already made
  • Appeal/stay status

22. Construction and infrastructure examples

Death of proprietor running construction business

A proprietor dies while several construction projects are active. The successor continues the projects. Historical GST exposure on RA bills, advances, subcontractor ITC and RCM must be preserved and addressed.

Partnership dissolution after project completion

A construction firm dissolves after completing a project, but a GST classification dispute is determined later. Section 93(3) keeps the historical liability issue relevant up to dissolution.

HUF-owned commercial property

An HUF with taxable rental/business activity is partitioned. Historical GST dues up to the partition date need to be mapped before assets are distributed.

Case 1 — Proprietor dies, business continues

Successor continues the business. Section 93(1)(a) applies to the deceased's relevant tax, interest and penalty exposure.

Case 3 — Demand raised after death

Later determination does not automatically extinguish the earlier liability.

Members receive property. Historical GST liability up to partition must be identified and allocated under Section 93(2)'s joint-and-several framework.

Members/groups receive property. The same historical-liability principle applies.

Case 6 — Firm dissolves

Former partners remain jointly and severally liable for firm dues up to dissolution.

Case 7 — Demand already determined before dissolution

Outstanding demand remains relevant after dissolution.

Case 8 — Demand determined after dissolution

Section 93 expressly covers later determination of dues relating to the period up to dissolution.

Case 9 — Guardian's appointment ends

The ward becomes liable for specified dues up to termination.

Case 10 — Trust terminates

The beneficiary becomes liable for specified dues up to termination.

Review Section 22, Section 29, Section 85, Section 18(3), registration rules and the relevant successor procedure together.

27. Common mistakes

  • Assuming death automatically cancels all GST liability.
  • Ignoring GST liabilities that are determined after death.
  • Failing to distinguish continued business from discontinued business.
  • Assuming a legal heir's personal assets are automatically liable in every situation.
  • Ignoring the estate limitation in Section 93(1)(b).
  • Assuming dissolution removes historical firm GST dues.
  • Ignoring HUF/AOP partition exposure.
  • Failing to identify the exact triggering date.
  • Transferring ITC without following the prescribed procedure.
  • Failing to handle GST registration after death/business transfer.
  • Destroying records after dissolution or death.
  • Confusing Section 93 with Section 94.

29. Legislative position

Section 93 is part of the original CGST Act liability framework and has been designed to address continuity of tax liability when the taxable person's legal or constitutional circumstances change.

The current statutory wording expressly preserves the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code where applicable and covers amounts determined after the triggering event.

The practical importance of the section therefore lies less in frequent textual amendments and more in correctly applying the appropriate subsection to the actual event.

30. Section 93 GST FAQs

What is Section 93 of the CGST Act?

Section 93 contains special GST liability rules for death, HUF/AOP partition, firm dissolution and termination of specified guardianship or trusts.

Does GST liability end when a proprietor dies?

No. Section 93 specifically provides rules for tax, interest and penalty due from the deceased.

Who pays GST after the death of a proprietor?

If the business continues, the legal representative or other person continuing the business can be liable. If the business is discontinued, the legal representative is liable out of the deceased's estate to the extent the estate can meet the charge.

Can GST demand be raised after death?

Yes. Section 93 expressly covers tax, interest or penalty determined after death.

What happens if the business continues after death?

The legal representative or other person continuing the business is liable for the deceased person's specified GST dues.

What happens if the business is discontinued?

The legal representative's liability is from the deceased's estate and is limited to the estate's capacity to meet the charge under Section 93(1)(b).

Are legal heirs personally liable for all GST dues?

Do not make that blanket assumption. The applicable subsection, whether the business continued, and the estate's position must be examined.

What happens to GST on HUF partition?

Each member or group of members is jointly and severally liable for GST tax, interest and penalty due from the HUF up to the time of partition.

What happens to GST when an AOP is partitioned?

Section 93(2) similarly applies to partition of property of an association of persons.

What happens to GST after partnership dissolution?

Every former partner is jointly and severally liable for tax, interest and penalty due from the firm up to dissolution.

Can a GST demand be issued after firm dissolution?

Yes. Section 93(3) expressly covers amounts determined after dissolution.

What happens when guardianship ends?

Under Section 93(4), the ward becomes liable for specified dues up to termination where the statutory conditions are satisfied.

What happens when a trust terminates?

The beneficiary becomes liable for specified dues up to termination where the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Does Section 93 apply during insolvency?

The section expressly says “save as otherwise provided in the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016”, so the IBC framework must also be considered where applicable.

What is the difference between Section 93 and Section 94?

Section 93 addresses specified events such as death, partition, dissolution and termination. Section 94 deals with liability in other cases, including discontinuance and certain changes in constitution.

What should be done immediately after a proprietor dies?

Identify whether the business will continue, secure GST records, review outstanding liabilities, address registration, returns and ITC transfer requirements and preserve evidence of the succession/transfer.

32. Key takeaways

  • Death does not automatically erase GST liability.
  • If business continues after death, the legal representative or continuing person can be liable for the deceased's GST dues.
  • If business is discontinued, the legal representative's liability is out of the deceased's estate to the extent the estate can meet the charge.
  • GST dues can be determined after death, partition or dissolution.
  • HUF/AOP partition creates a joint-and-several liability rule up to partition.
  • Firm dissolution creates joint-and-several liability for former partners for dues up to dissolution.
  • Guardianship or trust termination can shift liability to the ward or beneficiary for dues up to termination.
  • The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code must be considered where it provides otherwise.
  • Death, registration cancellation, business transfer and ITC transfer are separate compliance steps.
  • Historical GST records should be preserved even after death, partition or dissolution.
