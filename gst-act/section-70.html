Section 70 CGST Act — GST Summons, Inquiry & Investigation

Section 70 gives the proper officer power to summon a person whose attendance is considered necessary for giving evidence or producing documents or other things in an inquiry. This detailed guide explains GST summons in simple language, who can be summoned, documents and statements, judicial proceedings, the connection with Section 67 search and Section 69 arrest, CBIC summons guidelines and the practical way a business should respond.

1. Why Section 70 matters

Searches such as “Section 70 CGST Act”, “GST summons”, “GST summons notice”, “GST investigation summons”, “GST officer summons”, “what to do after GST summons”, “GST summons documents” and “GST statement” relate directly to this provision.

Section 70 is one of the main investigative tools under GST. It allows the proper officer to require a person to attend and give evidence or produce documents/things where the person's attendance is considered necessary for an inquiry.

Simple idea: A summons is primarily an information/evidence-gathering tool. It is different from a tax demand, search warrant or arrest order.

2. Section 70 in very simple language

Imagine the GST department is investigating a transaction and needs to understand who supplied the goods, how payment was made, why ITC was claimed or how an invoice was generated.

The officer may require a person who knows about the transaction to appear and answer questions or bring documents.

GST inquiry → information needed → person/document identified → summons → attendance/evidence/documents → investigation analysis

The person receiving a summons should take it seriously, but a summons should not automatically be treated as an allegation that the person has committed an offence.

3. What Section 70 actually says

Section 70(1) provides that the proper officer may summon any person whose attendance he considers necessary either to give evidence or to produce a document or any other thing in an inquiry in the same manner as provided in a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

Section 70(2) provides that every such inquiry referred to in sub-section (1) is deemed to be a judicial proceeding within the meaning of the relevant provisions of the Indian Penal Code, including the provisions concerning false evidence and intentional insult/interruption of a public servant sitting in a judicial proceeding.

The legal effect is important: statements and evidence given during the inquiry cannot be treated casually. The person summoned should provide accurate information and should not fabricate or deliberately mislead.

4. Who can be summoned?

Section 70 does not restrict summons only to the registered taxpayer.

Depending on the investigation, a summons can be directed to a person whose attendance is considered necessary for evidence or documents.

Company director
May be summoned where knowledge or responsibility is relevant to the inquiry.
Finance head
May be asked about accounting, GST returns, ITC and payments.
Accounts manager
May explain books, reconciliations and invoice processing.
Supplier/customer
May provide evidence concerning a transaction under investigation.
Authorised representative
May attend where the inquiry permits/accepts representation.
Other person
Any person whose evidence or documents are considered necessary may fall within Section 70.

5. Why can GST officers issue summons?

The purpose must relate to an inquiry under the GST law.

Common investigation subjects include:

  • suspected fake invoices;
  • wrong or excess ITC;
  • supplies not reported in returns;
  • tax collected but not paid;
  • circular trading or accommodation entries;
  • e-way bill and movement discrepancies;
  • e-invoice irregularities;
  • supplier/customer transaction verification;
  • valuation and related-party issues;
  • refund irregularities; and
  • other suspected tax-evasion matters.

A summons should be connected with the inquiry. CBIC's Instruction No. 03/2022-23 specifically directs officers to use the power judiciously and says a simple letter seeking information may sometimes be sufficient. citeturn0search4

7. Attendance and appearance

The person named in the summons should carefully read the date, time, place and purpose stated in the document.

If genuine difficulty prevents attendance, the person should communicate with the issuing officer promptly and seek an appropriate alternative or adjournment rather than simply ignoring the summons.

Where attendance is required, the person should carry the records specifically requested and maintain a clear understanding of the transaction history.

8. Giving a statement

A statement given during a GST investigation can become important evidence. The person giving the statement should therefore:

  • answer truthfully;
  • distinguish facts from assumptions;
  • avoid guessing figures;
  • ask for clarification if a question is unclear;
  • refer to records where an exact answer requires records;
  • read the statement carefully before signing; and
  • ensure the recorded statement accurately reflects what was stated.
Never manufacture an explanation. If a fact is not known personally, it is safer to say that the matter needs to be verified from records than to give an unsupported answer.

9. Judicial proceedings under Section 70(2)

Section 70(2) gives the inquiry the statutory character of a judicial proceeding for the specified penal-law purposes.

This makes accuracy particularly important. Deliberately giving false evidence or intentionally interfering with the proceedings can have consequences beyond an ordinary information request.

The practical lesson is simple: treat every summons appearance as a formal legal proceeding, not as an informal meeting.

10. CBIC guidelines on summons

CBIC issued Instruction No. 03/2022-23 (GST-Investigation), dated 17 August 2022, specifically on issuance of summons under Section 70.

The instruction was issued because the Board observed instances where summons were being issued routinely to senior company officials and for records already available on the GST portal.

The instruction emphasises:

  • summons should be issued judiciously;
  • the power should be used for obtaining evidence/documents relevant to inquiry;
  • officers should consider whether a letter requesting information would suffice;
  • senior officials should not be summoned routinely when another knowledgeable person can provide the required information;
  • statutory records already available online should not routinely be called through summons; and
  • summons should not be issued casually or mechanically.

This is one of the most important practical references for understanding how Section 70 should operate in an investigation. citeturn0search4

11. Summoning directors and senior management

A director or senior officer can be summoned where his/her attendance is necessary, but CBIC's guidelines specifically address the problem of routine summons to top management.

The company should identify the person who actually understands the records under investigation.

SituationBetter practical approach
Invoice-level accounting queryFinance/accounts person familiar with the records may be the appropriate factual witness.
Strategic company decisionDirector/senior management may be relevant.
Technical ERP issueAppropriate system/accounting personnel may be required.
Supplier transactionPerson responsible for vendor relationship and procurement records may have relevant evidence.

12. Online records and GST portal data

One of the clearest practical points in CBIC Instruction 03/2022-23 is that officers should not routinely use summons simply to obtain statutory records such as GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B that are already available online.

That does not mean the department can never ask questions about those returns. It means the summons power should be used for a genuine investigative purpose rather than merely obtaining copies of information already accessible through the GST system.

A summons can precede further investigation, but it should not be described as an arrest order.

15. Summons vs GST notice

For example, a summons under Section 70 should not be confused with a show-cause notice proposing tax demand under the applicable demand provisions.

16. Company and employee issues

A company is an artificial legal person, while summons are addressed to individuals whose attendance is necessary for evidence.

The fact that an employee works in the finance department does not mean that the employee personally bears the company's tax liability. Conversely, if the employee has direct knowledge of transactions, that person may be a relevant witness.

Where criminal allegations arise, Section 137 and the evidence concerning individual responsibility should be analysed separately.

17. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — ITC mismatch

The department is investigating substantial ITC differences. The finance manager may be summoned to explain reconciliation and supporting documents.

Example 2 — Fake invoice investigation

A supplier is suspected of issuing invoices without supply. Procurement personnel may be summoned to explain purchase orders, receipt of goods and vendor dealings.

Example 3 — Director summoned

The director receives a summons. The company should identify whether the director has factual knowledge of the transaction and prepare the records accordingly.

Example 4 — GSTR-3B requested

If the summons merely asks for copies of GSTR-3B already available on the portal, the CBIC summons guidelines are relevant to the manner in which such a request should be handled.

Example 5 — Bank statement

A person may be asked to produce bank records relevant to tracing consideration/payment in an investigated transaction.

Example 6 — E-way bill issue

The logistics manager may be asked about vehicle movements and delivery records.

Example 7 — Statement requires exact data

An accounts manager should not guess turnover figures from memory. The person can refer to the books and provide an accurate reconciliation.

Example 8 — Unable to attend

A summoned person has a genuine medical or unavoidable difficulty. The appropriate response is to promptly communicate the issue and seek directions rather than ignore the summons.

Example 9 — Multiple summons

If several employees receive summons on the same issue, management should coordinate responses to avoid contradictory factual statements.

Example 10 — Construction contractor

The department investigates subcontractor ITC. The finance/tax team should prepare work orders, invoices, measurement books, RA bills, GRNs, e-way bills, bank payments and ITC reconciliation.

18. Construction and infrastructure examples

Subcontractor invoices

Keep subcontract agreements, work orders, RA bills, measurement records, invoices, TDS records, payment evidence and GST returns together.

Material purchases

For steel, cement, aggregates and machinery, maintain purchase orders, invoices, GRNs, e-way bills and site consumption records.

Multiple project sites

Maintain project-wise records so a summoned finance person can explain exactly which site received the goods or services.

Mobilisation advances

Maintain advance receipts, tax invoices, RA bills and adjustment schedules so the officer can understand the tax timing.

Related-party transactions

Keep valuation workings, agreements and evidence supporting the commercial basis of the transaction.

20. Practical rights and safeguards

  • Read the summons carefully and identify the person, date, place and subject.
  • Respond to a valid summons rather than ignoring it.
  • Seek clarification/appropriate adjournment where genuine difficulty prevents attendance.
  • Provide truthful and accurate evidence.
  • Do not guess when records can establish the answer.
  • Read statements carefully before signing.
  • Maintain copies of documents submitted where permitted.
  • Keep a chronology of every appearance and submission.
  • Seek professional legal advice where the inquiry concerns potential criminal offences or significant exposure.
Important: Section 70 inquiries are treated as judicial proceedings for the statutory purposes specified in Section 70(2). Accuracy and consistency are therefore extremely important.

23. Common mistakes

  • Ignoring the summons.
  • Sending an employee who has no knowledge of the matter without preparation.
  • Guessing figures instead of checking records.
  • Giving inconsistent answers on different dates.
  • Signing a statement without reading it carefully.
  • Submitting documents without maintaining an internal copy/index.
  • Deleting or altering electronic records.
  • Assuming summons automatically means arrest.
  • Treating a summons as if it were a final tax demand.
  • Failing to involve legal counsel when the inquiry has criminal-offence implications.

25. Section 70 GST FAQs

What is Section 70 of the CGST Act?

Section 70 gives the proper officer power to summon a person whose attendance is necessary to give evidence or produce documents or other things in a GST inquiry.

Who can receive a GST summons?

Any person whose attendance is considered necessary for evidence or production of documents/things may be summoned. It is not restricted only to the registered taxpayer.

Does a GST summons mean that I am accused?

Not necessarily. A summons is an investigative tool for obtaining evidence or information. The person may be a witness or may possess relevant documents.

Can a GST officer summon a director?

Yes, where the director's attendance is considered necessary. However, CBIC has instructed officers not to routinely summon top senior officials where another appropriate person can provide the required evidence.

Can an accounts manager receive GST summons?

Yes, if the person's attendance is necessary to explain accounts, transactions or documents.

Can GST officers ask for GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B?

The department can investigate the contents of returns, but CBIC Instruction 03/2022-23 specifically cautions against routinely using summons simply to obtain statutory records already available online.

Is a Section 70 summons compulsory?

A valid summons issued under the statutory power should be taken seriously. If attendance is genuinely impossible, the person should communicate promptly with the issuing officer and seek appropriate directions rather than ignore it.

What happens if I do not attend?

The consequences depend on the circumstances and the nature of the summons. Ignoring a summons can create additional legal complications, so professional advice should be taken if attendance is difficult or disputed.

Can a lawyer attend instead of the summoned person?

Whether representation can substitute for the personal attendance of the summoned person depends on the nature of the evidence required and the officer's lawful requirements. A representative cannot automatically replace a person whose personal evidence is necessary.

Can GST summons lead to arrest?

A summons itself is not an arrest order. However, if an investigation establishes a specified offence and the separate statutory conditions of Section 69 are satisfied, arrest may become relevant.

Is a GST summons the same as a show-cause notice?

No. A summons is primarily an investigative evidence-gathering mechanism; a show-cause notice serves a different statutory purpose.

What should I carry for a GST summons?

Carry the documents specifically requested, together with relevant supporting records and a clear transaction chronology. Do not carry or submit fabricated records.

Can I ask for more time?

Where genuine circumstances prevent attendance or production, communicate promptly with the issuing officer and request an appropriate extension/alternative. Do not simply fail to appear.

What is Section 70(2)?

It provides that the inquiry is deemed to be a judicial proceeding for the specified penal-law purposes. This makes truthful and careful evidence particularly important.

27. Key takeaways

  • Section 70 is the principal CGST provision for summons during GST inquiry.
  • A person can be summoned to give evidence or produce documents/things.
  • The power should be connected with an inquiry and exercised judiciously.
  • Section 70(2) gives the inquiry the statutory character of a judicial proceeding for specified purposes.
  • A summons is not the same as a search under Section 67 or arrest under Section 69.
  • CBIC's 2022 summons instruction specifically discourages routine summons to senior officials and routine requests for records already available online.
  • The person attending should give truthful, accurate and evidence-backed answers.
  • Do not guess facts or figures that can be verified from records.
  • Maintain copies and a chronology of submissions and appearances.
  • Where an inquiry involves possible criminal offences, obtain specialist legal advice early.
