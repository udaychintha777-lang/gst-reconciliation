GST ACT — SECTION 89

Section 89 CGST Act — Liability of Directors of a Private Company

Can the GST department recover a private company's unpaid GST from its directors? The answer is not simply “yes” or “no”. Section 89 creates a specific statutory route where tax, interest or penalty due from a private company cannot be recovered, subject to the conditions in the section.

This detailed guide explains Section 89 in plain language, including when director liability can arise, why the company must first be unable to pay, which period of directorship matters, the statutory defence available to directors, the relationship with liquidation and the practical documents directors should preserve.

1. Why Section 89 matters

A company is a separate legal entity, but GST law contains specific provisions that can create personal exposure for directors in defined circumstances.

Section 89 is important because it deals with a situation where a private company owes tax, interest or penalty under the CGST Act, but that amount cannot be recovered from the company.

The provision does not say that every unpaid GST amount automatically becomes the personal debt of every director. Instead, it creates a statutory test. The tax must be due for a relevant period, recovery from the company must fail, and the person must have been a director during the relevant period.

Core idea: Section 89 is a conditional director-liability provision. It is not a general rule that directors automatically pay every GST liability of their company.

3. Section 89 in simple language

Suppose Private Company A owes ₹50 lakh of GST-related dues. The department has determined the liability, but the company has no recoverable assets and the amount cannot be recovered from the company.

Now the department examines who were the directors during the period for which the tax was due.

Section 89 says those directors can become jointly and severally liable for the tax, interest or penalty if the statutory conditions are satisfied.

However, a director has a defence. The director can establish to the satisfaction of the Commissioner that the non-recovery cannot be attributed to any gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty on that director's part in relation to the company's affairs.

Company's determined GST dues + recovery from company fails + relevant director period + statutory conditions = possible Section 89 liability

Step 3 — Recovery from company

7. Which directors are covered?

The wording focuses on persons who were directors of the private company at any time during the period for which the tax was due.

This means the department and the director should not look only at the date on which the demand order was issued. The relevant tax period and the director's appointment/resignation dates need to be mapped.

8. Why the tax period matters

Consider a director who joined a private company in April 2025. The company has a GST demand relating entirely to FY 2022–23.

The first question is not simply whether the person was a director when the demand order was issued. Section 89 focuses on whether the person was a director during the period for which the tax was due.

Therefore, a director-liability working should always contain a tax-period vs director-period matrix.

This matrix helps prevent overbroad assumptions about who falls within the relevant period.

9. What does “cannot be recovered” mean?

This phrase is central to Section 89.

The provision does not say that the moment a company misses a payment, its directors automatically become liable. The statutory trigger is connected with the inability to recover the determined amount from the company.

The actual recovery facts can therefore be important:

  • Company assets available for recovery
  • Bank balances
  • Receivables
  • Inventory
  • Other recoverable property
  • Prior recovery proceedings
  • Company's liquidation/closure status
  • Amounts already recovered
Important: “Company has unpaid GST” and “GST cannot be recovered from company” are not necessarily the same factual conclusion.

10. What liabilities are covered?

11. The statutory defence available to directors

Section 89 contains an important protection.

A director can establish to the satisfaction of the Commissioner that the non-recovery of the amount cannot be attributed to any gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty on that director's part in relation to the affairs of the company.

In simple language, the director's position may be that the company's inability to pay the GST was not caused by serious neglect, wrongful conduct or breach of responsibility attributable to that director.

This is why a director should not wait until recovery proceedings to start gathering evidence. Contemporary records are much stronger than explanations prepared years later.

13. Section 89 and company liquidation

Section 89 can become particularly important when a private company is wound up and its GST dues remain unrecovered.

Section 88 deals specifically with a company in liquidation. Section 89 separately addresses director liability where the statutory conditions concerning a private company and non-recovery are satisfied.

Therefore, in a liquidation file, the tax team should examine both provisions rather than assuming that Section 88 replaces Section 89.

16. Departmental proceedings

A director receiving communication concerning Section 89 should identify exactly what is being alleged.

Questions to ask immediately

  • What GST demand is involved?
  • Which tax period does it cover?
  • What tax, interest and penalty amounts are claimed?
  • Has the liability been finally determined?
  • What recovery steps were taken against the company?
  • Why is the amount stated to be unrecoverable?
  • Was the person a director during the relevant period?
  • What factual basis is alleged for director responsibility?
  • What evidence supports the statutory defence?
Practical rule: Do not answer a director-liability notice only by saying “I was just a director.” Respond to the statutory conditions and the actual facts.

17. Recovery after demand

GST recovery is governed by the recovery provisions of the Act. Section 89 should be read as a specific director-liability provision, not as a replacement for the general recovery machinery.

Where a director receives a recovery communication, the file should therefore contain the original company demand, recovery history and the legal basis on which director liability is being invoked.

Rule 160 separately deals with recovery from a company in liquidation through the liquidator and FORM GST DRC-24. This can be relevant when Section 88 and Section 89 issues overlap.

18. Does resignation automatically remove GST liability?

Resignation should not be analysed simply by looking at the date of the GST demand order.

Section 89 focuses on whether the person was a director at any time during the period for which the tax was due.

Therefore, a director who resigned before a demand was formally issued may still need to examine whether the underlying tax relates to a period during which that person was a director.

Conversely, someone appointed after the relevant tax period should not automatically be treated as a director for that earlier period merely because the person was a director when the order was passed.

19. Independent and non-executive directors — an important distinction

Section 89 should not be confused with the separate question of whether a director actually participated in day-to-day GST affairs.

The statutory language focuses on directorship during the relevant period and provides a specific defence concerning gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty.

Therefore, the role, responsibilities, board records, delegation arrangements and actual facts can become important evidence.

Do not promise blanket immunity. Whether a particular director can establish the statutory defence is fact-sensitive and should be assessed against the actual records and allegations.

21. Construction and infrastructure examples

Large contractor with GST demand

A private construction company has a GST demand relating to subcontractor ITC and output-tax classification. The company later faces severe financial stress. The director-liability analysis should identify the tax period, recovery status and the directors during that period.

Infrastructure SPV

A private project SPV has unpaid GST after project completion. The board should maintain evidence of tax reviews, return approvals, disputed positions and actions taken to address liabilities.

Multiple project GSTINs

A private infrastructure group operates through several State registrations. A Section 89 review should be GSTIN-wise because a liability relating to one registration and period should not be mixed blindly with another.

22. Detailed practical cases

Case 1 — Company has unpaid GST

Unpaid tax alone does not complete the Section 89 test. The recovery condition and relevant director period must also be examined.

Case 2 — Demand relates to FY 2021-22

Identify the directors who were directors during the relevant FY before assessing Section 89 exposure.

Case 3 — Director joined in FY 2023-24

If the tax is solely for FY 2021-22, the appointment date is a critical fact and the person should not be treated as though they were a director during an earlier period without factual/legal basis.

Case 4 — Director resigned before order

Resignation before the demand order does not by itself decide the issue because Section 89 focuses on the period for which tax was due.

Case 5 — Company still has assets

If recoverable company assets remain, the “cannot be recovered from the company” condition requires careful examination before director liability is asserted.

Case 6 — Company is in liquidation

Review Section 88 and Section 89 together, along with Rule 160 and the applicable recovery framework.

Case 7 — Director claims no involvement in GST

Collect board records, delegation documents and compliance evidence. The statutory defence must be established on facts rather than by a bare statement.

Case 8 — CFO handled GST

Operational delegation to finance personnel may be relevant evidence, but it does not automatically answer the statutory director-liability question.

Case 9 — Tax position was disputed

Preserve appeal documents, legal opinions, correspondence and evidence showing how the company addressed the disputed liability.

Case 10 — Multiple directors

Prepare a director-period matrix so that each person's relevant period is clearly identified.

Case 11 — Company closed but records remain

Preserve GST records because recovery or determination may continue after business operations stop.

Case 12 — Recovery notice to director

First reconstruct the company demand and recovery history, then examine each statutory condition and prepare the response accordingly.

26. Common mistakes

  • Assuming every unpaid company GST liability automatically becomes a director's personal liability.
  • Ignoring the requirement that the amount cannot be recovered from the company.
  • Looking only at the date of the demand order instead of the tax period.
  • Ignoring appointment and resignation dates.
  • Giving a response that only says “I was not involved in GST”.
  • Failing to preserve board and tax-compliance records.
  • Confusing Section 88 liquidation liability with Section 89 director liability.
  • Assuming an independent/non-executive designation automatically settles the issue.
  • Ignoring the company's recovery history.
  • Failing to distinguish tax, interest and penalty components.
  • Mixing liabilities of different GSTINs and tax periods.
  • Waiting until recovery proceedings to reconstruct old records.

28. Legislative position and current framework

Section 89 was part of the original CGST Act framework and came into force on 1 July 2017. The current statutory structure remains focused on director liability where tax, interest or penalty due from a private company cannot be recovered and the person was a director during the period for which the tax was due.

The provision also retains the important statutory defence relating to gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty. The current India Code record shows Section 89 in force as of September 2026.

Current-law point: Section 89 should be read together with the actual demand, recovery history, director-period records and the applicable procedural provisions. It should not be presented as automatic personal liability for all directors.

29. Section 89 GST FAQs

What is Section 89 of the CGST Act?

Section 89 deals with liability of directors of a private company where tax, interest or penalty due from the company cannot be recovered, subject to the conditions and defence provided in the section.

Are directors personally liable for a company's GST dues?

Not automatically. Section 89 creates personal liability only when its statutory conditions are satisfied.

Does Section 89 apply to all companies?

The provision specifically refers to a private company.

What is the most important condition under Section 89?

A key condition is that tax, interest or penalty due from the private company cannot be recovered.

Does unpaid GST itself create director liability?

No. Unpaid tax and unrecoverable tax are not necessarily the same factual situation.

Which directors can be liable?

The provision refers to persons who were directors of the private company at any time during the period for which the tax was due.

If I became a director after the tax period, can Section 89 apply?

The relevant period of directorship must be examined against the period for which the tax was due. The demand date alone does not answer the question.

If I resigned before the GST demand order, am I automatically safe?

Not necessarily. Section 89 focuses on whether the person was a director during the period for which the tax was due.

Can an independent director be liable?

The actual facts, role, period of directorship and statutory defence need to be examined. A designation alone should not be treated as a complete answer.

What defence is available to a director?

The director can establish to the satisfaction of the Commissioner that the non-recovery cannot be attributed to gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty on that director's part in relation to the company's affairs.

What does “jointly and severally liable” mean?

It means that, where the statutory conditions are satisfied, the qualifying directors can each be held responsible for the amount rather than liability being automatically divided into equal shares.

Does Section 89 apply after liquidation?

It can be relevant where a private company is wound up and its determined GST dues cannot be recovered. Section 88 should also be examined for the liquidation-specific framework.

What is the difference between Section 88 and Section 89?

Section 88 deals with a company in liquidation and contains a specific director rule in subsection (3). Section 89 separately deals with directors of a private company where tax, interest or penalty cannot be recovered.

Can GST recover director's personal property?

Any personal recovery must have a valid statutory basis and follow the applicable recovery procedure. Section 89 should not be interpreted as an automatic power to attach property merely because someone is a director.

What documents should a director keep?

Keep GST returns, tax-payment records, board minutes, tax reviews, notices, orders, appeals, appointment/resignation records and evidence of compliance responsibilities.

How should a Section 89 notice be answered?

Reconstruct the demand, relevant tax period, recovery history, director period and statutory defence. A factual and provision-by-provision response is stronger than a general denial.

31. Key takeaways

  • Section 89 deals specifically with liability of directors of a private company.
  • It does not mean every director automatically pays every GST liability of the company.
  • The tax, interest or penalty must be due under the Act.
  • A crucial condition is that the amount cannot be recovered from the private company.
  • The relevant director period is the period for which the tax was due.
  • Appointment and resignation dates therefore matter.
  • Directors have a statutory defence concerning gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty.
  • Company liability and director liability should be analysed separately.
  • Section 88 should be reviewed separately where the company is in liquidation.
  • Good GST governance and contemporaneous records are important for managing director risk.
  • A Section 89 notice should be answered against the actual statutory conditions, demand and recovery history.
