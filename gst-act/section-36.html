1. Why Section 36 matters

If you search for “GST record retention period”, “how many years GST records should be kept”, “GST books retention 72 months”, “Section 36 CGST Act”, or “GST records retention after assessment”, the answer begins with Section 36.

Section 35 tells a registered person what accounts and records must be maintained. Section 36 answers the next question: how long must those records be retained?

The normal statutory period is 72 months from the due date of furnishing the annual return for the year to which the accounts and records relate. A special extension applies to records connected with an appeal, revision, other proceedings or investigation for an offence under Chapter XIX. citeturn0search0turn0search5

Simple rule: do not build a GST document-destruction policy around “six years from the invoice date”. Section 36 links the normal retention period to the due date of the annual return.

3. Complete statutory rule

Section 36 provides that every registered person required to keep and maintain books of account or other records under Section 35(1) shall retain them until the expiry of seventy-two months from the due date of furnishing the annual return for the year pertaining to those accounts and records.

The proviso applies where the registered person is a party to an appeal, revision or other proceedings before an Appellate Authority, Revisional Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court, whether filed by the registered person or by the Commissioner, or is under investigation for an offence under Chapter XIX.

For the records pertaining to the subject matter of that appeal, revision, proceedings or investigation, the retention period becomes one year after final disposal of that matter, or the normal Section 36 period, whichever is later. citeturn0search0turn0search2

4. What does 72 months mean?

72 months means six years. But the legally important point is the starting reference date.

Incorrect shortcutCorrect approach
Six years from invoice date72 months from the due date of the annual return for the relevant year.
Six years from annual-return filing dateSection 36 refers to the due date of furnishing the annual return.
Destroy everything once 72 months expireFirst check whether any record is subject to the proviso for appeal, revision, proceedings or investigation.
Keep only ledgersPreserve the Section 35 records and related documents covered by the Rules.
Compliance policy point: a business can voluntarily retain records longer than the statutory minimum where its accounting, litigation, contractual, audit or internal-control policy requires it.

5. When does the retention period start?

The statutory clock is connected to the due date of furnishing the annual return for the relevant year.

It is therefore necessary to identify:

  1. the financial year to which the records relate;
  2. the annual return applicable to that year;
  3. the statutory due date of that annual return; and
  4. the date on which 72 months from that due date expires.

This is why a GST record-retention register should ideally be maintained financial-year-wise rather than invoice-date-wise.

6. Worked calculation example

Assume, for illustration, that the relevant annual return for a financial year has a statutory due date of 31 December 2026.

StepCalculation
Annual return due date31 December 2026
Normal retention period72 months
Normal retention end31 December 2032

The example demonstrates the statutory method. The actual annual-return due date applicable to a particular financial year must be verified under the law and notifications applicable to that year.

The key formula is: annual-return due date + 72 months = normal Section 36 retention end date.

7. Due date vs actual annual-return filing date

This distinction is one of the most important points for GST record-retention policies.

Section 36 says “from the due date of furnishing of annual return”, not simply “from the date the annual return was actually furnished”. Therefore, if an annual return is filed late, the business should not automatically move the normal retention clock forward by the number of days of delay.

For example, if the statutory due date was 31 December but the return was actually filed on 20 February, the normal Section 36 reference remains the statutory due date unless the applicable law provides otherwise.

Practical policy: calculate retention based on the legally applicable due date and separately record the actual filing date for audit evidence.

8. What records are covered?

Section 36 covers books of account and other records required under Section 35(1). Through the accounts-and-records Rules, the preservation requirement extends to the supporting GST documents maintained with those records.

Rule 56 specifically provides that accounts maintained by a registered person, together with invoices, bills of supply, credit notes, debit notes and delivery challans relating to stocks, deliveries, inward supplies and outward supplies, are to be preserved for the period specified in Section 36. citeturn0search1

Tax records

Commercial evidence

Electronic evidence
ERP data, electronic records, backups and relevant audit-trail information.

9. Rule 56(16) connection

Rule 56 connects the retention requirement directly to Section 36. It provides that accounts maintained by the registered person, together with invoices, bills of supply, credit and debit notes and delivery challans relating to stocks, deliveries, inward supply and outward supply, are to be preserved for the period provided in Section 36.

Where records are maintained manually, they are to be kept at every related place of business mentioned in the registration certificate. Where they are maintained digitally, they must be accessible at every related place of business where the digital records are maintained. citeturn0search1turn0search7

10. Electronic records and retention

Electronic records do not have a shorter Section 36 retention period simply because they are digital.

Businesses using ERP/accounting systems should maintain a retention architecture covering:

  • live ERP records;
  • archived financial years;
  • invoice/document repositories;
  • database backups;
  • e-invoice and e-way-bill data where retained by the business;
  • credit/debit note records;
  • GST return data and reconciliation files; and
  • audit-trail information necessary to explain changes to electronic records.

Rule 57 separately requires proper electronic backup and the ability to produce relevant electronic records in the required form. citeturn0search1turn0search7

11. Principal and additional places of business

Section 35 and Rule 56 establish where books and related records are maintained. Section 36 then determines how long they must be preserved.

For a business with multiple registered places, the retention policy should identify where the original/manual records are held and how digitally maintained records are made accessible.

Example

A construction company has a head office and ten registered project locations. A single central ERP database may hold the accounting records, but project-level supporting documents should remain retrievable for the relevant sites and financial years.

12. Appeal, revision and other proceedings

The Section 36 proviso is triggered when the registered person is a party to an appeal, revision or other proceedings before an Appellate Authority, Revisional Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court.

The provision expressly covers proceedings:

  • filed by the registered person; or
  • filed by the Commissioner.

The records subject to the proceeding must be retained for one year after final disposal of the relevant matter, or for the normal 72-month period, whichever is later. citeturn0search0

Important: the extended retention does not mean every GST record of the company is automatically extended. The proviso refers to records pertaining to the subject matter of the appeal, revision or proceeding.

13. Investigation for an offence

The proviso also applies where the registered person is under investigation for an offence under Chapter XIX of the CGST Act.

Records pertaining to the subject matter of that investigation must be retained for one year after final disposal of the investigation or for the normal Section 36 period, whichever is later. citeturn0search0

This makes a legal-hold process essential for businesses receiving investigation notices, summonses or other proceedings that may concern particular financial years or transactions.

14. “Whichever is later” explained

This phrase prevents the taxpayer from destroying subject-matter records merely because the ordinary 72-month period has expired while litigation or investigation is still active.

SituationNormal periodProceeding endsExtended rule
Normal record31 Dec 2032Not applicable31 Dec 2032
Subject record31 Dec 203230 Jun 203131 Dec 2032
Subject record31 Dec 203230 Jun 203430 Jun 2035

In the third example, one year after final disposal is later than the normal retention date, so the record must be retained until 30 June 2035.

15. Only subject-matter records need the special extension

The wording of the proviso is important. It requires extended retention of books and records pertaining to the subject matter of the appeal, revision, proceeding or investigation.

For example, if a dispute concerns a particular works contract and financial year, the business should identify the records connected with that matter:

  • contract;
  • invoices;
  • RA bills;
  • measurement records;
  • ITC documents;
  • tax calculations;
  • returns;
  • payments;
  • correspondence; and
  • relevant books and electronic data.

A well-designed legal-hold register should record the matter number, financial year, records covered, authority/forum and release date.

17. Section 36 and annual return

The annual return is important to Section 36 because the normal retention clock is linked to its due date.

Therefore, a GST document-retention system should maintain a financial-year master containing:

  • financial year;
  • annual-return form applicable;
  • statutory due date;
  • actual filing date;
  • normal Section 36 retention end date; and
  • any litigation/investigation hold.
This approach is safer than simply storing a document with a “delete after six years” tag.

18. Section 36 and departmental audit

Section 65 permits the Commissioner or an authorised officer to undertake an audit of the books and records of a registered person.

Because Section 36 requires records to remain available for the statutory period, a taxpayer should not destroy records merely because no audit has yet been initiated.

For an audit-ready system, the auditor should be able to trace the GST return figures back to the underlying books and documents for the relevant financial year.

19. Section 36 and special audit

Section 66 is a separate special-audit mechanism. If a special audit concerns records that are still within the Section 36 period, those records must remain available. If the special audit/proceeding extends beyond the normal retention period, the Section 36 proviso becomes relevant for the subject-matter records.

Therefore, businesses should establish a legal hold whenever a proceeding, investigation, appeal or special audit concerns records that might otherwise be scheduled for destruction.

20. Section 36 and newer tax-determination provisions

Section 36 is fundamentally a retention provision; it does not itself determine tax liability. The relevant tax-determination provision for a dispute depends on the period and nature of the case.

This is important after the introduction of Section 74A. Section 74A forms part of the newer demand framework for applicable periods, but it does not change the basic Section 36 retention formula.

Do not confuse the tax-demand limitation period with the record-retention period. They are related for practical litigation purposes but are legally distinct concepts.

23. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — normal six-year retention

A company's FY 2025-26 records fall under an annual-return due date of 31 December 2026 for this illustration. The normal Section 36 period would run for 72 months from that due date, ending 31 December 2032.

Example 2 — annual return filed late

The annual return was due on 31 December but filed on 20 February. The business should not automatically calculate the statutory Section 36 period from 20 February because Section 36 refers to the due date.

Example 3 — appeal continues after normal expiry

Records normally due for destruction on 31 December 2032 are subject to an appeal that is finally disposed of on 30 June 2034. The relevant subject-matter records should be retained until 30 June 2035 because one year after final disposal is later.

Example 4 — appeal ends before normal expiry

If the same appeal is disposed of on 30 June 2031, the normal 31 December 2032 date remains later, so the normal retention period controls.

Example 5 — investigation

An investigation concerns ITC claimed in FY 2024-25. The company should place the relevant invoices, ledgers, reconciliation, returns and supporting documents under legal hold until the Section 36 proviso permits release.

Example 6 — construction project

A road project is completed but a tax dispute remains pending. The project file should not be destroyed merely because the project itself has closed.

Example 7 — digital ERP migration

A company changes ERP systems after three years. It must ensure that the old GST records remain readable and retrievable for the remaining Section 36 period.

Example 8 — branch closure

A registered additional place of business closes. Closure does not automatically eliminate the record-retention obligation for the historical records of that location.

Example 9 — record destruction policy

The company proposes automatic deletion after six years from invoice date. The policy should be redesigned because Section 36 uses the annual-return due date and contains a litigation/investigation extension.

Example 10 — commercial records retained longer

A company retains major project contracts for ten years because of contractual and limitation requirements. This is compatible with Section 36; the statutory period is a minimum retention requirement, not a prohibition on longer retention.

25. GST Council background

Section 36 is a relatively stable provision. Unlike some later GST sections, its core retention formula has not undergone a major structural rewrite.

The practical development has instead occurred through the broader accounts-and-records Rules and the evolution of GST return, audit and electronic-record systems. The GST Council's accounts-and-records framework contemplated detailed preservation of records and electronic accessibility from the beginning of GST. citeturn0search0turn0search1

The most important practical lesson is therefore not a sequence of frequent Section 36 amendments, but the need to keep the retention rule aligned with:

  • annual-return due dates;
  • record categories under Section 35 and Rule 56;
  • electronic-record requirements under Rule 57;
  • audit and investigation matters; and
  • legal holds for pending disputes.

29. Common mistakes

  • Calculating six years from the invoice date.
  • Calculating the period from the actual annual-return filing date instead of the statutory due date.
  • Destroying records immediately when the normal 72-month period expires without checking for pending proceedings.
  • Applying the litigation extension to every company record instead of identifying records pertaining to the subject matter.
  • Deleting old ERP databases during system migration.
  • Keeping PDFs but losing the underlying accounting data needed to explain transactions.
  • Failing to preserve credit/debit notes and delivery challans.
  • Destroying project records after completion of construction work.
  • Ignoring Section 36 because financial statements are retained under another law.
  • Assuming a statutory retention period prevents the business from retaining records longer when another legal or contractual requirement exists.

30. Section 36 GST FAQs

What is Section 36 of the CGST Act?

Section 36 specifies how long a registered person must retain the books of account and other records required under Section 35(1).

How long should GST records be kept?

The normal statutory period is 72 months, or six years, from the due date of furnishing the annual return for the relevant year.

Is GST record retention 6 years from the invoice date?

No. Section 36 links the normal period to the due date of the annual return for the year to which the records relate.

Is the GST retention period 72 months?

Yes. Section 36 uses 72 months for the normal retention period.

Does the actual annual-return filing date matter?

The statutory wording refers to the due date of furnishing the annual return. Therefore, late filing should not automatically be treated as shifting the normal retention starting point.

What happens if a GST appeal is pending?

Records pertaining to the subject matter of the appeal must be retained for one year after final disposal or for the normal Section 36 period, whichever is later.

What if GST investigation is pending?

Records pertaining to the subject matter of an investigation for an offence under Chapter XIX must be retained for one year after final disposal or for the normal retention period, whichever is later.

Do I have to retain every record of the company because one GST appeal is pending?

Section 36 specifically refers to records pertaining to the subject matter of the appeal, revision, proceedings or investigation. A proper legal-hold process should identify those records.

Does Section 36 apply to electronic records?

Yes. Section 35 and Rule 57 permit/require electronic record maintenance subject to the prescribed safeguards, and Rule 56 links preservation of relevant records to Section 36.

How does Section 36 relate to Rule 56?

Rule 56 contains detailed accounts-and-records requirements and expressly provides that specified accounts and supporting GST documents are preserved for the period prescribed in Section 36.

How does Section 36 relate to Section 35?

Section 35 identifies the records that must be maintained; Section 36 identifies how long they must be retained.

Can a company retain GST records longer than 72 months?

Yes. Section 36 establishes a statutory retention requirement. Other laws, contracts, litigation, internal policy or business needs may justify longer retention.

Do construction companies need to retain old project records?

Yes, where those records form part of the GST accounts and supporting documents. Pending disputes may require even longer retention under the Section 36 proviso.

What is the safest GST record retention policy?

Maintain a financial-year-wise retention register based on the annual-return due date, combine it with a legal-hold register for disputes/investigations, and preserve both paper and electronic source records in an accessible form.

32. Key takeaways

  • Section 36 is the GST record-retention provision.
  • The normal statutory retention period is 72 months, or six years.
  • The 72-month period runs from the due date of the annual return for the relevant year.
  • Do not calculate the normal period simply from the invoice date.
  • Do not automatically shift the statutory clock merely because the annual return was filed late.
  • Records connected with an appeal, revision, other proceedings or Chapter XIX investigation may need to be retained for one year after final disposal if that is later than the normal period.
  • The special extension concerns records pertaining to the subject matter of the relevant matter.
  • Rule 56(16) connects invoices, bills of supply, credit/debit notes and delivery challans with the Section 36 retention period.
  • Electronic records need proper backup, accessibility and preservation.
  • Construction and infrastructure companies should retain complete project evidence, not merely final ledger balances.
  • Section 35 answers “what records?” while Section 36 answers “how long?”
