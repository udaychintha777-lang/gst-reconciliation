1. Why Section 88 matters

When a company enters liquidation, the finance team's job is not finished. GST returns, output tax, ITC reversals, asset sales, pending notices, interest, penalties and old liabilities can continue to require attention.

Section 88 creates a specific GST process for a company being wound up. It requires the person appointed as liquidator to intimate the Commissioner, gives the Commissioner a statutory opportunity to identify an amount sufficient to provide for tax, interest or penalty, and contains a special liability provision for directors of a private company when determined dues cannot be recovered.

Core idea: Liquidation does not automatically make GST liabilities disappear. Section 88 creates a mechanism to identify and protect Government dues and, in specified circumstances, can extend liability to directors of a private company.

3. Section 88 in very simple language

Imagine Company A is being wound up. A liquidator is appointed to take control of the company's assets and liabilities.

The GST law says the liquidator must tell the GST Commissioner about the appointment within 30 days.

The Commissioner can then examine the company's GST position and notify the liquidator, within the statutory period, of an amount considered sufficient to cover tax, interest or penalty already payable or likely to become payable.

There is another important rule: if a private company is wound up and GST dues determined under the Act cannot be recovered, directors who were directors during the relevant period can become jointly and severally liable, unless they establish the statutory defence concerning gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty.

Liquidation → Liquidator informs Commissioner → GST dues assessed/protected → Recovery process → Possible director liability in specified private-company cases

4. Complete statutory structure

SubsectionWhat it does
Section 88(1)Requires the liquidator to intimate the Commissioner within 30 days of appointment.
Section 88(2)Requires the Commissioner to notify the liquidator, within three months of receiving the intimation, of an amount considered sufficient to provide for tax, interest or penalty then payable or likely to become payable.
Section 88(3)Creates joint-and-several liability for persons who were directors of a private company during the relevant period when determined tax, interest or penalty cannot be recovered, subject to the statutory defence.

Section 88 should be read with the recovery provisions and the rules dealing with a company under liquidation. Rule 160 provides a specific recovery procedure involving FORM GST DRC-24.

5. What does liquidation mean?

Liquidation is the process through which a company's affairs are wound up, assets are dealt with and liabilities are settled according to the applicable legal framework.

Section 88 is triggered when a company is being wound up, whether under the orders of a court or tribunal or otherwise.

The GST team should therefore obtain the actual liquidation/winding-up order or appointment document instead of relying only on an internal management communication.

Important: Corporate insolvency/liquidation law and GST law interact, but Section 88 is specifically a GST provision. The GST team should map GST obligations into the overall liquidation process.

6. Who is the liquidator?

Section 88 refers to a person appointed as receiver of assets of the company and uses the term liquidator for the purpose of the section.

The identity and appointment date of the person acting as liquidator are therefore important facts for the Section 88 compliance timeline.

Once appointed, the liquidator should immediately identify:

✓ Appointment date
✓ Authority/order under which appointed
✓ Company PAN and GSTINs
✓ Pending GST liabilities
✓ Pending returns
✓ GST notices and proceedings
✓ Stock and fixed assets
✓ Cash and credit ledger balances

Control point: Keep proof of the date on which the Commissioner received the liquidator's intimation. The three-month period in Section 88(2) is linked to receipt of that intimation.

8. Liquidator's 30-day duty

Section 88(1) requires the liquidator to give intimation of appointment to the Commissioner within 30 days after appointment.

This is not merely a routine corporate filing. It starts the statutory communication between the liquidation process and GST administration.

What should the intimation package contain?

The exact procedural form/document should be checked under the current Rules and portal practice, but from a practical control perspective the file should contain the appointment order, appointment date, company identification, GST registrations, contact details and a preliminary statement of GST liabilities and pending proceedings.

9. Commissioner's role

After receiving the liquidator's intimation, the Commissioner may make such inquiry or call for such information as considered necessary.

The objective is to identify an amount sufficient to provide for tax, interest or penalty that is:

  • already payable; or
  • likely to become payable.

The provision therefore looks beyond only demands that have already become final on the appointment date.

10. What amount can the Commissioner notify?

The Commissioner can notify the liquidator of an amount which, in the Commissioner's opinion, would be sufficient to provide for any tax, interest or penalty which is then or is likely thereafter to become payable by the company.

ComponentExamples of issues to examine
TaxUnpaid output tax, RCM, tax arising from assessment or sale of assets.
InterestInterest arising from delayed tax payment or other applicable statutory situations.
PenaltyPenalty exposure arising from proceedings under the Act where applicable.

This is why a GST due-diligence review should be completed early in the liquidation process.

11. Liability of directors of a private company

Section 88(3) contains a special rule for a private company.

If the company is wound up and tax, interest or penalty determined under the CGST Act for any period cannot be recovered, every person who was a director of the private company at any time during the period for which the tax was due can become jointly and severally liable for the payment.

This is not an automatic statement that every director must pay every GST liability. The statutory conditions must first be satisfied, including the inability to recover the determined amount from the company.

Important: Section 88(3) is not the same as ordinary company management responsibility. It is a specific statutory liability provision that operates when the stated conditions are met.

12. What defence does a director have?

The provision gives the director a statutory defence.

The director can avoid the Section 88(3) liability if the director proves to the satisfaction of the Commissioner that the non-recovery cannot be attributed to any gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty on the director's part in relation to the affairs of the company.

This makes documentation extremely important.

✓ Board records
✓ Tax compliance approvals
✓ GST return review evidence
✓ Payment approvals
✓ Management correspondence
✓ Professional advice records
✓ Internal control evidence
✓ Evidence of actions taken to discharge tax

13. Tax determined before liquidation

A company may already have a GST demand before liquidation begins.

Examples include a completed assessment, adjudication order, accepted liability or other determined tax, interest or penalty.

The liquidation team should not treat the appointment of a liquidator as wiping out that demand. It becomes part of the company's GST liability position and must be mapped into the liquidation records.

14. Tax arising during liquidation

Liquidation itself can involve GST events. For example, the liquidator may sell taxable stock, machinery or other business assets. Tax may arise on taxable supplies made during the liquidation process.

Therefore, the GST team should maintain a separate post-appointment transaction register covering:

✓ Asset sales
✓ Stock sales
✓ Scrap sales
✓ Recovery of receivables
✓ Services supplied
✓ Reverse-charge expenses
✓ Credit/debit notes
✓ GST payments

15. Tax determined after liquidation

Section 88(3) expressly refers to tax, interest or penalty determined for a period whether before, during or after liquidation.

This is a very important point. A company cannot assume that because liquidation has already started, a later GST determination is legally irrelevant.

Practical implication: Preserve GST books, invoices, returns and supporting records even after the business operations have stopped, because a tax determination may occur later.

16. Recovery and DRC-24

Rule 160 deals with recovery from a company in liquidation under Section 88. It provides for notification to the liquidator for recovery of an amount representing tax, interest, penalty or other amount due under the Act in FORM GST DRC-24.

This means Section 88 is not merely an information provision. It connects the liquidation process with GST recovery.

17. GST returns during liquidation

Liquidation does not by itself mean that all GST return obligations disappear immediately.

The tax team should determine which returns remain due based on the registration status, effective cancellation date and transactions continuing during liquidation.

Before cancellation

Pending regular returns should be identified and reconciled.

During liquidation

Taxable supplies, asset disposals and other GST events should be reported as required.

After cancellation

Final-return and other post-cancellation requirements should be checked under the applicable provisions and Rules.

The exact return position should be mapped GSTIN-wise rather than using one blanket “company closed” assumption.

18. GST registration and cancellation

A liquidation event does not itself mean that every GST registration is instantly cancelled on the date of appointment of the liquidator.

The cancellation process must be examined under Section 29 and the applicable Rules, while Section 88 deals with liability and the liquidator/Commissioner interface.

QuestionProvision to examine

Final returnSection 45 and applicable Rules.
Recovery of GST duesChapter XV recovery provisions and Rule 160 where liquidation is involved.

19. Sale of assets and GST

Liquidation often involves sale of machinery, vehicles, inventory, scrap, land-related assets or other property. The GST treatment depends on the nature of the asset and whether the transaction constitutes a taxable supply.

Do not assume that “sale by liquidator” automatically means “no GST”. Analyse:

✓ Nature of asset
✓ Whether it is goods/services/immovable property
✓ Business-use history
✓ Applicable exemption
✓ Tax rate
✓ Place of supply
✓ Invoice requirement
✓ ITC/depreciation history where relevant

The tax on liquidation sales should be separately captured in the GST liquidation working.

20. Stock and ITC

Inventory and ITC should be reconciled before the company is closed.

Questions include:

  • What stock remains on the cancellation/liquidation date?
  • What ITC remains in the electronic credit ledger?
  • Is reversal required under the applicable cancellation rules?
  • Are capital goods being sold?
  • Are there pending supplier credits/debits?
  • Are there blocked or disputed credits?

Section 88 itself does not replace the normal ITC and cancellation provisions. It should be used alongside Sections 16–18 and 29, as applicable.

23. Construction and infrastructure examples

Construction company liquidation

A contractor enters liquidation while several projects remain incomplete. The liquidator sells machinery and remaining construction material. GST must be examined separately on taxable disposals and other continuing supplies.

HAM project company

A project SPV under a highway arrangement is wound up after project completion. The GST team should review final project invoices, retention-related amounts, subcontractor ITC, asset disposal and pending departmental matters before closing the GST registrations.

Mining contractor

A mining contractor is liquidated with machinery, spare parts and stock remaining. Asset-by-asset GST classification and documentation should be prepared before sale.

24. Detailed practical cases

Case 1 — Liquidator appointed on 1 September

The liquidator should track the 30-day Section 88(1) intimation requirement from the appointment date and retain proof of submission/receipt.

Case 2 — Commissioner identifies pending GST exposure

The Commissioner may notify an amount considered sufficient to provide for tax, interest or penalty already payable or likely to become payable.

Case 3 — Tax demand cannot be recovered

If a private company is wound up and a determined tax/interest/penalty cannot be recovered, Section 88(3) can bring directors within joint-and-several liability, subject to the statutory defence.

Case 4 — Director resigned before liquidation

The relevant question is whether the person was a director during the period for which the tax was due. The exact dates should therefore be documented.

Case 5 — GST demand determined after liquidation

Section 88(3) expressly contemplates tax determined before, during or after liquidation. Preserve the records needed to defend the company's and directors' positions.

Case 6 — Asset sale during liquidation

The liquidator sells taxable machinery. The GST team determines taxability, value, rate, invoice and reporting separately.

Case 7 — Pending GSTR-3B

The company enters liquidation with returns pending. The team should map the pending periods and determine the applicable filing/payment/cancellation consequences.

Case 8 — Large ITC balance

The company has substantial ITC. Reconcile the electronic credit ledger and examine cancellation/reversal/transfer rules as applicable; Section 88 does not itself create an ITC transfer.

Case 9 — Multiple GST registrations

A company operates in several States. Each GSTIN should be reviewed separately for pending returns, notices, balances and cancellation.

Case 10 — Records stored with outgoing finance team

Before personnel leave, the liquidator should secure GST portal access, accounting backups, invoice archives and proceedings records.

Case 11 — Director disputes liability

The director's defence under Section 88(3) should be supported by contemporaneous evidence concerning tax compliance, controls and actions taken.

Case 12 — Company had no business for months

Even if operations stopped earlier, pending GST liabilities, notices and determined dues should be reviewed before assuming there is no exposure.

28. Common mistakes

  • Missing the 30-day liquidator intimation requirement.
  • Failing to preserve proof that the Commissioner received the intimation.
  • Assuming liquidation automatically extinguishes GST dues.
  • Ignoring GST arising from asset sales during liquidation.
  • Ignoring pending GST returns.
  • Closing GST registrations without completing the applicable cancellation/final-return process.
  • Failing to identify pending assessments and investigations.
  • Assuming director liability is automatic without considering the statutory conditions and defence.
  • Failing to preserve records after employees leave.
  • Confusing Section 88 with Section 89 director liability.
  • Ignoring multi-State GSTIN exposure.
  • Failing to coordinate GST with the liquidator's overall claims and recovery process.

Do not use Section 88 and Section 89 interchangeably. The factual trigger matters.

30. Legislative position and current framework

Section 88 formed part of the original CGST Act framework and came into force on 1 July 2017. The core provision continues to contain three linked safeguards: liquidator intimation, Commissioner notification of likely/current dues, and a special private-company director-liability rule where determined dues cannot be recovered.

The Rules also connect liquidation with recovery. Rule 160 specifically provides for recovery from a company in liquidation and the use of FORM GST DRC-24 for notification to the liquidator of amounts representing tax, interest, penalty or other amounts due.

Current-law distinction: Section 88 is about the company's liquidation process and GST liability. Section 89 is a separate provision dealing with liability of directors of a private company. Both should be reviewed where appropriate.

31. Section 88 GST FAQs

What is Section 88 of the CGST Act?

Section 88 deals with GST liability when a company is being wound up. It prescribes duties for the liquidator, gives the Commissioner a mechanism to identify tax/interest/penalty exposure and contains a special director-liability rule for private companies.

What must a liquidator do under Section 88?

The liquidator must intimate the Commissioner of the appointment within 30 days.

When must the Commissioner respond?

The Commissioner must notify the liquidator within three months from receipt of the intimation of the amount considered sufficient to provide for tax, interest or penalty then payable or likely thereafter to become payable.

Does liquidation cancel GST liability?

No. Section 88 specifically exists to address GST dues during liquidation.

Can GST be recovered after liquidation starts?

Yes. Section 88 contemplates tax, interest and penalty that may be determined before, during or after liquidation, subject to the applicable recovery framework.

Can directors be personally liable for GST after liquidation?

In the specified private-company situation under Section 88(3), yes, where determined tax, interest or penalty cannot be recovered and the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Is director liability automatic?

No. Section 88(3) contains conditions and a statutory defence based on absence of gross neglect, misfeasance or breach of duty attributable to the director.

Does Section 88 apply to public companies?

Section 88(3)'s special director-liability rule specifically refers to a private company. Sections 88(1) and 88(2), however, deal generally with a company being wound up.

What is DRC-24?

Rule 160 provides for FORM GST DRC-24 in the recovery process for a company under liquidation, for notification to the liquidator regarding amounts due under the Act.

Does a liquidator have to file GST returns?

The exact return obligations depend on the registration status, applicable tax periods, transactions and cancellation process. Liquidation should not be treated as an automatic exemption from pending GST compliance.

What happens to GST on sale of assets during liquidation?

Each sale should be analysed under the normal GST supply, valuation, rate, invoicing and reporting provisions. Section 88 does not make taxable liquidation sales automatically exempt.

What records should be preserved?

Returns, invoices, ledgers, e-way bills, e-invoices, notices, orders, payment records, ITC reconciliations, asset-sale records and other GST books should be preserved.

What if the company has several GST registrations?

Each GSTIN should be reviewed separately for returns, balances, notices, asset sales and cancellation consequences.

What if a GST demand is raised after liquidation?

Section 88(3) expressly contemplates tax, interest or penalty determined after liquidation. The company's records and the relevant director-period evidence should therefore be preserved.

What is the difference between Section 88 and Section 89?

Section 88 specifically deals with a company in liquidation and contains a director-liability rule in subsection (3). Section 89 is a separate provision dealing with directors of private companies where tax, interest or penalty cannot be recovered.

Does Section 88 override insolvency law?

No. Section 88 is a GST provision and must operate within the broader legal framework applicable to the liquidation. The GST team should coordinate with the liquidator and legal advisers.

33. Key takeaways

  • Section 88 is the GST provision specifically dealing with a company in liquidation.
  • The liquidator must intimate the Commissioner within 30 days of appointment.
  • The Commissioner has three months from receipt of the intimation to notify the liquidator of an amount considered sufficient for tax, interest or penalty.
  • The amount can cover liabilities already payable as well as liabilities likely to become payable.
  • Liquidation does not automatically extinguish GST liabilities.
  • Section 88(3) can make directors of a private company jointly and severally liable where determined dues cannot be recovered, subject to the statutory defence.
  • The director-liability rule specifically considers gross neglect, misfeasance and breach of duty.
  • Tax can be determined before, during or after liquidation.
  • Taxable asset sales during liquidation require separate GST analysis.
  • GST registrations, returns, ITC and records must be reviewed GSTIN-wise.
  • Rule 160 connects liquidation with GST recovery through FORM GST DRC-24.
  • Section 88 and Section 89 are separate provisions and should not be confused.
