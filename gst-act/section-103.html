Section 103 CGST Act — Applicability of Advance Ruling

Section 103 answers one of the most misunderstood questions in GST: when an advance ruling is passed, exactly who is bound by it? This practical guide explains the limited binding scope of an AAR/AAAR ruling, the applicant-specific rule, the role of the concerned and jurisdictional officer, what happens when law or facts change, and how businesses should implement a ruling without applying it blindly across unrelated GST registrations or transactions.

1. Why Section 103 matters in real business

Searches such as “Section 103 CGST Act”, “is advance ruling binding on other taxpayers”, “does AAR ruling apply to all GST registrations”, “AAAR binding effect”, and “advance ruling applicability GST” usually arise because businesses assume an advance ruling is either completely universal or completely optional. Neither assumption is correct.

Section 103 creates a deliberately limited binding framework. The advance ruling is binding on the applicant who sought it for the matter covered by the ruling, and on the concerned or jurisdictional officer in respect of that applicant. The binding effect continues unless the law, facts or circumstances supporting the original ruling have changed. citeturn0search12

Core practical idea: Before copying an advance-ruling treatment into another GSTIN, project, customer contract or group company, first perform an applicability check: same applicant? same transaction? same facts? same law? same circumstances?

2. Section 103 in simple language

Suppose Company A applies for an advance ruling about a transaction it is undertaking. The AAR gives a ruling. Under Section 103, that ruling is not automatically a rule for every other taxpayer in India.

RulingAAR/AAAR decides the question

Change testCheck whether law/facts/circumstances changed

CBIC's advance-ruling material specifically explains that an advance ruling is limited to the person who applied and is not automatically applicable to similarly placed other taxable persons. citeturn0search16

3. What Section 103 actually provides

ProvisionRulePractical meaning
103(1)(a)Binding on the applicant who sought the ruling in respect of the matter referred to in Section 97(2).The applicant cannot treat the ruling as merely optional for the same covered matter simply because the outcome is inconvenient.
103(1)(b)Binding on the concerned officer or jurisdictional officer in respect of the applicant.The specified tax authorities are bound in relation to that applicant and matter, subject to the statutory framework.
103(2)Binding unless the law, facts or circumstances supporting the original ruling have changed.Applicability must be continuously tested against the basis on which the ruling was obtained.

The statutory text is clear that the ruling's binding effect is limited to the applicant and the concerned/jurisdictional officer in respect of that applicant. citeturn0search12turn0search13

4. Who is actually bound by an advance ruling?

A. The applicant

The applicant who sought the ruling is within the binding scope for the matter covered by the ruling. This is why businesses should not obtain an advance ruling and then casually ignore it in subsequent invoices for the same factual transaction.

B. Concerned/jurisdictional officer

Section 103 also binds the concerned officer or jurisdictional officer in respect of the applicant. The exact officer relationship should be understood from the applicant's registration and the transaction covered.

C. Other taxpayers

Another company or taxpayer should not assume that an advance ruling obtained by somebody else automatically binds it. CBIC's own advance-ruling material explains that the ruling is not applicable to similarly placed other taxable persons merely because their transactions look similar. citeturn0search16

Practical warning: “Same industry” is not the same as “same applicant.” A ruling obtained by one construction company, manufacturer or service provider does not automatically become the legal position for every competitor.

6. Role of the concerned and jurisdictional officer

Section 103 is particularly important from a departmental perspective because it identifies the concerned/jurisdictional officer in relation to the applicant as part of the binding framework. citeturn0search12

For businesses, this means the advance ruling should be preserved in the GST legal file and made available when the same covered transaction is examined during departmental verification or other proceedings, subject to the limits of the ruling.

Recommended internal document pack

  • Original AAR application and question.
  • Supporting facts and documents submitted.
  • AAR order.
  • AAAR appeal and order, if any.
  • Rectification order, if any.
  • Applicability analysis.
  • Current law/fact change review.
  • Tax matrix and implementation approval.

7. When does the binding effect change?

Section 103(2) does not provide a fixed calendar expiry date. Instead, the ruling remains binding unless the law, facts or circumstances supporting the original advance ruling have changed. citeturn0search12

Original ruling → identify its legal/factual basis → monitor changes → reassess applicability when basis changes

This is one of the most important practical concepts. A company should not maintain an advance-ruling conclusion in an SOP forever without reviewing whether the assumptions on which it was based remain true.

8. Change in law — the first major exception

If the law supporting the original ruling changes, the business must reassess whether the original conclusion can continue to be relied upon. The change may arise from an amendment to the Act, Rules, notification or another legally relevant change.

ScenarioFinance-team action
GST rate notification changesCheck whether the original ruling depended on the earlier rate/notification.
Exemption notification changesReview the exact exemption condition and effective date.
Definition changesRe-test classification and scope.
Rule changesReview whether the operational mechanism assumed in the ruling still exists.
Judicial/legal developmentObtain updated legal analysis before continuing a high-value position.
Do not use an old ruling as a shield against a changed law: Section 103 itself makes the binding effect conditional on the continuing existence of the law, facts and circumstances supporting the original ruling.

9. Change in facts — often missed by businesses

A business can remain the same legal entity while its actual transaction changes materially.

Examples of factual changes

  • Contract scope changes.
  • Customer category changes.
  • Nature of consideration changes.
  • Goods/services supplied change.
  • Delivery or performance model changes.
  • Bundling or packaging of supplies changes.
  • Project structure changes.
  • Subcontracting arrangement changes.
  • Commercial documents no longer match the facts presented to AAR.

If the original ruling depended on a specific factual structure, a materially different transaction should be separately analysed.

10. Change in circumstances — beyond the written contract

“Circumstances” should be monitored as part of the tax control environment. A contract may look similar on paper while the commercial environment around the supply changes.

ChangePotential consequence
Business model changedOriginal ruling may no longer fit the transaction.
Customer relationship changedNature of supply or commercial terms may differ.
Supply chain changedVendor/subcontractor structure may alter the facts.
Project delivery changedActual performance may no longer match the ruling's factual assumptions.
Regulatory framework changedOriginal reasoning may require fresh review.

11. Same business, different GSTIN — a practical trap

Large businesses often have GST registrations in multiple States. One registration may have obtained an advance ruling, and the finance team may be tempted to copy the same tax treatment everywhere.

Do not make the shortcut: “Our company has an AAR ruling, therefore every GSTIN can follow it automatically.” Section 103's wording focuses on the applicant and the concerned/jurisdictional officer in respect of that applicant. citeturn0search12

Better approach

  1. Identify the exact applicant and registration involved.
  2. Compare the other GSTIN's transaction and facts.
  3. Check whether the same legal provisions apply.
  4. Document why the ruling is being used as a reference or why separate analysis is required.
  5. For material positions, obtain professional confirmation before rolling the treatment across registrations.

12. Group companies and related entities

A parent company, subsidiary, associate or joint venture may operate a similar business, but a separate legal entity should not assume that another entity's advance ruling is automatically binding on it.

EntityExampleRecommended treatment

SubsidiarySame business model but separate GSTIN/legal entity.Use ruling as a reference, not an automatic binding order.
Joint venture/AOPSeparate taxable person and contract.Analyse its own facts and legal status.
Branch/other registrationSame legal entity but different registration/factual setting.Perform a documented applicability review.

14. How a business should implement an advance ruling

Once the company concludes that Section 103 makes the ruling binding for its covered transaction, implementation should be controlled.

1. Legal fileArchive ruling and facts.
2. Tax matrixDocument classification/rate/treatment.
3. ERPMap tax codes and invoice logic.
4. MonitoringTrack law/fact changes.

Minimum implementation controls

  • Approved tax treatment note.
  • Applicable GSTIN list.
  • Contract/project applicability matrix.
  • ERP tax-code mapping.
  • Invoice review control.
  • GSTR-1/GSTR-3B reporting check.
  • ITC review where relevant.
  • Annual or event-driven legal review.

16. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Ruling obtained by Company A

Company A obtains an advance ruling that a specific service supplied under its contract has a particular GST treatment.

Company A: The ruling is within Section 103's binding scope for the covered matter.

Company B: Even if Company B provides the same service, Company B should not assume that Company A's ruling is automatically binding on it. It can use the ruling as a persuasive reference while performing its own analysis.

Example 2 — Same company, different GSTIN

A company has GSTINs in Telangana, Maharashtra and Karnataka. The Telangana registration was the applicant in an advance ruling.

Solution: Do not simply copy the ruling into all three GSTINs. Prepare a comparison of the registrations, transaction facts and legal basis. If the business decides to follow the same treatment elsewhere, document why.

Example 3 — Same contract model, changed law

A ruling was obtained under a particular notification. The notification is subsequently amended.

Solution: Trigger a tax-law review. Section 103(2) expressly says the ruling is binding unless the law, facts or circumstances supporting the original ruling have changed.

Example 4 — Same product, different commercial arrangement

The product is unchanged, but the new contract bundles installation, maintenance and other services differently.

Solution: Compare the new factual structure with the facts considered in the ruling. Do not assume the old conclusion automatically covers the new bundle.

Example 5 — Construction project changes scope

An advance ruling is based on a particular infrastructure contract. A later project includes additional activities and a different payment structure.

Solution: Use a project applicability matrix. If material facts differ, obtain a fresh legal analysis rather than mechanically applying the old ruling.

Example 6 — Departmental verification

A department officer questions a transaction covered by the taxpayer's advance ruling.

Solution: Provide the certified ruling and the evidence demonstrating that the actual transaction continues to match the facts supporting it. This is why the original application, documents and implementation records should be preserved.

Example 7 — Ruling becomes outdated

A company continues using a ten-year-old tax position without checking whether the relevant law and commercial model changed.

Solution: Create an annual/event-driven advance-ruling review. A ruling has no arbitrary expiry date under Section 103; its continued binding effect depends on the law, facts and circumstances supporting it remaining unchanged. citeturn0search16

For infrastructure companies, Section 103 is particularly important because project structures vary even within the same organisation.

Best practice: Maintain a “ruling applicability register” with columns for ruling number, applicant GSTIN, transaction, key assumptions, current projects, law-change check, fact-change check and review date.

18. Common mistakes businesses should avoid

  1. Assuming every AAR ruling is universal: Section 103 creates a limited binding scope.
  2. Applying another company's ruling as if it were your own: A ruling is not automatically binding on unrelated taxpayers.
  3. Ignoring the applicant/GSTIN: Record exactly who obtained the ruling.
  4. Ignoring changed law: Check amendments and notifications that affect the original basis.
  5. Ignoring changed facts: A new contract can invalidate the factual assumption behind the old analysis.
  6. Applying a ruling to every project: Build a project-wise comparison.
  7. Changing ERP without documentation: Legal positions should have controlled tax-master changes.
  8. Failing to preserve the factual record: The ability to demonstrate that facts remain unchanged is critical.

20. Section 101 vs Section 103

Section 101Section 103
Deals with the AAAR's order after an appeal/reference.Deals with the applicability/binding effect of the advance ruling.
AAAR may confirm or modify the ruling.Identifies who is bound and when the binding effect continues.
Focus: outcome of appeal.Focus: scope and continuing applicability.

Practical sequence: first determine what the AAAR decided under Section 101; then determine who is bound and whether the legal/factual basis remains unchanged under Section 103.

21. Section 103 vs Section 104

Section 103 concerns the normal binding/applicability framework. Section 104 addresses a much more serious situation where the advance ruling was obtained by fraud, suppression of material facts or misrepresentation of facts and can be declared void ab initio after the required hearing.

Important distinction: “The facts have changed after the ruling” under Section 103 is not the same concept as “the original ruling was obtained through fraud or suppression” under Section 104. Do not mix the two mechanisms.

23. Section 103 CGST Act — FAQs

What is Section 103 of the CGST Act?

Section 103 deals with the applicability and binding effect of an advance ruling pronounced by the Authority or Appellate Authority under the advance-ruling chapter.

Is an advance ruling binding on everyone?

No. Section 103 limits the binding effect to the applicant for the relevant matter and the concerned/jurisdictional officer in respect of that applicant. citeturn0search12

Is an AAR ruling binding on another company in the same industry?

No, not automatically. Another taxpayer may consider the ruling as a reference, but Section 103 does not make it automatically binding on every similarly placed taxpayer. citeturn0search16

Is an AAR ruling binding on another GSTIN of the same company?

The business should not assume automatic universal application. The applicant and factual scope should be checked, and the legal/factual basis should be documented before extending the treatment.

How long does an advance ruling remain binding?

Section 103 does not prescribe a simple fixed expiry period. It remains binding unless the law, facts or circumstances supporting the original ruling have changed. citeturn0search12

What if the GST law changes after the ruling?

The business should reassess the position because Section 103(2) expressly makes continued binding effect conditional on the supporting law, facts or circumstances not having changed.

What if the contract facts change?

Compare the new transaction with the facts underlying the ruling. A material factual change can affect whether the original ruling continues to be binding.

Can a business use another taxpayer's AAR as guidance?

Yes, it may be useful as a reference or persuasive material, but it should not be confused with a ruling that is binding on that business under Section 103.

What should a finance manager maintain?

Maintain the ruling, original application/factual record, applicant GSTIN, tax treatment note, applicability matrix, law/fact-change tracker and implementation evidence.

25. Key takeaways

  • Section 103 is about the applicability and binding scope of an advance ruling.
  • The ruling is binding on the applicant for the matter covered by the application.
  • It is also binding on the concerned/jurisdictional officer in respect of that applicant.
  • It is not automatically binding on every other taxpayer in the same industry or State.
  • There is no simple fixed expiry date; binding effect continues unless the supporting law, facts or circumstances change.
  • Businesses should monitor legal amendments and factual changes rather than treating an old ruling as permanently applicable.
  • Different GSTINs, group companies and projects should be checked separately before extending an advance-ruling treatment.
  • Maintain an applicability register and evidence trail showing why the current transaction still matches the ruling.
Ruling → identify applicant + matter → document original facts → monitor law/facts/circumstances → test applicability → controlled implementation
