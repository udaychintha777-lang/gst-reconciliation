GST ACT — SECTION 29

Cancellation or Suspension of Registration

Section 29 is the statutory exit-and-control provision for GST registration. It deals with voluntary cancellation, cancellation initiated by the proper officer, suspension while proceedings are pending, the continuing liability for earlier periods, coordination with State/UT GST registration and the tax/ITC consequences of cancellation.

1. What Section 29 does

Cancellation ends the GST registration, but it does not erase the taxpayer's earlier GST liabilities.

Section 29 provides the legal framework for cancelling a GST registration either on the taxpayer's request or through action by the proper officer. It also recognises suspension while cancellation proceedings are pending.

The provision is particularly important because cancellation is not merely an administrative deletion of a GSTIN. It can affect return filing, invoicing, input tax credit, stock, capital goods, tax recovery, notices and the taxpayer's ability to make taxable supplies.

Core principle: cancellation is an exit from registration, not an exit from past tax obligations.

Section 29 was brought into force on 22 June 2017, along with the original registration provisions of the CGST Act. citeturn2search0

3. Voluntary cancellation

Under Section 29(1), cancellation can be initiated by the registered person. In the case of death of a proprietor, the legal heirs can make the application.

Typical voluntary-cancellation situations include:

  • business discontinued or permanently closed;
  • business transferred, sold or otherwise disposed of;
  • business amalgamated with another legal entity;
  • demerger or other restructuring;
  • change in constitution where the old registration should cease;
  • the taxpayer is no longer liable to registration under Sections 22 or 24; or
  • the taxpayer wants to opt out of a voluntary registration under Section 25(3).

Rule 20 requires the cancellation application to be filed electronically in the prescribed form, with details of stock, capital goods and the amount payable, within the prescribed period. CBIC's current published Registration Rules specify 30 days from the occurrence of the event warranting cancellation. citeturn0search0

5. Officer-initiated cancellation

Section 29(2) allows the proper officer to cancel registration where specified statutory circumstances exist. This is fundamentally different from a taxpayer's own cancellation application.

The officer must have reasons to believe that the registration is liable to cancellation and must follow the prescribed procedural safeguards.

Natural justice: Section 29(2) expressly prevents cancellation without giving the person an opportunity of being heard.

Rule 22 provides the operational show-cause and order procedure. The current CBIC Registration Rules provide for a cancellation notice, taxpayer reply, and an order where cancellation is justified. citeturn0search0

7. Retrospective cancellation

Section 29(2) expressly allows the proper officer to cancel registration from such date, including a retrospective date, as he may deem fit, where the statutory cancellation grounds are established.

Retrospective cancellation is especially sensitive because the effective date can affect:

  • validity of tax invoices;
  • recipient input tax credit;
  • returns and tax periods;
  • tax collected from customers;
  • stock and ITC reversal;
  • notices and recovery proceedings.
Do not assume that an order dated today automatically means cancellation starts today. Always read the effective date stated in the cancellation order and examine the statutory ground supporting that date.

CBIC's FAQ has historically recognised retrospective cancellation where registration was obtained by fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts. citeturn0search7

8. Opportunity of being heard

Before the proper officer cancels a registration under Section 29(2), the person must be given an opportunity of being heard.

This safeguard is important in both ordinary compliance cases and serious cases involving alleged fraud or suppression. The taxpayer should use the show-cause process to establish:

  • the correct facts;
  • the reason for any delayed return or compliance failure;
  • proof that business actually exists;
  • proof that supplies were genuine;
  • payment of pending tax, interest and late fee where relevant;
  • why cancellation should not be retrospective; and
  • why the alleged statutory ground does not apply.
Practical rule: never ignore a REG-17 cancellation notice merely because the business intends to surrender the GSTIN anyway.

9. Suspension during cancellation proceedings

Section 29 contains suspension language allowing registration to be suspended during pending cancellation proceedings in the manner prescribed.

Rule 21A provides the operational framework. Suspension may arise:

  • when the registered person has applied for cancellation, pending completion of the cancellation process; or
  • when the proper officer has reasons to believe the registration is liable to cancellation and proceedings are pending.

During suspension, the Rules impose restrictions on making taxable supplies and provide return-related consequences. The suspension is not the same legal event as final cancellation.

CBIC's Rule 21A text states that a person whose registration is suspended should not make taxable supplies during the suspension period and explains that this means not issuing a tax invoice/charging tax on taxable supplies during suspension. citeturn0search4

10. Earlier-period liabilities survive cancellation

Section 29(3) is one of the most important safeguards in the GST registration chapter.

Cancellation does not extinguish liability for tax, interest, penalty or other dues for any period before cancellation. It also does not remove other obligations under the Act or Rules.

Key distinction: cancellation removes the registration status; it does not grant immunity from the past.

11. CGST–SGST/UTGST coordination

Section 29(4) provides that cancellation under the State GST Act or Union Territory GST Act is deemed to be cancellation under the CGST Act.

This keeps the Central and corresponding State/UT registration records aligned.

12. Section 29(5) exit payment

Cancellation can create a tax/ITC consequence for goods and capital assets held immediately before cancellation.

Section 29(5) requires the registered person whose registration is cancelled to pay, by debit to the electronic credit ledger or electronic cash ledger, an amount based on:

  • the input tax credit relating to inputs in stock;
  • ITC relating to inputs contained in semi-finished goods in stock;
  • ITC relating to inputs contained in finished goods in stock; and
  • ITC relating to capital goods or plant and machinery;

or the output tax payable on those goods, whichever is higher, calculated in the prescribed manner.

The current statutory text also contains a specific capital-goods/plant-and-machinery proviso comparing reduced ITC with tax on transaction value under Section 15. citeturn3search2turn3search1

13. Capital goods calculation

For capital goods or plant and machinery, Section 29(5) does not simply require the entire original ITC to be repaid in every case. The proviso requires a prescribed reduction to the ITC taken and comparison with tax on transaction value under Section 15, with the higher amount becoming relevant.

The detailed calculation is provided through the Rules, particularly Rule 44. The practical exercise should therefore be performed asset by asset, considering:

  • original ITC;
  • period for which the asset has been used;
  • prescribed percentage reduction;
  • transaction value where the asset is disposed of; and
  • the output-tax comparison required by Section 29(5).
Do not use a blanket “reverse all ITC” approach for capital goods without applying the prescribed calculation.

15. Rule 20 — application for cancellation

A registered person seeking cancellation under Section 29(1) files FORM GST REG-16 electronically.

The application contains information such as:

  • reason for cancellation;
  • effective date sought;
  • stock of inputs;
  • inputs contained in semi-finished/finished goods;
  • capital goods held in stock;
  • tax liability arising from cancellation;
  • payment made against that liability; and
  • supporting documents where required.

CBIC's published Rule 20 framework states that the application is to be made within 30 days of the event warranting cancellation. It also states that voluntary registration is generally not cancelled through this route before one year from the effective date of registration. citeturn0search0

16. Rule 21 — registration liable to be cancelled in certain cases

Rule 21 supplies additional prescribed cancellation grounds under the authority of Section 29(2)(a).

Examples include:

  • no business being conducted from the declared place of business;
  • issuing invoices/bills without actual supply in violation of the law or Rules; and
  • other specified contraventions added through amendments to the Rules.

Because Rule 21 is delegated legislation, the exact ground should always be checked against the Rule applicable on the date of the alleged contravention.

CBIC's published Rule 21 text confirms the declared-place and fake-invoice grounds and records later amendments to the rule. citeturn0search4

17. Rule 21A — suspension

Rule 21A is important because suspension is a temporary procedural status, not the final cancellation order.

Suspension on taxpayer application

Where a person applies for cancellation, registration can be deemed suspended from the relevant date pending completion of cancellation proceedings.

Suspension by officer

Where the officer has reasons to believe cancellation is warranted, the officer may suspend registration after providing the prescribed opportunity of being heard and pending the cancellation proceedings.

Effect

The Rules restrict taxable supplies during suspension and provide that the suspension is deemed revoked when the cancellation proceedings are completed. citeturn0search4

18. Rule 22 — officer cancellation process

Where the proper officer has reasons to believe that registration is liable to cancellation, Rule 22 provides the procedural sequence.

StepAction
1Officer identifies cancellation ground.
2Show-cause notice in FORM GST REG-17.
3Taxpayer replies in FORM GST REG-18 within the prescribed period.
4Officer evaluates reply and documents.
5If cancellation justified, order in FORM GST REG-19.
6If reply satisfactory, proceedings are dropped through FORM GST REG-20.

CBIC's Registration Rules page presently states a seven-working-day period for the REG-17 show-cause response framework and provides the REG-19/REG-20 order structure. citeturn0search0

19. Returns and cancellation

Cancellation does not automatically close every return obligation. The taxpayer must determine what returns, statements, tax payments and final compliance remain due for the period up to the effective cancellation date.

In officer-initiated cancellation for return default, the Rules also contain mechanisms under which proceedings can be dropped where the taxpayer furnishes pending returns and pays the applicable tax, interest and late fee in the prescribed circumstances.

Practical approach: before applying for cancellation, prepare a return-status reconciliation from the GST portal. Do not rely only on the ERP's return calendar.

20. GSTR-10 and final compliance

Cancellation should be followed by a structured closure exercise. Where applicable, the taxpayer must furnish the final return and discharge the statutory obligations associated with cancellation.

FORM GSTR-10 is the final return associated with cancellation/surrender of registration in the prescribed circumstances. The Rule 44 mechanism is relevant for calculating ITC-related amounts, and the GST portal may impose additional filing dependencies before closure is fully regularised.

Do not confuse cancellation order with closure of tax affairs. Pending returns, tax, interest, late fee, stock reversal, notices and audits can continue after cancellation.

21. Detailed examples

Example 1 — business permanently closed

A trading company closes its business permanently on 31 March. It should identify the event date, calculate the cancellation-related amount, file REG-16 within the prescribed period and complete final compliance.

Example 2 — turnover below threshold

A small business is no longer liable for registration under the applicable threshold and no compulsory-registration provision applies. It may seek cancellation, subject to the statutory and Rule 20 conditions.

Example 3 — voluntary registration

A person voluntarily registered under Section 25(3). The person cannot simply cancel immediately where the Rule 20 restriction on cancellation within the first year applies.

Example 4 — sale of business

A business is sold as a going concern to another entity. The old GST registration should be reviewed for cancellation, while the purchaser's registration and transfer documentation are handled separately.

Example 5 — proprietorship to company

A proprietor incorporates a company and receives a new PAN. The old proprietorship registration should not be converted by merely amending the PAN; fresh registration is required for the new legal person.

Example 6 — non-filing

A registered person repeatedly fails to furnish returns. The officer may initiate cancellation under the applicable Section 29(2) ground and Rule 22 procedure.

Example 7 — fake invoice allegation

The officer alleges that invoices were issued without actual supply. The taxpayer should produce purchase orders, delivery records, e-way bills, GRNs, bank evidence, stock records and other evidence before the cancellation proceedings are decided.

Example 8 — no business at declared address

A taxpayer no longer operates from the declared principal place. This can trigger Rule 21 proceedings and potentially Section 29 cancellation.

Example 9 — fraud in registration

If registration was obtained by fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts, cancellation may be ordered with retrospective effect where legally justified.

Example 10 — capital goods on cancellation

A contractor closes a GST registration while machinery remains on hand. The finance team must calculate the Section 29(5) amount using the prescribed capital-goods methodology rather than simply reversing every historical ITC amount.

26. GST Council background

Cancellation of registration formed part of the original GST registration architecture and has subsequently been refined mainly through amendments to the Act and Rules.

Registration exit
The framework was designed to permit cancellation when a taxpayer ceased to require registration.
Officer control
Specified contraventions and false registration information could trigger cancellation.
Natural justice
The officer-cancellation mechanism contains a hearing safeguard.
Suspension
Later amendments created a clearer temporary status while cancellation proceedings remain pending.

The 53rd GST Council material reproduces the current Section 29 architecture, including voluntary cancellation, officer cancellation, suspension, continuing liability and the Section 29(5) calculation. citeturn3search2

27. Common mistakes

  • Assuming cancellation erases earlier GST liabilities.
  • Applying for cancellation without reconciling pending returns.
  • Ignoring the 30-day Rule 20 application period.
  • Failing to calculate stock and capital-goods consequences.
  • Assuming every project closure means the entire GSTIN must be cancelled.
  • Using cancellation instead of amendment for closure of an additional place of business.
  • Ignoring the distinction between cancellation and suspension.
  • Not responding to REG-17 within the prescribed period.
  • Accepting retrospective cancellation without checking the legal basis and effective date.
  • Forgetting that audits, assessments and recovery can continue after cancellation.
  • Assuming a new legal entity can simply take over the old GSTIN.
  • Failing to preserve GST records after cancellation.

29. Key takeaways

  • Section 29 is both an exit provision and a compliance-control provision.
  • Voluntary cancellation is initiated by the registered person/legal heirs; officer cancellation requires a statutory ground and procedural safeguards.
  • Rule 20 generally requires the cancellation application within 30 days of the event warranting cancellation.
  • Return defaults, specified contraventions, non-commencement after voluntary registration and fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression can trigger officer proceedings.
  • Cancellation can have retrospective effect where Section 29(2) permits and the facts justify the selected effective date.
  • Suspension is not the same as cancellation; it is a temporary status during prescribed proceedings.
  • Earlier tax, interest, penalty and other obligations survive cancellation.
  • Section 29(5) requires a prescribed calculation for stock and capital goods immediately before cancellation.
  • A capital-goods calculation should not be treated as an automatic reversal of the entire original ITC.
  • Cancellation under the corresponding State/UT GST law is deemed cancellation under CGST.
  • Cancellation should be followed by a formal closure checklist covering returns, stock, capital assets, notices, final return and record retention.
