Chapter XVIII — Appeals and Revision

Section 113 – Orders of Appellate Tribunal

A simple, practical guide to understanding what the GST Appellate Tribunal can do after hearing an appeal — including confirmation, modification, annulment, remand, adjournment, rectification and finality of its order.

Section 113 at a glance

Section 113 tells us what the Appellate Tribunal may do with a GST appeal after hearing the parties and how its order is dealt with thereafter.

ConfirmThe Tribunal may confirm the decision or order appealed against.
ModifyThe Tribunal may modify the decision or order to the extent permitted by the appeal and the law.
AnnulThe Tribunal may annul the decision or order appealed against.
RemandThe matter may be sent back for fresh adjudication or decision with directions.
AdjournFor sufficient cause, the hearing may be adjourned with reasons recorded in writing.
RectifyAn error apparent on the face of the record may be rectified subject to statutory conditions.

Why Section 113 matters

Winning an appeal is not limited to one possible outcome. Depending on the facts and the grounds raised, the Appellate Tribunal can uphold the lower order, change it, cancel it, or send the matter back for a fresh decision.

For a taxpayer or the Department, this makes Section 113 important because it explains the legal consequences of the Tribunal's decision and the route available after that decision.

In simple words: Section 113 is the provision that explains what the GST Appellate Tribunal can do after hearing an appeal and what happens to its order thereafter.

Statutory provision — Section 113

(1) The Appellate Tribunal may, after giving the parties to the appeal an opportunity of being heard, pass such orders thereon as it thinks fit, confirming, modifying or annulling the decision or order appealed against or may refer the case back to the Appellate Authority, or the Revisional Authority or to the original adjudicating authority, with such directions as it may think fit, for a fresh adjudication or decision after taking additional evidence, if necessary.

(2) The Appellate Tribunal may, if sufficient cause is shown, at any stage of hearing of an appeal, grant time to the parties or any of them and adjourn the hearing of the appeal for reasons to be recorded in writing: Provided that no such adjournment shall be granted more than three times to a party during hearing of the appeal.

(3) The Appellate Tribunal may amend any order passed by it under sub-section (1) so as to rectify any error apparent on the face of the record, if such error is noticed by it on its own accord, or is brought to its notice by the Commissioner or the Commissioner of State tax or the Commissioner of the Union territory tax or the other party to the appeal within a period of three months from the date of the order: Provided that no amendment which has the effect of enhancing an assessment or reducing a refund or input tax credit or otherwise increasing the liability of the other party, shall be made under this sub-section, unless the party has been given an opportunity of being heard.

(4) The Appellate Tribunal shall, as far as possible, hear and decide every appeal within a period of one year from the date on which it is filed.

(5) The Appellate Tribunal shall send a copy of every order passed under this section to the Appellate Authority or the Revisional Authority, or the original adjudicating authority, as the case may be, the appellant and the jurisdictional Commissioner or the Commissioner of State tax or the Union territory tax.

(6) Save as provided in section 117 or section 118, orders passed by the Appellate Tribunal on an appeal shall be final and binding on the parties.

Section 113 explained in simple language

1The parties must get an opportunity of being heard

Before deciding the appeal, the Tribunal has to give the parties an opportunity of being heard. This is an important procedural safeguard because the decision should be made after considering the submissions relevant to the appeal.

2The Tribunal can confirm the order

If the Tribunal finds that the decision or order under appeal is sustainable, it may confirm it.

Example: A demand order is challenged before the Tribunal. After considering the grounds and evidence, the Tribunal agrees with the lower authority's decision. The Tribunal may confirm the order.

3The Tribunal can modify the order

Modification means the Tribunal changes the decision or order to the extent warranted by its findings. For example, the amount or part of a demand may be altered where the Tribunal accepts only some of the appellant's grounds.

4The Tribunal can annul the order

Annulment means setting aside the decision or order appealed against. The precise consequence depends on the findings and the nature of the matter before the Tribunal.

5The Tribunal can remand the case

Instead of finally deciding every factual or procedural issue itself, the Tribunal can send the matter back to the Appellate Authority, Revisional Authority or original adjudicating authority for fresh adjudication or decision, with directions.

The provision also permits the fresh adjudication or decision to involve additional evidence where necessary.

6Adjournment is possible, but subject to conditions

If sufficient cause is shown, the Tribunal may grant time and adjourn the hearing. The reasons for the adjournment have to be recorded in writing.

The statutory proviso also says that an adjournment shall not be granted more than three times to a party during the hearing of the appeal.

7Rectification of an error apparent on the face of the record

Section 113(3) provides a limited mechanism for correcting an error apparent on the face of the record in an order passed under section 113(1).

The error may be noticed by the Tribunal itself or brought to its notice by the specified tax authority or the other party to the appeal, within three months from the date of the order.

Important: Rectification is not a general opportunity to re-argue the entire appeal. The statutory wording is specifically directed at an error apparent on the face of the record.

8Opportunity of hearing where liability may increase

If a rectification amendment would enhance an assessment, reduce a refund or input tax credit, or otherwise increase the liability of the other party, that party must be given an opportunity of being heard before the amendment is made.

9Target of one year for disposal

Section 113(4) says the Tribunal shall, as far as possible, hear and decide every appeal within one year from the date on which it is filed.

The expression “as far as possible” is important. It describes a statutory timeframe objective rather than wording that every appeal must necessarily be disposed of within exactly one year in all circumstances.

10Copies of the order must be sent to specified persons and authorities

Section 113(5) requires a copy of every order passed under the section to be sent to the relevant Appellate Authority, Revisional Authority or original adjudicating authority, as applicable, as well as the appellant and the relevant jurisdictional Commissioner / Commissioner of State tax / Union territory tax.

11Finality of the Tribunal's order

Section 113(6) provides that, subject to sections 117 and 118, an order of the Appellate Tribunal on an appeal is final and binding on the parties.

Therefore, Section 113 should be read together with the provisions dealing with appeal to the High Court and appeal to the Supreme Court.

Practical examples

Example 1 — Demand confirmed

A taxpayer appeals against a GST demand of ₹12 lakh. The Tribunal examines the grounds and concludes that the lower order is sustainable. The Tribunal may confirm the decision.

Example 2 — Demand partly modified

A taxpayer disputes several components of a demand. The Tribunal accepts one ground but rejects another. The resulting order may modify the original decision to reflect the Tribunal's findings.

Example 3 — Matter remanded for fresh adjudication

Suppose the lower authority decided an issue without properly considering material evidence that was relevant to the dispute. The Tribunal may refer the matter back with directions for a fresh adjudication or decision, including consideration of additional evidence where necessary.

Example 4 — Adjournment

A party demonstrates sufficient cause for requiring additional time at the hearing. The Tribunal may grant an adjournment, but the reasons have to be recorded in writing and the statutory limit on adjournments to a party applies.

Example 5 — Rectification

An order contains an obvious record-based error that falls within the scope of an “error apparent on the face of the record”. Section 113(3) provides a mechanism for the Tribunal to amend the order, subject to its conditions.

Example 6 — Rectification affecting the other party

If a proposed rectification would increase the other party's liability, such as by enhancing an assessment or reducing a refund or input tax credit, the affected party must be given an opportunity of being heard.

Section 113 — detailed practical application

Section 113 becomes much easier to understand when the Tribunal's possible actions are connected with an actual GST dispute. The following illustrations show how the same appeal can lead to different types of orders depending on the evidence, findings and issues before the Tribunal.

1. How a GST dispute can reach the Tribunal

Consider a registered company that receives an adjudication order confirming a tax demand of ₹50 lakh, together with applicable interest and penalty. The taxpayer challenges the order through the appellate mechanism and the matter eventually comes before the Appellate Tribunal.

At the Tribunal stage, the question is not simply whether the taxpayer has filed an appeal. The Tribunal examines the grounds of appeal, the impugned order, the evidence on record, the submissions of the parties and the applicable law. Section 113 then sets out the principal ways in which the Tribunal may dispose of that appeal.

2. Four principal outcomes under Section 113(1)

OutcomeIllustrationWhat the order means in practical terms
Confirm Demand appealed against: ₹50 lakh. Tribunal finds the challenged order sustainable. The original decision/order remains confirmed to the extent stated in the Tribunal's order.
Modify Demand appealed against: ₹50 lakh. Tribunal finds ₹15 lakh is not sustainable but the balance is sustainable. The original order is changed in accordance with the Tribunal's findings; the final demand follows the Tribunal's order and applicable summary.
Annul The Tribunal finds that the decision/order appealed against cannot stand on the basis of the issues before it. The challenged decision/order is annulled, subject to the exact wording and consequential directions in the Tribunal's order.
Remand A material factual issue was not properly examined by the lower authority and requires a fresh decision. The matter is sent back with directions for fresh adjudication or decision. It is not the same as the Tribunal finally deciding every issue itself.

3. Example — partial relief through modification

Suppose a company receives an order confirming:

  • Tax: ₹40 lakh
  • Interest: ₹8 lakh
  • Penalty: ₹4 lakh
  • Total components in dispute: ₹52 lakh

The taxpayer appeals on three separate issues. After examining the record, the Tribunal accepts the taxpayer's argument on one issue involving ₹10 lakh of tax but does not accept the other grounds. The Tribunal's order may therefore modify the original decision rather than simply confirming or annulling it in full.

Key point: “Modified” does not automatically mean that the entire demand disappears. It means the original decision is changed according to the Tribunal's findings.

4. Example — why a remand may be different from an annulment

Assume a taxpayer disputes a ₹25 lakh ITC-related demand. The taxpayer says that invoices, payment evidence and reconciliation records were available, but the adjudicating authority did not properly examine a material set of documents before passing the order.

If the Tribunal considers that a fresh factual examination is required, Section 113(1) allows it to refer the case back to the relevant authority with directions for fresh adjudication or decision. The authority then has to deal with the matter in accordance with the Tribunal's directions and the applicable law.

By contrast, an annulment is an outcome in which the decision or order appealed against is annulled. Therefore, when reading a Tribunal order, the exact operative portion matters more than simply seeing the word “allowed” or “disposed”.

5. Additional evidence and remand

Section 113(1) expressly contemplates a fresh adjudication or decision after taking additional evidence, if necessary. This connects with the broader appellate procedure governing when additional evidence can be admitted and how the other side gets an opportunity to respond.

For example, if a taxpayer seeks to rely on an important document that was not considered earlier, the procedural rules concerning additional evidence must also be examined. The Tribunal's order should be read together with the relevant appellate rules rather than treating Section 113 in isolation.

6. Adjournment — what “sufficient cause” means in practice

Section 113(2) permits the Tribunal to grant time to a party and adjourn the hearing if sufficient cause is shown. The reasons must be recorded in writing.

For example, if a party demonstrates a genuine procedural reason why the hearing cannot effectively proceed on the scheduled date, the Tribunal may consider an adjournment. The provision does not create an unrestricted right to repeatedly postpone a matter.

Statutory limit: the proviso to Section 113(2) says that an adjournment shall not be granted more than three times to a party during the hearing of the appeal.

7. Rectification — a very important distinction

Section 113(3) is frequently misunderstood. It permits amendment of an order passed under Section 113(1) to rectify an error apparent on the face of the record, subject to the conditions specified in the section.

It is useful to distinguish three situations:

SituationSection 113(3) questionIllustration
Obvious record-based error Can it qualify as an error apparent? A clear arithmetical or record-reading mistake is discovered in the Tribunal's order.
Fresh debate on the merits Is this merely an attempt to re-argue the appeal? A party simply disagrees with the Tribunal's interpretation and wants the entire issue reconsidered.
Amendment increasing liability Has the other party received an opportunity of being heard? A proposed correction would enhance assessment or reduce refund/ITC or otherwise increase the other party's liability.

8. The three-month period — how to understand it

The section says that the specified authority or the other party to the appeal may bring the error to the Tribunal's notice within three months from the date of the order. The Tribunal may also notice the error on its own accord.

For example, if an order is dated 10 September, the statutory three-month period must be tracked from the date specified by Section 113(3). For an actual filing, the exact computation should be checked against the applicable procedural rules and the relevant dates on the order and filing record.

9. Rectification that increases liability — hearing is mandatory

Suppose the Tribunal's original order determines a particular liability at ₹20 lakh. A proposed rectification would increase the assessment by ₹3 lakh. The proviso to Section 113(3) protects the affected party by requiring an opportunity of being heard before such an amendment is made.

The same safeguard applies where the amendment would reduce a refund or input tax credit, or otherwise increase the liability of the other party.

10. One-year disposal provision — read the exact wording

Section 113(4) states that the Appellate Tribunal shall, as far as possible, hear and decide every appeal within one year from the date on which it is filed.

This wording should not be simplified into an absolute statement that every appeal must necessarily be completed within exactly one year regardless of circumstances. The statutory phrase “as far as possible” forms part of the provision.

11. Why the order summary matters

The Tribunal's detailed order explains the findings and reasons. The prescribed order-summary mechanism is separately important because it captures the final amount of demand confirmed by the Tribunal.

From 22 September 2025, Rule 113(2) was substituted so that the Appellate Tribunal issues, or causes to be issued, the summary in FORM GST APL-04A along with its order under Section 113(1). This replaced the earlier mechanism under which the jurisdictional officer issued FORM GST APL-04 for the Tribunal's confirmed demand.

Practical reading method: When reviewing a Tribunal case, keep the detailed Tribunal order and the applicable demand/order summary together. The detailed order explains why the appeal was decided; the prescribed summary helps identify the final amount of demand confirmed.

12. Finality does not mean “no further legal remedy” in every case

Section 113(6) says that, except as provided in Sections 117 and 118, orders passed by the Appellate Tribunal on an appeal are final and binding on the parties.

Therefore, Section 113 must be read together with the provisions dealing with further appeals. For example, Section 117 provides for an appeal to the High Court in the circumstances specified there, including the requirement concerning a substantial question of law.

13. A complete worked example

Facts: ABC Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd. receives a GST order with a disputed tax demand of ₹60 lakh. The taxpayer contests ₹35 lakh relating to ITC and ₹25 lakh relating to another classification issue.

At Tribunal:

  1. The taxpayer and the Department are given an opportunity of being heard.
  2. The Tribunal examines the impugned order, grounds of appeal and evidence.
  3. On the ITC issue, the Tribunal finds that a material factual verification was not properly completed.
  4. On the classification issue, the Tribunal decides the legal issue on the record.
  5. The Tribunal may therefore use different statutory powers within the same order — for example, remanding the factual issue with directions while deciding the other issue according to its findings.
  6. The resulting demand has to be determined in accordance with the operative portion of the Tribunal's order and the applicable order-summary procedure.
  7. If a subsequent apparent error is identified, Section 113(3) provides a limited rectification mechanism subject to its conditions.
  8. Any question of a further appeal must then be examined under the applicable provisions, including Sections 117 and 118.

14. Practical checklist for a finance or tax team

What happens after the Appellate Tribunal's order?

Section 113(6) expressly connects the finality of the Tribunal's order with sections 117 and 118. This means that the statutory framework for a further appeal to the High Court or Supreme Court has to be considered where applicable.

Section 117Provides the framework for appeal to the High Court in the circumstances specified there.
Section 118Deals with appeal to the Supreme Court in the circumstances specified there.
Practical point: Do not treat every disagreement with a Tribunal order as automatically giving an unrestricted further appeal. The applicable statutory conditions, jurisdiction and grounds under the subsequent appeal provisions must be examined.

Key compliance and litigation takeaways

  • Ensure the appellant's grounds and supporting documents clearly address the issues under challenge.
  • Keep the record of the proceedings and evidence organised because the Tribunal's findings depend on the record and applicable procedural rules.
  • Where a remand is sought, clearly identify the factual or procedural issue that requires fresh consideration.
  • For rectification, distinguish an apparent record-based error from an attempt to reopen the merits of the appeal.
  • Track the date of the Tribunal order carefully because Section 113(3) specifies a three-month period for bringing the relevant error to its notice.
  • When an amendment could increase the other party's liability, check the hearing safeguard in the proviso to Section 113(3).
  • After receiving an order, check whether any further statutory remedy under sections 117 or 118 is applicable.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Section 113 of the CGST Act?

Section 113 deals with orders of the Appellate Tribunal. It specifies the Tribunal's powers to confirm, modify, annul or remand an order, along with provisions on adjournment, rectification, disposal, communication and finality.

Can the GST Appellate Tribunal remand a case?

Yes. Section 113(1) permits the Tribunal to refer the case back to the Appellate Authority, Revisional Authority or original adjudicating authority for fresh adjudication or decision, with directions and, where necessary, after taking additional evidence.

Can the Tribunal adjourn a hearing?

Yes, where sufficient cause is shown. Reasons for the adjournment must be recorded in writing, and the statutory proviso limits adjournments to not more than three times to a party during the hearing of the appeal.

What is rectification under Section 113(3)?

It is a mechanism for correcting an error apparent on the face of the record in an order passed under Section 113(1), subject to the statutory conditions including the three-month period.

Can rectification increase the other party's liability?

Where the amendment would enhance an assessment, reduce a refund or input tax credit, or otherwise increase the liability of the other party, that party must be given an opportunity of being heard.

Is a GSTAT order final?

Section 113(6) states that, save as provided in sections 117 and 118, an order of the Appellate Tribunal on an appeal is final and binding on the parties.

Does Section 113 itself provide a one-year mandatory deadline?

Section 113(4) says that the Tribunal shall, as far as possible, hear and decide every appeal within one year from the date on which it is filed. The wording “as far as possible” is relevant when understanding the nature of this timeframe.

Section 113 — quick revision

The easiest way to remember Section 113 is: hear the parties → decide the appeal → correct apparent errors where permitted → communicate the order → apply finality subject to further statutory appeals.
