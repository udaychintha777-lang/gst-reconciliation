CGST Act Section 166 – Laying of Rules, Regulations and Notifications

Section 166 provides the parliamentary laying mechanism for GST rules, Board regulations and Government notifications made or issued under the CGST Act.

Where?Before each House of Parliament, while it is in session.

3. Statutory Text of Section 166

166. Laying of rules, regulations and notifications.— Every rule made by the Government, every regulation made by the Board and every notification issued by the Government under this Act, shall be laid, as soon as may be after it is made or issued, before each House of Parliament, while it is in session, for a total period of thirty days which may be comprised in one session or in two or more successive sessions, and if, before the expiry of the session immediately following the session or the successive sessions aforesaid, both Houses agree in making any modification in the rule or regulation or in the notification, as the case may be, or both Houses agree that the rule or regulation or the notification should not be made, the rule or regulation or notification, as the case may be, shall thereafter have effect only in such modified form or be of no effect, as the case may be; so, however, that any such modification or annulment shall be without prejudice to the validity of anything previously done under that rule or regulation or notification, as the case may be.
Core idea: Section 166 is not the provision that creates the rule-making or regulation-making power. Sections 164 and 165 provide those powers; Section 166 provides the parliamentary laying mechanism for the specified instruments.

4. Meaning in Simple Language

GST law contains primary legislation in the CGST Act and subordinate instruments such as rules and regulations. Section 166 requires the specified subordinate instruments to be placed before each House of Parliament while it is in session.

The provision establishes a process of parliamentary oversight. It does not say that a rule, regulation or notification becomes a completely different type of instrument merely because it is laid before Parliament. Instead, it specifies what must happen after the instrument is made or issued and what consequences follow if both Houses agree on a modification or agree that it should not be made.

Remember the sequence: Make/issue → lay before both Houses → 30-day statutory period → possible modification or annulment by agreement of both Houses → protection for things already done.

5. What Must Be Laid Before Parliament?

NotificationGovernmentEvery notification issued by the Government under the CGST Act is to be laid.

The wording is tied to instruments made or issued under the CGST Act. A taxpayer researching a document should first identify its legal source and issuing authority.

Important: Do not assume that every document published on a GST website is a “notification” for Section 166 purposes. Circulars, instructions, press releases, FAQs and portal advisories can have different legal character.

6. Understanding the 30-Day Period

Section 166 says the instrument must be laid before each House of Parliament while it is in session for a total period of thirty days.

The section allows those 30 days to be comprised in one session or in two or more successive sessions. This means the full statutory period does not necessarily have to occur within one single parliamentary session.

QuestionSection 166 position
Is the period 30 days?Yes, a total period of thirty days.
Must all 30 days be in one session?No. The section permits one session or two or more successive sessions.
Does the section require laying before only one House?No. It says each House of Parliament.
When is the instrument laid?As soon as may be after it is made or issued, while Parliament is in session.

7. One Session or Two or More Successive Sessions

Parliamentary sessions do not always provide a single uninterrupted block in which a full 30-day period can occur. Section 166 therefore expressly accommodates a total of 30 days spread across one session or two or more successive sessions.

Illustration A – One session

If the required laying period is completed during one session, the statutory 30-day total is satisfied according to the provision.

Illustration B – Successive sessions

If the instrument is laid during one session but the session ends before the total period is completed, the section permits the period to be comprised in two or more successive sessions.

Illustration C – Legal research

When researching whether a particular rule or notification was laid, a legal researcher should examine parliamentary records rather than assuming that Gazette publication and parliamentary laying are the same event.

8. What Happens If Both Houses Agree to Modify or Reject It?

Before the expiry of the session immediately following the session or successive sessions mentioned in the provision:

  • If both Houses agree to make a modification, the rule, regulation or notification thereafter has effect only in the modified form.
  • If both Houses agree that it should not be made, the rule, regulation or notification thereafter has no effect.
Important: The statutory language refers to agreement by both Houses. The special consequence described by Section 166 is therefore not triggered merely because one House expresses a different view.
Parliamentary outcomeEffect under Section 166
Both Houses agree on modificationInstrument thereafter has effect only in the modified form.
Both Houses agree it should not be madeInstrument thereafter has no effect.
No such agreementThe special modification/annulment consequence described in the section does not arise on that basis.

9. Protection of Things Already Done

The final part of Section 166 contains an important saving clause. It says that any modification or annulment is without prejudice to the validity of anything previously done under the rule, regulation or notification.

In practical terms, the provision distinguishes between the future effect of a parliamentary modification/annulment and the validity of actions already taken under the instrument before that modification or annulment.

Example: If a rule has operated and businesses or officers have taken actions under it before a later parliamentary modification or annulment contemplated by Section 166, the saving language says that the modification or annulment is without prejudice to the validity of things previously done under that instrument.
Do not overread this clause: The saving language concerns validity of things previously done under the relevant instrument. A specific dispute still requires examination of the exact facts, dates, instrument and applicable law.

10. Practical Examples

Example 1 – Government rule

The Government makes a CGST Rule. Section 166 requires the rule to be laid before each House of Parliament as soon as may be after it is made, while Parliament is in session.

Example 2 – Board regulation

The Board makes a regulation under Section 165. Section 166 applies to that regulation as well.

Example 3 – Government notification

A Government notification issued under the CGST Act falls within the category expressly mentioned in Section 166.

Example 4 – 30 days across sessions

The statutory 30-day period may be comprised in one session or two or more successive sessions.

Example 5 – Both Houses modify

If both Houses agree to modify the instrument within the statutory framework, the instrument thereafter operates in the modified form.

Example 6 – Both Houses reject

If both Houses agree that the rule, regulation or notification should not be made, it thereafter has no effect.

Example 7 – Earlier compliance action

The final saving clause protects the validity of things previously done under the instrument from being prejudiced merely by the later modification or annulment.

Example 8 – Circular versus notification

A company receives a circular. It should not automatically classify the circular as a Section 166 notification. The legal source must first be identified.

Example 9 – Historical tax audit

During an audit, the team should record the rule/notification version and its effective date rather than relying only on the current consolidated law.

Example 10 – ERP legal register

An ERP or tax-control register can include instrument type, notification number, issue date, effective date and source document to support historical compliance.

Example 11 – Parliamentary research

Where the validity or later modification of a subordinate instrument is material to litigation, the research file can include the relevant parliamentary laying record as well as the Gazette notification.

Example 12 – Regulation amendment

If a Board regulation is later modified, maintain the original and amended versions with their dates so that transactions can be tested against the correct legal version.

Example 13 – Business SOP

A company SOP can record the current requirement, but the underlying rule/regulation/notification should remain the authoritative legal source for the compliance control.

Example 14 – Notice response

If a notice relies on a particular notification, the taxpayer can verify its issuing authority, statutory source, effective date and applicable version before preparing a response.

11. Finance, Tax and Legal Workflow

  1. Identify the instrument: rule, regulation or notification.
  2. Identify the issuer: Government or Board, as applicable.
  3. Locate the official notification: retain the original legal document.
  4. Record the effective date: critical for historical compliance.
  5. Check parliamentary laying: where relevant to a legal review, identify the laying record.
  6. Monitor subsequent changes: check for amendments or parliamentary modification/annulment.
  7. Update ERP/SOP controls: map the legal change to affected GSTINs, transactions and periods.
  8. Preserve audit evidence: maintain the legal source and version used in the tax working.

13. Important Interpretive Points

  • “As soon as may be”: The statutory text requires laying as soon as may be after the instrument is made or issued, while Parliament is in session.
  • “Each House”: The provision expressly refers to both Houses of Parliament.
  • “Total period of thirty days”: The period can be comprised in one session or two or more successive sessions.
  • “Both Houses agree”: The special modification or annulment consequences described in the section require agreement of both Houses.
  • “Thereafter”: The modified or no-effect consequence operates thereafter according to the statutory language.
  • Saving clause: Modification or annulment is without prejudice to the validity of anything previously done under the instrument.
Research caution: Section 166 explains the statutory mechanism; a particular instrument requires document-level verification of the relevant parliamentary record.

14. Common Mistakes

  • Assuming every GST circular must be laid under Section 166.
  • Failing to distinguish a rule from a Board regulation.
  • Ignoring the requirement that the instrument be made or issued under the CGST Act.
  • Assuming the 30-day period must always occur in one session.
  • Ignoring the words “each House of Parliament”.
  • Assuming one House alone can trigger the statutory modification/annulment consequence.
  • Forgetting the final saving clause concerning things previously done.
  • Using only the current consolidated rule when auditing an old tax period.
  • Failing to retain the original notification and subsequent amendments.

16. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 166 of the CGST Act?

It provides for laying every Government rule, every Board regulation and every Government notification issued under the CGST Act before each House of Parliament for the statutory period.

2. How long must the instrument be laid?

The section specifies a total period of thirty days.

3. Must the 30 days be in one parliamentary session?

No. Section 166 allows the total period to be comprised in one session or in two or more successive sessions.

4. Is the instrument laid before both Houses?

Yes. The section expressly says it shall be laid before each House of Parliament.

5. What does “as soon as may be” mean?

The section requires laying as soon as may be after the rule is made or regulation/notification is issued, while Parliament is in session.

6. What if both Houses agree to modify the rule?

The rule, regulation or notification thereafter has effect only in the modified form.

7. What if both Houses agree that it should not be made?

The relevant rule, regulation or notification thereafter has no effect.

8. Can one House alone cause the Section 166 modification consequence?

The statutory text describes the consequence where both Houses agree. It does not state that agreement of only one House produces that consequence.

9. What happens to actions already taken?

Section 166 says that modification or annulment is without prejudice to the validity of anything previously done under the rule, regulation or notification.

10. Does Section 166 itself make GST rules?

No. Section 164 provides the Government's rule-making power; Section 166 provides the laying mechanism.

11. Does Section 166 apply to Board regulations?

Yes. The section expressly includes every regulation made by the Board.

12. Does Section 166 apply to Government notifications?

Yes, where the notification is issued by the Government under the CGST Act.

13. Does every GST circular have to be laid under Section 166?

Not automatically. The document's legal character and statutory source must be identified.

14. What is the relation between Section 165 and Section 166?

Section 165 gives the Board the power to make regulations; Section 166 provides for laying those regulations before Parliament.

15. Why is the final saving clause important?

It protects the validity of things previously done under the instrument from being prejudiced by a later modification or annulment under Section 166.

16. Should businesses track parliamentary laying?

For litigation, legal research or material legal-risk reviews, parliamentary laying records may be relevant. For routine compliance, the operative instrument and effective date remain central.

17. Should old GST periods be reviewed using current rules?

No assumption should be made. Verify the instrument and legal position applicable to the relevant historical period.

18. Is this article legal advice?

No. It is an educational explanation. For a specific dispute or legal issue, verify the current and historical statutory provisions, notifications, parliamentary records and applicable judicial interpretation.

17. Quick Revision – Section 166
