Section 154 of the CGST Act, 2017

Power to take samples. Understand when GST authorities can take samples of goods from a taxable person's possession, the receipt requirement, what the provision means in practice, and how businesses should document sampling activity.

1. Statutory Text of Section 154

154. Power to take samples.—

The Commissioner or an officer authorised by him may take samples of goods from the possession of any taxable person, where he considers it necessary, and provide a receipt for any samples so taken.
Current-law position: Section 154 is part of Chapter XIX of the CGST Act, dealing with miscellaneous provisions. Current CBIC and India Code materials reproduce the provision in this form. The section itself contains no separate percentage, quantity, fixed sampling interval or detailed laboratory procedure.

2. Section 154 Explained in Simple Language

GST administration sometimes requires more than looking at invoices and returns. The physical characteristics of goods can be relevant to a tax issue. For example, an authority may need to examine a product, compare its characteristics with the description in records, or obtain a sample for technical examination.

Section 154 provides the statutory mechanism for taking a sample of goods from the possession of a taxable person when the competent authority considers it necessary.

The key point: Section 154 is a sampling power. It is not, by itself, a provision determining tax liability, classification, valuation, confiscation or penalty.

3. Who Can Take Samples?

The provision identifies two possible authorities:

  1. The Commissioner; or
  2. An officer authorised by the Commissioner.
PersonPosition under Section 154Practical point
CommissionerExpressly empowered.Can take samples where the statutory condition is met.
Officer authorised by CommissionerExpressly covered.Authority comes from the Commissioner's authorisation.
Any employee of the departmentNot automatically covered merely because the person works in the department.The Section 154 wording specifically refers to the Commissioner or an officer authorised by him.
Private technical personNot independently empowered by Section 154 merely because they are an expert.An expert's involvement and the statutory power to take samples are separate questions.
Important distinction: Section 153 deals with taking assistance from an expert. Section 154 separately deals with the authority to take samples. An expert does not automatically acquire Section 154 powers simply because expert assistance is being used.

4. What Can Be Sampled?

The statutory language refers to samples of goods. The section therefore operates on physical goods rather than a standalone service transaction or an abstract accounting entry.

Depending on the facts, goods could include finished products, raw materials, components, packaged products, stock held for sale, manufactured articles or other taxable goods in the possession of the taxable person.

SituationIllustration
Manufacturing stockA sample of a manufactured product may be taken where physical characteristics are relevant.
Raw materialA sample may be relevant where the nature or composition of an input matters.
Finished goodsA product sample may help establish technical or physical characteristics.
Packaged goodsA sample may be taken from stock to examine the actual goods and packaging.
Bulk goodsSampling may be relevant where a representative portion needs technical examination.
Do not overread the section: Section 154 does not itself say that every type of product must be sampled, nor does it establish a universal quantity or sampling method.

5. Goods in the Possession of a Taxable Person

The section expressly refers to goods from the possession of any taxable person.

This makes the factual location and possession of the goods important. In a practical situation, the goods may be located at a factory, warehouse, business premises, shop, project site or another location where the taxable person's goods are held.

Possession is a factual question

Businesses should maintain clear records showing where stock is held and under whose custody it is maintained. This becomes particularly important for businesses with multiple warehouses, project sites, third-party storage arrangements or goods moving between locations.

Example:

A manufacturer has stock at its factory and at a registered warehouse. If sampling is carried out at one location, the business should record the product, quantity, batch/lot details and the location from which the sample was taken.

6. When Is Sampling Necessary?

Section 154 uses the words “where he considers it necessary.” The provision therefore does not prescribe an automatic sampling requirement for every inspection or proceeding.

Sampling may be relevant where physical examination could assist in understanding an issue connected with the GST proceeding.

Potential reasons for sampling

  • Understanding the actual nature or characteristics of goods.
  • Checking whether physical goods correspond with records or descriptions.
  • Supporting technical examination of a product.
  • Clarifying an issue involving classification or product characteristics.
  • Investigating a dispute where physical composition or quality is relevant.
  • Comparing goods from different batches or categories where the factual issue requires it.
Necessary does not mean routine. The section gives a power to take samples when the competent authority considers it necessary; it does not state that samples must be taken in every GST inspection.

7. Receipt for Samples

One of the clearest safeguards written directly into Section 154 is the requirement to provide a receipt for any samples so taken.

For a business, the receipt is an important record because it establishes that samples were taken and helps connect the physical sample with the relevant stock and proceeding.

8. Practical Sampling Process

Section 154 itself is short. A practical workflow can nevertheless be understood as follows, subject to the facts and any applicable procedural requirements:

StepWhat happens
1. Identify the issueThe authority identifies a GST matter where physical goods may be relevant.
2. Determine necessityThe Commissioner or authorised officer considers whether sampling is necessary.
3. Identify goodsThe relevant goods are identified from the taxable person's possession.
4. Take sampleThe sample is taken from the goods.
5. Provide receiptA receipt is provided for samples taken, as required by Section 154.
6. Internal reconciliationThe business records the sample quantity and connects it to the inventory records.
7. Technical examination, if applicableWhere the proceeding requires technical examination, the sample may be used for that purpose under the applicable process.
8. Preserve recordsThe sampling receipt and related documents are retained with the proceeding file.
Do not invent a statutory laboratory procedure. Section 154 itself does not prescribe a universal laboratory, testing method, sample size or time limit. Any additional procedure must be checked against the applicable rules, notifications, departmental instructions or the specific proceeding.

9. What the Taxpayer Should Do When Samples Are Taken

1. Cooperate with the statutory process

Do not obstruct a validly authorised sampling exercise.

2. Record the details

Maintain an internal note of the date, location, product, batch/lot, approximate quantity and the officer involved.

3. Obtain and preserve the receipt

Section 154 expressly requires a receipt for samples taken. Keep the receipt safely.

4. Reconcile inventory

Record the quantity removed as a sample in the inventory or stock-control system where appropriate.

5. Preserve technical documents

If the product's characteristics are relevant, maintain product specifications, test reports, manufacturing records, labels, catalogues and other supporting material.

6. Track the proceeding

Connect the sample receipt with the relevant inspection, inquiry or investigation file.

10. Sample vs Other Evidence

A sample is one part of the factual record. Its significance depends on the issue for which it was taken and the other evidence available in the proceeding.

11. Detailed Practical Examples

Example 1 – Manufactured product

A factory manufactures a specialised product. During a GST proceeding, the physical characteristics of the product are relevant. The authorised officer considers a sample necessary and takes one from the manufacturer's stock, providing a receipt.

Example 2 – Raw material

A dispute concerns the nature of a raw material used in production. A sample from the taxable person's possession may assist in establishing the physical characteristics relevant to the issue.

Example 3 – Multiple batches

A business has several batches of the same product. If batch characteristics matter, the relevant batch should be clearly identified in the business's internal records and the sampling receipt.

Example 4 – Warehouse stock

Goods are stored in a warehouse. The authority takes a sample from goods in the taxable person's possession. The company should reconcile the sampled quantity with its warehouse inventory records.

Example 5 – Packaged goods

A product is sold in multiple package sizes. If physical packaging is relevant to the proceeding, a sample may be taken from the stock available at the business premises.

Example 6 – Bulk commodity

A business stores goods in bulk. A sample may be relevant where physical composition or characteristics are part of the factual issue. The quantity and source should be documented carefully.

Example 7 – Engineering product

A technical product has specifications that are relevant to the GST issue. The physical sample can be connected with engineering specifications and other technical documents.

Example 8 – Sample quantity and stock records

A company has 10,000 units in stock and a small quantity is taken as a sample. The finance/inventory team should record the reduction so that later reconciliation does not incorrectly show unexplained shortage.

Example 9 – Expert assistance and sample

An officer may take a sample under Section 154 and may separately use expert assistance under Section 153 where specialist knowledge is needed. The two provisions serve different functions.

Example 10 – Sample does not itself determine tax

The fact that a sample was taken does not automatically establish a tax demand. The sample is relevant evidence whose significance depends on the legal and factual issue under examination.

Example 11 – Sample receipt missing from internal file

If the business cannot find its copy or record of the receipt, it may become difficult later to reconcile what was taken. This is why the receipt should be immediately scanned and linked to the proceeding file.

Example 12 – Multi-location business

A company operates several factories. Each sampling event should be tracked separately by GSTIN, location, product and proceeding reference to avoid mixing records between units.

15. Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 – Thinking every GST officer automatically has the power

Section 154 specifically names the Commissioner or an officer authorised by the Commissioner.

Mistake 2 – Ignoring the receipt

The section expressly requires a receipt for samples taken. The business should preserve it.

Mistake 3 – Treating sampling as a tax demand

Sampling is a factual/investigative step. It does not itself determine tax liability.

Mistake 4 – Failing to adjust inventory records

Sample quantities can create unexplained stock differences if they are not recorded internally.

Mistake 5 – Mixing samples from different GSTINs

Businesses with multiple registrations should maintain GSTIN-wise and location-wise sampling records.

Mistake 6 – Assuming the Act fixes a sample quantity

Section 154 itself does not prescribe a universal quantity or percentage.

Mistake 7 – Confusing an expert with the sampling authority

Section 153 and Section 154 are separate provisions with different functions.

Mistake 8 – Losing batch/lot identity

For batch-controlled products, the sampled batch should be identified wherever the business's records permit.

18. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 154 of the CGST Act?

Section 154 gives the Commissioner or an officer authorised by him the power to take samples of goods from the possession of a taxable person where the authority considers it necessary, with a receipt to be provided for samples taken.

2. Who can take samples under Section 154?

The Commissioner or an officer authorised by the Commissioner may take samples under the section.

3. Does the officer have to provide a receipt?

Yes. Section 154 expressly requires a receipt for any samples so taken.

4. Can samples be taken from any taxable person?

The statutory wording permits samples from the possession of any taxable person, where the competent authority considers sampling necessary.

5. Is sampling compulsory in every GST inspection?

No. Section 154 uses the condition “where he considers it necessary”; it does not prescribe routine sampling in every case.

6. Does Section 154 prescribe a fixed sample quantity?

No fixed universal quantity or percentage is specified in Section 154 itself.

7. Can a private expert take the sample?

Section 154 itself gives the sampling power to the Commissioner or an officer authorised by him. An expert does not independently receive that statutory power merely because expert assistance is involved.

8. Is Section 154 the same as Section 153?

No. Section 153 concerns taking assistance from an expert. Section 154 concerns taking samples of goods.

9. Does taking a sample mean tax is payable?

No. Sampling is a procedural/factual step. Tax liability depends on the applicable legal provisions and facts of the case.

10. What should a business do with the receipt?

Preserve it with the proceeding file and connect it with the inventory/sample register and relevant GSTIN/location records.

11. Can samples be taken from a warehouse?

Where the goods are in the possession of a taxable person and the statutory conditions are satisfied, the location can be a relevant place for sampling.

12. Should sample quantities be recorded in inventory?

As a practical internal control, yes. Recording sample movement helps reconcile book stock with physical stock.

13. Can sampling be relevant to product classification?

Potentially. Where physical or technical characteristics are relevant to a classification issue, a sample may assist the factual examination. The legal classification still depends on the applicable law and facts.

14. Can an expert test the sample?

Where technical examination is required, expert assistance may be relevant under the applicable process. Section 154 itself does not prescribe a universal laboratory or testing procedure.

15. Does Section 154 create a power of seizure?

Section 154 specifically concerns taking samples. The separate statutory powers relating to inspection, search and seizure must be considered under the relevant provisions, such as Section 67, where applicable.

16. Is Section 154 currently in force?

Yes. Section 154 is part of the CGST Act and has been in force from 1 July 2017.

17. Does Section 154 specify a time limit for testing a sample?

The section itself does not specify a general testing deadline.

18. What if a company has several GST registrations?

Maintain sampling records separately by GSTIN, location, product and proceeding reference so that samples and stock movements are not incorrectly attributed to another registration.

19. Quick Revision – Section 154

  • Section 154 = Power to take samples.
  • The power is given to the Commissioner or an officer authorised by him.
  • Samples are taken from goods in the possession of a taxable person.
  • The authority must consider sampling necessary.
  • A receipt must be provided for samples taken.
  • Section 154 itself does not prescribe a universal sample quantity or percentage.
  • Section 154 is different from Section 153, which deals with expert assistance.
  • Sampling itself does not establish tax liability, fraud or an offence.
  • Businesses should preserve the receipt and update inventory/sample records.
  • For technical products, retain specifications, batch records and other supporting documents.
