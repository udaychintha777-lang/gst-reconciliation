GST ACT — SECTION 73

Section 73 CGST Act — GST Demand, Show Cause Notice, Interest & Penalty

Section 73 is the non-fraud demand provision for tax not paid or short paid, erroneous refunds, and input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised for reasons other than fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. This guide explains the complete demand process in simple language, including SCN, DRC-01, payment before notice, representations, order, interest, penalty and the transition to Section 74A.

1. Why Section 73 matters

Searches such as “Section 73 CGST Act”, “Section 73 GST”, “GST demand notice”, “GST SCN”, “GST tax short paid”, “GST tax not paid”, “GST erroneous refund”, “wrongly availed ITC”, “GST penalty Section 73”, “DRC-01” and “GST demand order” are directly connected with this provision.

Section 73 is one of the most important GST demand provisions. It is used where tax has not been paid or has been short paid, a refund has been erroneously granted, or ITC has been wrongly availed or utilised, where the case is not based on fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.

Simple idea: Section 73 is the statutory route for determining and recovering certain GST shortfalls where the department is not proceeding on the fraud/suppression basis covered by the fraud-demand provision.

2. Section 73 in very simple language

Suppose a business filed its GST returns but later the department finds that ₹10 lakh of tax was not paid, or that ₹10 lakh of ITC was wrongly availed/utilised.

The department cannot simply treat its calculation as the final tax demand. The taxpayer is given a statutory opportunity to respond through the demand process.

Tax issue identified → voluntary payment opportunity → show cause notice → reply/hearing → adjudication order → payment/appeal

The exact process depends on the facts, tax period, nature of discrepancy and the statutory provision applicable to that period.

3. What Section 73 covers

Section 73 historically applies where it appears to the proper officer that:

  • tax has not been paid;
  • tax has been short paid;
  • tax has been erroneously refunded; or
  • input tax credit has been wrongly availed or utilised;

and the reason is other than fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.

The provision also prescribes the procedure for notice, voluntary payment, adjudication, interest and penalty.

4. What “other than fraud” means

This phrase is central to understanding Section 73.

The historical statutory structure separates ordinary/non-fraud demand cases from cases where the department alleges fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.

Section 73 typeTypical character
Non-fraudWrong tax treatment, calculation error, reconciliation difference or other tax shortfall without the statutory fraud/suppression allegation.
Fraud/suppression frameworkCases where the statutory ingredients of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax are alleged.
The classification should never be made merely from the amount involved. The actual allegations, evidence and statutory wording of the notice matter.

5. Four common types of demand

Tax not paid
Tax that should have been paid was completely omitted.
Tax short paid
Only part of the tax that should have been paid was discharged.
Erroneous refund
A refund was granted even though it was not legally due.
Wrong ITC
ITC was wrongly availed or utilised under the applicable law.

6. Payment before show cause notice

Section 73 contains an important voluntary-payment mechanism. Before service of notice, a person may pay the tax based on self-ascertainment or as ascertained by the proper officer, together with applicable interest under Section 50.

Where the statutory conditions are satisfied, payment before notice can prevent issuance of notice in respect of the amount so paid.

The penalty treatment depends on the exact stage and statutory subsection applicable to the payment.

This mechanism is useful where a business discovers a genuine short payment during reconciliation and wants to regularise the position rather than wait for formal adjudication.

7. Show cause notice

The show cause notice is the formal starting point of the adjudication process under Section 73 where the matter has not already been resolved through the statutory pre-notice payment mechanism.

The notice should identify the tax issue and require the person to show cause why the specified tax, interest and applicable penalty should not be recovered.

A good taxpayer response should examine:

  • tax period;
  • nature of supply/transaction;
  • tax rate and classification;
  • taxable value;
  • ITC eligibility;
  • payments already made;
  • interest calculation;
  • penalty basis;
  • limitation; and
  • supporting documents.

8. Statement for other periods

Where a notice has been issued for a period, Section 73 provides a mechanism for the proper officer to issue a statement containing details of tax shortfall or other specified demand for other periods, subject to the statutory conditions.

This is important because a recurring issue may exist across several tax periods. The taxpayer should therefore check the complete period-wise working rather than responding only to one invoice or month.

9. Reply and personal hearing

The taxpayer should use the opportunity to present the complete factual and legal position.

A strong reply should contain

  • issue-wise response;
  • facts and chronology;
  • legal position;
  • reconciliation of figures;
  • supporting documents;
  • payments already made;
  • interest calculation, where applicable;
  • objection to incorrect penalty/limitation where justified; and
  • a clear conclusion/request.

Where the taxpayer requests a personal hearing in accordance with the applicable law, the adjudicating authority must follow the statutory hearing framework.

10. Order and time limit

The original Section 73 framework contains separate time limits for issuance of notice and passing of the order. The order is required to be issued within the statutory period prescribed for the relevant tax period.

For the historical Section 73 framework, the show cause notice had to be issued at least three months before the statutory deadline for the order. The order was generally required within three years from the due date for the annual return for the relevant financial year, subject to the law and amendments applicable to the relevant period.

Important: Do not apply an old Section 73 limitation period mechanically to every current tax period. Section 74A now provides the common demand framework for specified periods, so the tax period and applicable law must be checked first.

11. Penalty under Section 73

Section 73 contains a specific penalty framework. The amount and whether the penalty is attracted depends on the stage of payment and the applicable subsection.

For example, where tax and interest are paid within the statutory period after service of notice, the law provides a different penalty consequence from a case where the demand proceeds to adjudication.

StagePractical significance
Before noticeStatutory voluntary-payment route may avoid notice for the amount paid if conditions are met.
Within prescribed period after noticePayment can have a reduced/limited penalty consequence under the applicable subsection.
After orderDemand, interest and applicable penalty follow the adjudication order, subject to appeal/recovery law.

12. Interest under Section 50

Section 73 demand generally has an interest component because Section 73 requires payment of interest under Section 50 along with the tax in the relevant circumstances.

Section 50 separately governs interest on delayed tax payment and interest on wrongly availed and utilised ITC.

For a demand working, the taxpayer should reconcile:

  • principal tax;
  • date tax became payable;
  • payments already made;
  • cash versus credit implications where relevant;
  • period of delay; and
  • applicable interest rate and Rule 88B calculation.

13. DRC-01, DRC-03 & DRC-07

GST demand proceedings are implemented through prescribed electronic forms and portal functionality.

FormCommon role
DRC-01Summary of show cause notice in the electronic demand framework.
DRC-03Intimation/payment of tax, interest, penalty or other amounts in prescribed circumstances.
DRC-07Summary of the order creating the demand.

The form number should never be considered in isolation. The underlying notice/order and statutory provision determine the taxpayer's actual legal position.

14. Section 73 vs Section 74

Section 73Section 74
Historically applies where tax/ITC issue arises for a reason other than fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.Applies to specified cases involving fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.
Lower statutory penalty consequences.Higher penalty structure.
Shorter historical limitation framework.Longer historical limitation framework.

The department must establish the statutory basis for using the fraud/suppression provision. A large tax amount by itself does not automatically convert a case into a Section 74 case.

15. Section 73 vs Section 74A

This distinction is essential for current GST research.

The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 introduced Section 74A as a common determination framework for tax not paid/short paid, erroneous refunds and wrongly availed or utilised ITC for the relevant tax periods. Accordingly, Section 73 should not simply be described as the current provision for every future tax period.

Tax-period rule: Always identify the financial year/period first. Then determine whether Section 73, Section 74, Section 74A or another provision applies to that period.

16. Section 128A waiver connection

Section 128A was introduced with effect from 1 November 2024 to provide a conditional waiver of interest or penalty, or both, relating to certain Section 73 demands for FY 2017-18, FY 2018-19 and FY 2019-20.

CBIC Circular No. 238/32/2024-GST explains the statutory and procedural framework. The benefit was subject to conditions including payment of the full tax demanded within the notified time limit. citeturn0search5

Section 128A is a special transitional relief. It should not be presented as a general waiver of interest and penalty for all Section 73 demands.

18. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Output tax short payment

A business discovers that ₹2 lakh of output tax was omitted from GSTR-3B. It can examine the statutory voluntary-payment route before formal notice.

Example 2 — ITC wrongly availed

An invoice was booked and ITC claimed, but later the business finds that the statutory eligibility conditions were not satisfied. The amount and interest need to be evaluated.

Example 3 — Rate error

A taxable supply was reported at 5% when 12% was applicable. The difference can become a tax short-payment issue.

Example 4 — RCM omission

A reverse-charge transaction was missed. The business should determine tax, interest and applicable compliance consequences.

Example 5 — GSTR-1/GSTR-3B mismatch

GSTR-1 reports higher taxable supplies than GSTR-3B. The reconciliation may establish whether it is a timing/error issue or an actual short payment.

Example 6 — Erroneous refund

A refund was sanctioned in excess of the legally admissible amount. The excess can be subject to recovery under the applicable demand framework.

Example 7 — Exempt turnover

A supply was incorrectly treated as exempt. The business should prepare the contract, notification, classification and tax calculation before responding to a demand.

Example 8 — Construction RA bills

A contractor applied an incorrect GST treatment to RA bills across several months. The tax-period-wise calculation and payment trail should be reconstructed.

Example 9 — Subcontractor ITC

ITC was claimed on subcontractor invoices but supporting receipt/payment/tax conditions are disputed. The complete work-order and payment evidence should be prepared.

Example 10 — Demand across several periods

A recurring classification error affects multiple financial years. The taxpayer should review every period and the applicable limitation before accepting a consolidated calculation.

19. Construction and infrastructure examples

Works-contract rate dispute

21. Practical safeguards

  • Check the exact tax period before accepting the legal provision cited.
  • Read the entire SCN, not merely the demand amount.
  • Reconcile every figure with books and returns.
  • Separate principal tax, interest and penalty.
  • Check limitation independently.
  • Verify whether the allegation actually satisfies the statutory basis for Section 73.
  • Do not accept a departmental working merely because it appears in a DRC form.
  • Maintain documentary evidence supporting the factual response.
  • Use the hearing opportunity effectively.
  • Obtain professional advice for material demands.

24. Common mistakes

  • Assuming every GST demand is automatically Section 73.
  • Ignoring the distinction between Section 73, Section 74 and Section 74A.
  • Accepting a demand calculation without checking the tax period.
  • Ignoring limitation.
  • Not separating tax, interest and penalty.
  • Failing to reconcile DRC-01 figures with books.
  • Responding without supporting documents.
  • Missing the personal hearing opportunity.
  • Confusing a mismatch with proof of tax evasion.
  • Ignoring special relief such as Section 128A where the relevant period qualifies.

26. Section 73 GST FAQs

What is Section 73 of the CGST Act?

Section 73 is the historical non-fraud demand provision for tax not paid or short paid, erroneous refunds and wrongly availed or utilised ITC where the case is not based on fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.

What is a Section 73 GST notice?

It is a show cause notice requiring the taxpayer to explain why specified tax, interest and applicable penalty should not be recovered under the statutory demand framework.

What is DRC-01?

DRC-01 is the electronic summary associated with a GST show cause notice in the demand framework.

Can tax be paid before a Section 73 notice?

Yes. Section 73 contains a voluntary-payment mechanism before service of notice, subject to the statutory conditions.

What happens after a Section 73 notice?

The taxpayer can submit a reply, provide evidence and seek a personal hearing where applicable. The proper officer then determines the amount through the statutory adjudication process.

Does Section 73 always include penalty?

Penalty consequences depend on the stage of payment and the specific statutory subsection. The taxpayer should not assume that every case has the same penalty.

What is the difference between Section 73 and Section 74?

Historically, Section 73 covers non-fraud cases, while Section 74 applies where fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax is alleged under the statutory conditions.

What is Section 74A?

Section 74A is the newer common demand framework introduced for specified tax periods. The applicable provision must be determined by checking the relevant tax period and commencement provisions.

How long does GST have to issue a Section 73 order?

For the historical Section 73 framework, the order was generally subject to a three-year period from the due date for the annual return, with the notice required at least three months before the order deadline. Always verify the law applicable to the relevant period.

Can a Section 73 demand be challenged?

Yes. A taxpayer can contest the factual calculation, legal interpretation, limitation, interest, penalty and other grounds through the statutory adjudication and appeal framework.

Can Section 73 apply to wrongly availed ITC?

Yes, where the statutory conditions are satisfied and the case falls within the non-fraud framework applicable to the relevant period.

Can an erroneous GST refund be recovered under Section 73?

Yes, the provision historically includes erroneous refunds within its scope.

What is Section 128A?

Section 128A provides a conditional waiver of interest or penalty, or both, for specified Section 73 demands relating to FY 2017-18, FY 2018-19 and FY 2019-20, subject to its conditions.

28. Key takeaways

  • Section 73 is the historical non-fraud GST demand provision.
  • It covers tax not paid, short paid, erroneous refunds and wrongly availed or utilised ITC, subject to the statutory conditions.
  • The taxpayer gets a statutory opportunity to respond before adjudication.
  • Voluntary payment before notice can have important consequences under the statutory scheme.
  • Tax, interest and penalty must be analysed separately.
  • Limitation should always be checked independently.
  • Section 73 should not be confused with the fraud/suppression framework or the newer Section 74A framework.
  • Section 128A is a special, period-specific relief and is not a general waiver.
  • DRC forms communicate the proceeding but do not replace the underlying statutory analysis.
  • For every demand, identify the tax period first and then determine the correct statutory provision.
