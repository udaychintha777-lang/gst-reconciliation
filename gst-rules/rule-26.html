1. Rule 26 at a glance

Rule 26 of the CGST Rules, 2017 deals with the method of authentication of documents submitted under the GST Rules. It is the rule that connects electronic filing with the legally recognised signature or verification mechanism.

Rule 26 is important because GST compliance is heavily portal-based. Filing a return, replying to a notice, submitting an application or filing an appeal is not complete merely because information has been entered into the portal; the prescribed authentication/verification requirement must also be satisfied.

Core principle: Documents covered by the rule are submitted electronically using a digital signature certificate, e-signature, or another mode of signature/verification notified by the Board.

2. Electronic submission and authentication

Rule 26(1) provides that applications, replies to notices, returns including details of outward and inward supplies, appeals and other documents required under the GST Rules are to be submitted electronically with the prescribed authentication.

Authentication routeMeaning in practice
Digital Signature Certificate (DSC)Cryptographic digital signature used by eligible taxpayers/authorised signatories for portal filings where DSC is prescribed or selected.
E-signatureElectronic signature mechanism recognised under the Information Technology Act framework and enabled for the relevant GST transaction.
Other notified modeAnother signature or verification method specifically notified by the Board for the relevant filing or taxpayer category.

The exact authentication screen and permitted method can depend on the taxpayer type, filing and portal process. Therefore, a business should not assume that one authentication method is universally available for every GST action.

3. Who must sign or verify?

Rule 26(2) sets out who is responsible for signing or verifying documents furnished online. The authorised person depends on the legal constitution of the taxpayer.

Taxpayer typePerson who signs/verifies
IndividualThe individual himself; where the rule permits, an authorised person may act in the specified circumstances.
Hindu Undivided Family (HUF)The Karta, subject to the rule's provisions for absence from India or incapacity.
CompanyThe Chief Executive Officer or an authorised signatory.
Government / Government agency / local authorityAn officer authorised for this purpose.
FirmA partner who is not a minor or an authorised signatory, as permitted by the rule.
Other associationA member of the association or an authorised signatory.
TrustThe trustee or an authorised signatory.
Other personA competent person or a person authorised in accordance with Section 48, as applicable.

4. Companies and authorised signatories

For a company, Rule 26 specifically recognises the Chief Executive Officer or authorised signatory for signing/verification. In practical GST administration, this makes maintenance of the authorised-signatory master particularly important.

Finance-team checks

  • Confirm the authorised signatory is correctly reflected in the GST registration profile.
  • Ensure DSC ownership and validity are monitored where DSC is used.
  • Remove or update former employees/directors/signatories when authority changes.
  • Maintain internal approval evidence for high-value or sensitive filings.
  • Do not share DSC credentials or private keys between employees.
Security point: A DSC is not simply a username/password. It is a legally significant digital-signature credential. Keep the private key/token and PIN under controlled access.

5. Firms, HUFs, trusts and other entities

The entity-wise provisions matter when a GST registration is held by a partnership firm, HUF, trust, association or other legal/person category.

For example, a partnership firm's GST filing should be linked to an eligible partner or properly authorised signatory rather than an employee who has no authority to represent the firm.

Practical issue: An employee may prepare the return, reconciliation or reply, but preparation and legal authentication are separate functions. The authorised person must complete the prescribed verification/signature.

6. Notices, certificates and orders

Rule 26(3) deals with notices, certificates and orders issued under the relevant GST Rules. These are issued electronically by the proper officer or other authorised officer through the prescribed digital-signature, e-signature or other notified verification mechanism.

This is important when a taxpayer downloads a GST notice, certificate or order from the portal. The electronic document and its authentication should be preserved as part of the statutory record.

For audit files

  • Keep the original downloaded notice/order/certificate.
  • Record the date and time of download where your document-control policy requires it.
  • Preserve the communication/reference number.
  • Keep the corresponding reply and acknowledgement together.

7. Practical examples

Example 1 — Company filing a GST return

The accounts team prepares the return and management reviews the workings. The authorised signatory then completes the prescribed electronic authentication. Preparation by the accountant does not replace the legal verification requirement.

Example 2 — Reply to a GST notice

A tax consultant drafts a response to a departmental notice. The taxpayer can use professional assistance for preparation, but the reply must be submitted using the prescribed authentication by the person authorised to act for the taxpayer.

Example 3 — Partnership firm

A partner authorises a responsible employee to prepare GST filings. The employee may perform the operational work, but the filing should be authenticated through the partner/authorised-signatory arrangement recognised in the GST registration and Rule 26.

Example 4 — Change of authorised signatory

A company replaces its finance head. The GST master should be updated where the change affects the authorised-signatory structure, and DSC access belonging to the former employee should be disabled according to the company's security policy.

8. Common authentication mistakes

  • Using a DSC belonging to a person who is no longer authorised.
  • Allowing an employee to control another person's private DSC credentials.
  • Assuming return preparation and return authentication are the same responsibility.
  • Failing to update authorised-signatory details after a management change.
  • Ignoring DSC expiry or token/driver issues until the filing deadline.
  • Assuming EVC or another verification mode is available for every taxpayer and every filing.
  • Not retaining filing acknowledgements and electronically issued notices/orders.

11. FAQs

What is Rule 26 of the CGST Rules?

Rule 26 prescribes the methods by which applications, replies, returns, appeals and other documents covered by the GST Rules are electronically authenticated.

Is DSC mandatory for every GST filing?

Not necessarily. Rule 26 recognises DSC, e-signature and other modes of signature or verification notified by the Board. The applicable method depends on the taxpayer and the particular GST process.

Who can sign for a company?

Rule 26 recognises the Chief Executive Officer or an authorised signatory of the company.

Who can sign for a partnership firm?

The rule recognises a partner who is not a minor or an authorised signatory, subject to the applicable GST registration and portal process.

Can an accountant prepare a GST return?

Yes, preparation and authentication can be separate functions. The return must ultimately be authenticated in the prescribed manner by the person authorised under the applicable rules.

What happens if the authorised signatory leaves the company?

The company should update its GST registration/authorised-signatory records where required and ensure the former person's DSC and portal access are no longer used for the company's filings.

Are GST notices also electronically authenticated?

Rule 26(3) provides for notices, certificates and orders under the relevant Rules to be issued electronically through the prescribed authentication mechanism.

12. Compliance checklist

  • ☐ Maintain GSTIN-wise authorised-signatory records.
  • ☐ Ensure the correct person authenticates filings.
  • ☐ Track DSC validity where DSC is used.
  • ☐ Protect DSC private keys and PINs.
  • ☐ Review signatory access when employees/directors/partners change.
  • ☐ Do not assume EVC is universally available.
  • ☐ Preserve filing acknowledgements and electronically issued notices/orders.
  • ☐ Maintain maker-checker controls for important GST submissions.
