GST ACT — SECTION 81

Section 81 CGST Act — Transfer of Property to Defeat GST Recovery & Void Transfers

Section 81 protects Government revenue where a person, after an amount has become due, creates a charge on or parts with property with the intention of defrauding Government revenue. This detailed guide explains the provision in simple language, including sale, mortgage, exchange, other transfers, fraudulent intent, adequate consideration, good faith, notice, previous permission of the proper officer and practical property due-diligence controls.

1. Why Section 81 matters

Searches such as “Section 81 CGST Act”, “Section 81 GST”, “GST transfer of property”, “GST property transfer to avoid recovery”, “GST fraudulent transfer of property”, “can GST department challenge property sale”, “GST mortgage after demand”, “Section 81 void transfer”, “GST property attachment” and “GST recovery property transfer” relate directly to this provision.

GST recovery does not only concern money sitting in a bank account. A taxpayer with a significant outstanding liability may also own land, buildings, machinery or other assets. If property is deliberately transferred after the liability has become due with the intention of defeating Government revenue, Section 81 protects the Government's claim.

The provision is therefore important for both sides of a transaction: the taxpayer transferring property and the person acquiring or taking a charge over that property.

Simple idea: A person cannot deliberately move or encumber property after a GST amount has become due merely to put that property beyond the Government's recovery claim.

2. Section 81 in very simple language

Suppose Company A owes the Government a large GST amount. After the amount becomes due, Company A transfers its only valuable property to another person without genuine commercial substance, intending to prevent the Government from recovering the GST.

Section 81 says that such a charge or transfer can be void against the Government's claim, subject to the statutory exception.

GST amount due → Property transfer/charge → Intention to defraud revenue → Transfer may be void against Government claim

But the law does not automatically invalidate every property transaction made after a GST amount becomes due. The proviso protects certain genuine transactions, including transactions made for adequate consideration and in good faith without the relevant notice, and transactions made with previous permission of the proper officer.

3. What Section 81 says

The core statutory rule provides that where a person, after any amount has become due from him, creates a charge on or parts with property belonging to him or in his possession by sale, mortgage, exchange or any other mode of transfer, with the intention of defrauding the Government revenue, that charge or transfer is void as against the Government's claim for tax or any other sum payable by that person.

The proviso creates an important protection: the charge or transfer is not void where it is made for adequate consideration, in good faith and without notice of the pendency of proceedings under the Act or of the tax/other sum payable, or where it is made with the previous permission of the proper officer.

Main ruleProviso
Fraudulent transfer intended to defeat Government revenue can be void against the Government claim.Specified genuine transactions are protected when the statutory conditions are satisfied.

5. “After any amount has become due”

The timing of the transaction is critical.

The provision applies to a transaction occurring after any amount has become due from the person.

Therefore, a professional review should establish a clear chronology:

Event 1
Tax liability/demand arises.
Event 2
Amount becomes due.
Event 3
Property transaction occurs.
Event 4
Government recovery claim is considered.

Timing alone does not prove fraudulent intent. But a transaction immediately after a material GST liability becomes due may require closer examination of the commercial purpose, consideration, parties, funding and surrounding circumstances.

6. Creating a charge

Section 81 is not limited to an outright sale. It also covers creation of a charge over property.

In practical terms, a charge can give another person a legal/economic claim or security interest over an asset. If a taxpayer creates such a charge with the prohibited intention of defeating Government revenue, Section 81 can become relevant.

For finance teams, this means a property transaction review should consider not only sale deeds but also mortgages, security arrangements and other encumbrances.

7. Parting with property

The words “parts with the property” are deliberately broad. Section 81 covers a transfer of property belonging to the person or property in the person's possession.

The provision therefore should not be reduced to a simple “sale of land” rule.

Potentially relevant transactions may include:

  • sale;
  • mortgage;
  • exchange;
  • other forms of transfer;
  • transactions creating a charge over property.

The exact legal effect of a particular transaction must be determined from the documents and applicable property law.

8. Sale, mortgage, exchange and other transfers

TransactionSection 81 question
SaleWas property sold after an amount became due and was the sale intended to defraud Government revenue?
MortgageWas a security interest created to defeat the Government's claim?
ExchangeWas property exchanged with prohibited intent?
Other transferDoes the transaction fall within another mode of transferring/parting with property?

The existence of one of these transaction types does not by itself make the transaction void. The statutory ingredients and the proviso must be examined.

9. Intention to defraud Government revenue

This is the central factual issue in Section 81.

The statute does not say that every transfer made after a GST amount becomes due is void. It targets a transfer made with the intention of defrauding Government revenue.

In a real dispute, intention may need to be assessed from surrounding evidence, such as:

✓ Timing of transaction
✓ Relationship between parties
✓ Commercial purpose
✓ Sale consideration
✓ Payment trail
✓ Valuation
✓ Continued possession/use
✓ Existing GST proceedings
✓ Board/management records
✓ Funding arrangements
Important: A later GST demand does not automatically prove that an earlier transaction was fraudulent. The facts, timing and statutory conditions must be analysed carefully.

10. What “void” means

Section 81 does not simply say that the property transaction never existed for every purpose. It says the charge or transfer shall be void as against any claim in respect of tax or other sums payable by the person.

The phrase “as against” is important. The statutory effect is directed at protecting the Government's claim.

Therefore, in a live transaction or litigation, legal counsel should examine the precise statutory effect, property documents, purchaser rights and other applicable laws instead of describing the transaction as universally cancelled.

11. Government's claim

The protected claim is not limited to the word “tax”. The section refers to any claim in respect of any tax or any other sum payable by the person.

Accordingly, the finance team should identify the complete outstanding amount and its statutory basis before assessing Section 81 exposure.

12. Exceptions to the main rule

The proviso is extremely important because it prevents the provision from treating every genuine third-party transaction as automatically void.

The statutory protection covers a charge or transfer where:

  • it is made for adequate consideration;
  • it is made in good faith;
  • it is made without notice of the pendency of proceedings under the Act or without notice of the tax/other sum payable; or
  • it is made with the previous permission of the proper officer.

The precise drafting of the proviso matters. In an actual dispute, the evidence should be mapped against each relevant statutory requirement.

13. Adequate consideration

A genuine transaction normally has an identifiable commercial price or consideration. Section 81 specifically refers to adequate consideration.

Therefore, when a property is transferred during a period of GST exposure, maintain:

  • valuation report where appropriate;
  • sale agreement;
  • registered deed;
  • bank payment evidence;
  • accounting entry;
  • board/partner approval;
  • commercial justification; and
  • evidence of actual possession/transfer.

A nominal or unexplained consideration can raise questions. However, adequacy must be considered in the factual and legal context; it should not be judged solely by an unsupported assumption.

14. Good faith

Good faith is another important part of the proviso.

A transaction is more defensible where it has a genuine commercial purpose, transparent documentation, normal payment channels and no evidence that it was structured simply to defeat tax recovery.

Examples of legitimate commercial reasons can include:

  • sale of a surplus asset;
  • genuine business restructuring;
  • refinancing/security creation;
  • sale of a non-core property;
  • business transfer for commercial reasons.

Again, the existence of a commercial explanation does not by itself decide the legal issue. The entire factual record matters.

15. Notice of proceedings or tax

The proviso refers to absence of notice of the pendency of proceedings under the Act or notice of the tax/other sum payable.

This is particularly important for a third-party purchaser or lender.

Before acquiring or taking security over significant property from a taxpayer with known GST exposure, due diligence should consider:

GST proceedings
Notices, orders, appeals and recovery.
Tax dues
Outstanding statutory liabilities.
Property records
Title and existing encumbrances.
Commercial evidence
Price, funding and transaction purpose.

16. Previous permission of the proper officer

The proviso also recognises a transfer made with the previous permission of the proper officer.

This should not be confused with an informal conversation with a departmental officer. Where a transaction relies on statutory permission, the taxpayer should preserve the actual written approval and identify the property, transaction and relevant authority.

Control: If a transaction is being structured specifically around Section 81, obtain and preserve the formal permission before completing the transfer rather than relying on oral assurance.

18. Section 78 connection

Section 78 concerns initiation of recovery proceedings after an order and the payment period. Section 81 addresses a different problem: a person attempting to place property beyond the Government's recovery claim.

Therefore, when a major demand exists, the timeline should show:

Demand/order → Section 78 payment period → unpaid amount → Section 79 recovery → Section 81 property-transfer protection where applicable

19. Section 79 connection

Section 79 is the principal GST recovery provision. Section 81 supports that recovery framework by preventing certain deliberately structured property transfers from defeating the Government's claim.

It is useful to remember:

ProvisionMain purpose
Section 79How Government can recover unpaid amounts.
Section 81Protects the recovery claim against specified fraudulent property transfers.

20. Section 80 instalments

If an eligible taxpayer genuinely cannot pay an amount immediately, Section 80 may provide a payment-time/instalment mechanism subject to its conditions.

This is important because a taxpayer considering a property sale should not assume that transferring property is the only solution to a GST cash-flow problem.

Where Section 80 is legally available, a taxpayer should consider the statutory payment option before entering into a transaction that could raise Section 81 questions.

21. Section 82 vs Section 81

Section 82 states that, notwithstanding anything contained in other laws, any amount payable by a taxable person or another specified person in respect of tax, interest or penalty is a first charge on the property of that person, subject to the statutory exceptions in Section 82 itself.

Section 81 and Section 82 therefore address different legal problems.

Section 81Section 82
Focuses on certain transfers/charges intended to defraud Government revenue.Creates a statutory first-charge framework over property, subject to specified exceptions.
Contains a detailed proviso protecting certain genuine transfers.Contains its own statutory exceptions.

22. Section 83 vs Section 81

Section 83 deals with provisional attachment to protect revenue in specified circumstances. Section 81 deals with property transfers after an amount has become due and fraudulent intent to defeat Government revenue.

Section 81Section 83
Transfer/charge protection after amount becomes due.Provisional attachment to protect revenue in specified proceedings.
Focuses on defeating a Government claim through transfer.Focuses on temporarily securing property during specified proceedings.

These provisions should not be used interchangeably.

23. Insolvency and other laws

Property can also be affected by insolvency proceedings, secured lending, court orders, corporate law and property law.

Therefore, Section 81 should not be analysed in isolation in a complex transaction.

For an insolvent or financially distressed company, legal counsel should map:

  • GST dues;
  • GST recovery status;
  • insolvency proceedings;
  • moratorium, if applicable;
  • secured creditors;
  • property title;
  • court/NCLT orders; and
  • the proposed transaction.

24. Property buyer due diligence

A buyer purchasing property from a taxpayer with known GST exposure should not rely only on the seller's statement that “there is no issue”.

Recommended checks

✓ GST registration details
✓ Outstanding demand disclosure
✓ Notices/orders
✓ Recovery proceedings
✓ Tax dues certificate/evidence where appropriate
✓ Property title
✓ Existing charges
✓ Consideration/payment trail
✓ Valuation
✓ Seller's authority
✓ Written permissions if relevant
✓ Legal opinion for material transactions

This is especially important for high-value land, buildings, project assets and business transfers.

25. Seller-side compliance

Before selling a major asset while a GST demand is outstanding, the finance/legal team should document the commercial reason for the sale and ensure the transaction is not structured to defeat Government recovery.

Maintain:

  • board/management approval;
  • valuation;
  • commercial justification;
  • buyer due diligence;
  • banking trail;
  • tax assessment;
  • GST demand status;
  • recovery status; and
  • proper officer permission where the transaction relies upon it.

26. Banks and secured lenders

Banks and other lenders may take security over property. When the borrower has significant GST exposure, lenders should conduct appropriate statutory and title due diligence.

A security transaction should not be assumed to be protected merely because it is described as a “bank mortgage”. Section 81's statutory wording covers mortgages, while the proviso and the full facts determine the legal position.

For lenders, the practical file should contain:

  • sanction documents;
  • security documents;
  • valuation;
  • disbursement evidence;
  • GST due-diligence records;
  • knowledge/notice analysis; and
  • legal review where material.

27. Construction and infrastructure examples

Sale of project land

A construction company with a large GST demand proposes to sell land. The transaction should be reviewed for timing, valuation, buyer relationship, consideration, commercial purpose and recovery status.

Sale of machinery

A contractor sells high-value cranes or batching equipment after a GST demand becomes due. The finance team should document why the equipment is surplus and preserve actual consideration/payment evidence.

Mortgage for working capital

A contractor mortgages project property to a bank to fund genuine working capital. Because Section 81 expressly refers to mortgage, the transaction should receive proper legal review where GST dues are outstanding.

Business transfer

A construction business transfers a project/business undertaking to another entity. The transaction should be supported by commercial documentation and GST due-diligence rather than being structured merely to move assets away from the original taxpayer.

Group-company transfer

A company transfers land to a group entity after a GST demand arises. The related-party relationship and commercial consideration should be carefully documented because a connected-party transaction can receive closer scrutiny.

28. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Genuine sale for adequate consideration

A company sells an unused building for a commercially supported price through normal banking channels. The transaction has a genuine business purpose and the buyer had no relevant notice. The Section 81 proviso becomes important; the transaction is not automatically void merely because the seller has GST dues.

Example 2 — Transfer to related party for nominal amount

A company with a large GST liability transfers valuable land to a related entity for a nominal unexplained amount. The timing, relationship, valuation and payment trail may raise serious questions about the intention behind the transfer.

Example 3 — Mortgage to bank

A company creates a mortgage after a GST amount becomes due. Because mortgage is expressly covered, the transaction requires careful Section 81 analysis, including the statutory proviso and the bank's knowledge.

Example 4 — Sale before tax liability became due

A company sells property for genuine business reasons before the relevant amount became due. Section 81's timing requirement must be examined before alleging that the provision applies.

Example 5 — Purchaser had notice

A buyer purchases property after receiving information that the seller has a pending GST liability/proceeding. The proviso's no-notice condition must be carefully examined.

Example 6 — Prior permission

A taxpayer obtains written previous permission from the proper officer for the transaction. The transaction should preserve the actual permission and ensure that the completed transaction matches the permission.

Example 7 — Commercial restructuring

A group reorganises its business for documented commercial reasons and transfers assets for genuine consideration. The transaction should preserve the restructuring documents, valuations and payment trail.

Example 8 — Asset sale used to fund GST

A company sells surplus machinery and uses the proceeds to pay its GST liability. The commercial purpose and actual payment of the GST can be relevant evidence that the sale was not designed to defeat Government recovery.

Example 9 — Retained possession after sale

A seller claims to have sold property but continues to control it without a credible commercial explanation. This can create factual questions about the substance and purpose of the transaction.

Example 10 — Undocumented family transfer

A taxpayer transfers valuable property to a family member after a major GST liability arises. The relationship, consideration, payment and purpose should be examined carefully.

Example 11 — Bank refinancing

A borrower refinances an existing secured loan. The transaction should preserve lender documents, valuation and funding trail and should be reviewed against the statutory notice/permission requirements.

Example 12 — Government receivable versus property sale

A contractor has large project receivables and proposes selling property to generate cash for GST. Before the sale, it should consider whether a lawful payment arrangement or other solution is available and document the commercial necessity of the transaction.

32. Common mistakes

  • Assuming every property transfer after a GST demand is automatically void.
  • Ignoring the requirement of intention to defraud Government revenue.
  • Failing to establish when the amount actually became due.
  • Using nominal consideration without commercial justification.
  • Transferring property to a related party without proper documentation.
  • Relying on an oral statement from a departmental officer instead of written permission.
  • Ignoring buyer/lender notice and due-diligence evidence.
  • Confusing Section 81 with Section 83 provisional attachment.
  • Confusing Section 81 with Section 82 first charge.
  • Failing to review insolvency and secured-creditor implications.
  • Not documenting the commercial reason for an asset sale.
  • Completing a transaction before obtaining required legal/tax sign-off.

34. Section 81 GST FAQs

What is Section 81 of the CGST Act?

Section 81 deals with certain transfers or charges over property made after an amount becomes due, where the transaction is intended to defraud Government revenue.

Does Section 81 make every property sale after a GST demand void?

No. The main provision requires the specified conditions, including intention to defraud Government revenue. The proviso also protects specified genuine transactions.

Can GST department challenge a property transfer?

Section 81 provides statutory protection for the Government's claim against qualifying fraudulent transfers. The exact facts and statutory conditions must be established.

Does Section 81 apply to mortgage?

Yes. Mortgage is expressly mentioned in the section.

Does Section 81 apply to exchange?

Yes. Exchange is expressly mentioned.

What is the most important condition in Section 81?

The transfer/charge must be made with the intention of defrauding Government revenue, along with the other statutory ingredients.

What is the Section 81 proviso?

It protects a charge or transfer made for adequate consideration, in good faith and without the specified notice, or made with previous permission of the proper officer.

What is adequate consideration?

It refers to genuine consideration supporting the transaction. The evidence should include the transaction value, valuation where appropriate and actual payment trail.

What does good faith mean in Section 81?

It generally requires a genuine transaction rather than a transaction structured to defeat Government revenue. The facts and evidence determine the issue.

What if the buyer did not know about the GST dues?

The no-notice condition in the proviso can become important, subject to the exact facts and other requirements.

Can previous permission protect the transfer?

The proviso expressly recognises a transfer made with previous permission of the proper officer. Preserve the formal written permission.

What is the difference between Section 81 and Section 82?

Section 81 addresses certain fraudulent transfers/charges; Section 82 deals with the statutory first charge on property, subject to its exceptions.

What is the difference between Section 81 and Section 83?

Section 81 deals with fraudulent transfers after an amount becomes due; Section 83 concerns provisional attachment to protect revenue in specified proceedings.

Does Section 81 apply to machinery?

The provision refers broadly to property. A transaction involving machinery should therefore be analysed based on the nature of the property and the statutory conditions.

Does Section 81 apply to related-party transfers?

Related-party status does not by itself prove fraud, but it can make the transaction's commercial purpose, valuation and payment trail particularly important.

Can a company sell an asset to pay GST?

A genuine sale can be commercially legitimate. The company should document the purpose, consideration, payment and GST/recovery status and obtain professional review where the transaction occurs in a material recovery context.

Can a bank mortgage be affected by Section 81?

Mortgage is expressly covered by the wording. The transaction should therefore be reviewed against the main rule and proviso, including notice, good faith, consideration and any applicable permission.

What should a buyer check before buying property from a GST defaulter?

Review GST dues, proceedings, recovery status, title, charges, consideration, valuation, seller authority and the buyer's notice/due-diligence position.

Is Section 81 the same as GST property attachment?

No. Section 81 concerns specified transfers/charges; attachment and sale are addressed through the recovery framework and other provisions.

36. Key takeaways

  • Section 81 protects Government revenue from specified fraudulent property transfers.
  • The timing matters: the provision applies where an amount has become due before the relevant transfer/charge.
  • Sale, mortgage, exchange and other modes of transfer can fall within the wording.
  • Fraudulent intention to defeat Government revenue is a central element.
  • Not every transfer after a GST demand is automatically void.
  • The proviso protects specified genuine transactions involving adequate consideration, good faith and the required absence of notice, or previous permission of the proper officer.
  • Property buyers and lenders should conduct GST due diligence where the seller/borrower has material GST exposure.
  • Section 81 should be distinguished from Section 79 recovery, Section 82 first charge and Section 83 provisional attachment.
  • Construction and infrastructure businesses should review land, buildings, machinery, project assets and mortgages before executing major transactions when GST recovery exposure exists.
  • Maintain a complete evidence trail: demand, timing, valuation, consideration, payment, commercial purpose, notice and permission.
