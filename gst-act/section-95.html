1. Why Section 95 matters

Section 95 is short, but it defines the vocabulary used throughout the GST advance-ruling system.

If you do not understand what “advance ruling”, “applicant”, “application”, “Authority” and “Appellate Authority” mean, it becomes difficult to understand Sections 96 to 106.

For a business, the practical benefit is that the definitions tell you who may enter the advance-ruling process, what kind of decision is being sought and which authorities are involved.

Simple way to remember Section 95: it tells you what the key words in the advance-ruling chapter mean. The actual questions that can be asked are mainly dealt with in Section 97(2).

3. Section 95 in simple language

Think of Chapter XVII as the GST “advance ruling” system.

Section 95 is the chapter's dictionary. It defines the words that appear repeatedly in the following sections.

Advance ruling

A decision of the appropriate advance-ruling authority on specified GST questions relating to a supply being undertaken or proposed to be undertaken by the applicant.

Applicant

A person who is already registered or who wants to obtain GST registration.

Application

The application made to the Authority under Section 97(1).

Authority

The Authority for Advance Ruling constituted under Section 96.

Appellate Authority

The Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling referred to in Section 99.

5. What is an advance ruling?

An advance ruling is not simply an informal opinion from a tax consultant or a departmental officer.

It is a statutory decision under the GST advance-ruling chapter on questions falling within the permitted scope.

Section 95(a) links the definition to the questions specified in Section 97(2) for an original ruling and Section 100(1) for an appeal.

Practical meaning: You cannot use the advance-ruling mechanism to ask every possible GST question. First identify whether the question falls within the statutory list in Section 97(2).

6. What does “being undertaken or proposed to be undertaken” mean?

This phrase is extremely important.

The definition covers supplies that are being undertaken as well as supplies that are proposed to be undertaken by the applicant.

Therefore, the GST advance-ruling system is not limited only to a completely hypothetical future business. It can also address a qualifying activity that the applicant is currently undertaking, subject to the other statutory requirements.

At the same time, the advance-ruling mechanism is not a general forum for obtaining a ruling on any unrelated historical transaction simply because a taxpayer wants an answer after the event.

7. Who is an “applicant” under Section 95?

Section 95(c) uses a deliberately broad formulation: an applicant means any person registered or desirous of obtaining registration under the Act.

This means the definition is not restricted only to businesses that already possess a GSTIN.

Already registered
A registered person can qualify as an applicant.
Desirous of registration
A person intending to obtain registration can also fall within the definition.
But eligibility is not the same as maintainability. Being an “applicant” under Section 95 does not mean that every question or every transaction automatically qualifies for an advance ruling. Sections 97, 98 and the other Chapter XVII provisions still have to be satisfied.

8. Can an unregistered person apply?

Yes, the definition expressly includes a person desirous of obtaining registration.

So an unregistered person is not automatically excluded merely because a GST registration certificate has not yet been issued.

However, the person must genuinely fall within the statutory concept of being desirous of obtaining registration, and the question must otherwise be one on which an advance ruling can be sought.

A recent Rajasthan High Court decision also considered the scope of the word “applicant” in Section 95 and rejected an overly narrow reading that would exclude a person merely because of the nature of the applicant's tax liability. citeturn1search7

9. What is an “application”?

Section 95(d) defines an application as an application made to the Authority under Section 97(1).

This creates a direct link between Section 95 and the formal advance-ruling process.

The application is not merely an email asking the department for an opinion. The applicant has to follow the prescribed form, manner and fee requirements under the advance-ruling rules.

10. What is the Authority for Advance Ruling?

Section 95(e) refers to the Authority for Advance Ruling constituted under Section 96.

In practical language, this is the first-level statutory forum that considers the advance-ruling application.

Section 95 itself does not explain the complete composition, appointment or procedure of the Authority. Those matters are dealt with in the following provisions, especially Section 96 and the relevant Rules.

11. What is the Appellate Authority?

Section 95(b) defines the Appellate Authority as the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling referred to in Section 99.

This creates a two-level structure:

Applicant → Authority for Advance Ruling (AAR) → Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling (AAAR)

An applicant or the jurisdictional officer can appeal an advance ruling in accordance with Section 100 and the applicable procedure.

12. Section 95 does not decide the question by itself

A common mistake is to read Section 95 and assume that it itself gives a business the right to obtain a ruling on any GST problem.

It does not.

13. Connection with Section 97

Section 95(a) specifically points to Section 97(2). That subsection is therefore critical when deciding whether a proposed advance-ruling question is actually permitted.

The permitted categories include matters such as classification, applicability of notifications affecting tax rate, principles for valuation, admissibility of ITC, determination of liability to pay tax, requirement to register and whether an activity amounts to a supply.

Therefore, a good application should be framed around a precise statutory question rather than a broad request such as “please tell us whether our GST treatment is correct”.

14. Connection with Section 100

Section 95(a) also refers to Section 100(1), which deals with an appeal against the advance ruling.

This is why “advance ruling” in the definition includes a decision of the Authority or the Appellate Authority.

In simple terms:

AAR stage
Original advance ruling.
AAAR stage
Appellate decision where an appeal is maintainable.

15. Practical meaning for businesses

Section 95 becomes useful when a business is considering a transaction where the GST treatment has significant financial consequences.

Examples include:

✓ Classification of a specialised product
✓ Classification of a complex service
✓ ITC eligibility
✓ Applicability of a rate notification
✓ Valuation methodology
✓ Whether an activity is a supply
✓ Registration requirement
✓ Tax liability on a proposed arrangement

However, the question must fit within Section 97(2), and the application must satisfy the procedural and jurisdictional requirements of Chapter XVII.

16. Construction and infrastructure examples

Works-contract classification

A construction company proposes a specialised arrangement and wants clarity on classification. If the question falls within Section 97(2), an advance-ruling application may be considered.

Infrastructure project valuation

A project company has a complex contractual arrangement and needs to determine the valuation principle. Section 97(2) should be checked before preparing the application.

Government contract

A contractor wants certainty regarding GST treatment of a proposed government project. The applicant should frame the question around the statutory categories rather than asking for a general tax opinion.

ITC on project inputs

A company wants clarity on whether a particular input or service qualifies for ITC. Admissibility of ITC is expressly one of the areas referred to in Section 97(2).

17. Detailed practical cases

Case 1 — Registered company

A registered company proposes a new taxable service. It identifies a question falling within Section 97(2) and can consider an advance-ruling application.

Case 2 — Person planning registration

A business not yet registered but genuinely desirous of obtaining registration may fall within the definition of applicant.

Case 3 — Historical transaction

A taxpayer wants a ruling merely on an old completed transaction. Section 95 should not be read as automatically permitting such an application; the “being undertaken or proposed” language and other Chapter XVII conditions must be considered.

Case 4 — Classification dispute

A manufacturer is unsure about classification of a new product. Classification is one of the statutory question categories.

Case 5 — ITC question

A company wants to know whether ITC on a proposed expenditure is admissible. The Section 97(2) category should be identified precisely.

Case 6 — Rate notification

A supplier wants to know whether a notification affecting the rate applies. The question should be framed with the relevant notification and facts.

Case 7 — Valuation

A business has a related or bundled transaction and wants a ruling on valuation principles. The statutory scope must be checked.

Case 8 — Supply question

A business is uncertain whether a particular activity amounts to a supply. This is expressly relevant to the advance-ruling framework.

Case 9 — Registration question

A person wants clarity on whether registration is required. The statutory question category should be identified.

Case 10 — Broad advisory request

An applicant asks AAR to review all GST implications of its business. A broad request should be narrowed into specific questions falling within Section 97(2).

Case 11 — Recipient/RCM applicant

A person liable under a reverse-charge arrangement should not assume that only the supplier can seek advance ruling. The statutory definition of applicant is broader, subject to the question and facts.

Case 12 — Appeal

If the applicant or jurisdictional officer is aggrieved by the AAR ruling, the Section 100 appeal mechanism should be considered within the prescribed time.

18. Important limitations

✗ Section 95 is not itself a tax exemption.
✗ It does not create a new GST rate.
✗ It does not permit every possible GST question.
✗ Applicant status alone does not guarantee a ruling on the merits.
✗ AAR is not a substitute for a general legal-advisory service.
✗ A broad hypothetical question may create maintainability issues.
✗ Section 95 should be read with Sections 96–106.
✗ The specific question must fit Section 97(2).

21. Common mistakes

  • Thinking Section 95 itself tells you whether a transaction is taxable.
  • Ignoring Section 97(2) while framing the question.
  • Asking a broad question instead of a precise statutory question.
  • Providing incomplete facts or contracts.
  • Applying for a ruling without checking whether the activity is actually being undertaken or proposed.
  • Assuming every unregistered person automatically qualifies.
  • Confusing an advance ruling with a private tax opinion.
  • Ignoring the appeal mechanism under Section 100.
  • Failing to consider the effect of an existing ruling on the same question and facts.
  • Assuming an AAR ruling automatically decides every taxpayer's identical transaction.
  • Not checking the current composition/procedure of the advance-ruling authorities.

22. National Appellate Authority amendment — important distinction

The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2019 inserted provisions referring to a National Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling, including amendments to Section 95 and the insertion of Sections 101A–101C.

However, this should not be presented as though a functioning National Appellate Authority automatically forms part of the ordinary AAR/AAAR process today. The amendment's operative status must be distinguished from the existing Authority and Appellate Authority framework.

For your website: The article therefore does not present the National Appellate Authority as a routine current forum. The existing practical route is AAR → AAAR, subject to the Act and applicable legal developments.

Current position.

24. Section 95 GST FAQs

What is Section 95 of the CGST Act?

Section 95 contains definitions used in Chapter XVII dealing with advance rulings.

What is an advance ruling under GST?

It is a statutory decision by the Authority or Appellate Authority on specified questions concerning a supply being undertaken or proposed to be undertaken by the applicant.

Who is an applicant under Section 95?

Any person registered or desirous of obtaining registration under the CGST Act.

Can an unregistered person apply for advance ruling?

A person desirous of obtaining registration is included in the statutory definition of applicant, subject to the other requirements of Chapter XVII.

What is AAR in GST?

AAR means Authority for Advance Ruling, referred to in Section 96.

What is AAAR in GST?

AAAR means Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling, referred to in Section 99.

What is the difference between Section 95 and Section 97?

Section 95 defines the key terms. Section 97 deals with the application and the questions on which an advance ruling may be sought.

Can I ask AAR any GST question?

No. The question must fall within the statutory categories in Section 97(2), in addition to satisfying other requirements.

Can advance ruling be sought for an ongoing supply?

The definition refers to supplies being undertaken as well as proposed to be undertaken, subject to the other statutory conditions.

Can advance ruling be sought for a completely completed historical transaction?

The wording of Section 95 focuses on a supply being undertaken or proposed to be undertaken by the applicant. A completed historical transaction should therefore not automatically be treated as eligible.

Is an advance ruling the same as a GST opinion from a consultant?

No. An advance ruling is a statutory decision under Chapter XVII. A professional opinion is advisory and is not itself an AAR/AAAR ruling.

Who can appeal an AAR ruling?

The applicant or the concerned/jurisdictional officer can appeal in accordance with Section 100 and the applicable procedure.

What is the National Appellate Authority?

It was provided for by the 2019 amendment framework, but its operative status should not be confused with the existing AAR/AAAR mechanism.

Does Section 95 itself decide taxability?

No. It defines the terms. The substantive question must be examined under the relevant provisions, including Section 97(2).

Why is Section 95 important for businesses?

It establishes the vocabulary and entry point for the GST advance-ruling framework, helping businesses understand who can apply and what kind of statutory decision they are seeking.

26. Key takeaways

  • Section 95 is the definitions section of the GST advance-ruling chapter.
  • It defines advance ruling, Appellate Authority, applicant, application and Authority.
  • An applicant can be a registered person or a person desirous of obtaining registration.
  • An advance ruling concerns specified questions under the statutory framework.
  • The supply may be one being undertaken or proposed to be undertaken, subject to the other conditions.
  • Section 95 must be read together with Section 97 because Section 97 identifies the questions that can be asked.
  • AAR is the first-level authority and AAAR is the appellate authority under the ordinary current framework.
  • Section 95 itself does not decide whether a transaction is taxable.
  • The National Appellate Authority amendment should be described carefully because its operative status must be distinguished from the existing AAR/AAAR process.
  • A strong advance-ruling application starts with a precise statutory question and complete factual documentation.
