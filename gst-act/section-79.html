GST ACT — SECTION 79

Section 79 CGST Act — GST Tax Recovery, Bank Account Attachment & Recovery Proceedings

Section 79 is the main recovery provision of the CGST Act. It becomes especially important when an amount payable to the Government remains unpaid and the department moves from the demand/payment stage to actual recovery. This detailed guide explains the recovery machinery in simple language, including recovery from money owed to the taxpayer, sale of goods, third-party recovery, attachment and sale of property, recovery from business assets, demand registers, DRC-07, Section 78, Section 80 and practical controls for finance teams.

1. Why Section 79 matters

Searches such as “Section 79 CGST Act”, “Section 79 GST”, “GST recovery”, “GST tax recovery after demand order”, “GST bank account attachment”, “GST recovery from customer”, “GST property attachment”, “GST recovery proceedings”, “Section 79(1) GST” and “GST recovery after DRC-07” all relate to this provision.

GST law does not stop at determining a tax liability. If an amount legally payable to Government remains unpaid, the law contains a separate machinery to recover it. Section 79 is the central provision for that stage.

This distinction is important. Sections dealing with assessment or determination establish the liability. Section 78 provides the ordinary payment window after an order. Section 79 provides the recovery tools when an amount is recoverable and remains unpaid.

Simple idea: Section 79 is the Government's statutory recovery toolbox for unpaid GST amounts. It can reach money, goods, receivables, property and other assets through the modes prescribed by law.

2. Section 79 in very simple language

Imagine a company receives a final GST demand of ₹1 crore. The payment period under Section 78 expires and the recoverable amount remains unpaid.

The Government is not limited to repeatedly asking the company to pay. Section 79 provides legal methods through which the amount can be recovered.

For example, depending on the circumstances and applicable Rules, recovery may involve:

  • money payable to the taxpayer being intercepted;
  • goods belonging to the taxpayer being detained and sold;
  • a person who owes money to the taxpayer being directed to pay the Government instead;
  • movable or immovable property being attached and sold; or
  • other prescribed recovery mechanisms.
Demand → Payment period → Unpaid recoverable amount → Section 79 recovery machinery

3. When GST recovery starts

Section 79 applies where an amount payable by a person to Government under the Act or Rules is not paid.

The phrase is broad enough to cover amounts arising under different provisions, subject to their statutory conditions.

A typical demand lifecycle is:

1. Liability identified
Through return, self-assessment, scrutiny, audit, investigation or adjudication.
2. Amount becomes payable
Under the relevant statutory mechanism/order.
3. Section 78 period
Taxpayer receives the applicable payment window after order.
4. Amount remains unpaid
Recovery exposure arises.
5. Section 79
Proper officer proceeds through one or more permitted modes.
6. Recovery/relief
Demand is paid, recovered, stayed, reduced or otherwise legally disposed.

There are also important situations where the law permits recovery of certain self-assessed amounts through Section 79 without waiting for a conventional adjudication order. Section 75(12), including its explanation, is particularly important in this context.

4. Section 79 statutory structure

Section 79(1) contains the principal recovery modes. The provision allows the proper officer to proceed by one or more specified methods when an amount payable remains unpaid.

Recovery routePlain-language meaning
Money owing to taxpayerGovernment can recover from money belonging to the taxpayer but held under the control of the proper officer or another specified officer.
GoodsGoods belonging to the taxpayer can be detained and sold according to the statutory procedure.
Third-party moneyA person who owes money to the taxpayer can be required to pay Government instead, subject to the statutory conditions.
Movable/immovable propertyProperty can be attached and sold through the prescribed recovery procedure.
Specified officer/authorityRecovery can be carried out through another officer or authority in the manner provided by the Act/Rules.

The detailed operational procedure is supplemented by the GST Rules, particularly the recovery provisions in the relevant part of Chapter XV.

5. Recovery modes under Section 79

Section 79 is designed as a menu of recovery mechanisms rather than a single compulsory sequence.

The proper officer may use one or more modes. The appropriate route depends on the facts, the assets available, the amount recoverable and the applicable Rules.

Cash-flow route
Recover money already payable to the taxpayer.
Inventory route
Detain and sell goods belonging to the taxpayer.
Receivable route
Approach customers/debtors or other persons owing money to the taxpayer.
Asset route
Attach and sell movable or immovable property through prescribed procedure.

For a company, this means a recovery action can directly affect working capital, receivables, inventory and fixed assets.

6. Recovery from money owed to the taxpayer

One recovery route allows the proper officer to deduct, or require another specified officer to deduct, the recoverable amount from money owing to the taxpayer that is under the control of the relevant officer.

The practical principle is straightforward: if Government already controls money that belongs to the defaulter, the law can use that money to satisfy the Government liability.

Example

A contractor has a recoverable GST demand of ₹20 lakh and ₹25 lakh is otherwise payable to the contractor under a Government-controlled payment mechanism. Subject to the statutory conditions and applicable procedure, recovery may be made from money owing to the contractor.

7. Detention and sale of goods

Section 79 also permits recovery by detaining and selling goods belonging to the taxpayer that are under the control of the proper officer or another specified officer.

This is different from routine inspection or seizure. The purpose here is recovery of an already recoverable amount.

The taxpayer should therefore distinguish:

  • inspection/search powers;
  • seizure/detention under other provisions; and
  • recovery by sale of goods under Section 79.
A business should not assume that every detention of goods is a Section 79 recovery. The legal provision and order authorising the action should be identified.

8. Recovery from third parties

One of the most commercially important recovery mechanisms is recovery from a third party who owes money to the taxpayer.

In simple terms:

Customer owes taxpayer → Government has recoverable demand → Customer may be required to pay Government instead, subject to Section 79 procedure

This can have a major impact on a business because a recovery action can reach its receivables even though the money has not yet reached the taxpayer's bank account.

Finance implication

The accounts receivable team should immediately identify whether any GST recovery communication has been received by major customers, project owners, government departments, lenders or other parties who owe money to the taxpayer.

9. Attachment and sale of property

Where other recovery routes are insufficient or where the statutory procedure permits, movable or immovable property can be attached and sold for recovery.

This is particularly significant for businesses holding:

  • land;
  • buildings;
  • plant and machinery;
  • vehicles;
  • large equipment;
  • other saleable assets.

The applicable Rules prescribe the procedural steps, notices, valuation/sale mechanism and treatment of proceeds.

Practical message: Once recovery reaches asset attachment/sale stage, the issue should be treated as a serious treasury, legal and board-level matter.

10. Recovery through specified officers

Section 79 allows the proper officer, in specified situations, to require another specified officer to carry out the recovery action.

This enables the GST administration to coordinate recovery where the money, goods or property is under the control of another government authority or officer.

For taxpayers, the practical lesson is that recovery should not be evaluated only by asking which officer originally passed the demand order. The recovery machinery can involve other officers or authorities in accordance with the law.

11. Recovery from a business

A business facing GST recovery should immediately map its financial exposure.

AreaWhat to check
Bank accountsWhether any statutory attachment/recovery communication has been received.
CustomersWhether third-party recovery notices affect receivables.
InventoryWhether goods are subject to any recovery action.
Fixed assetsWhether any attachment has been made.
Government contractsWhether amounts payable by government bodies could be affected.
AppealWhether the demand is under challenge and whether recovery protection exists.
Cash flowWhether payment or instalment arrangements are feasible.

12. Joint and several liability

Certain GST provisions create liability involving multiple persons, such as specific situations involving transfer of business or persons responsible for a taxable entity. Recovery teams must therefore identify the exact legal basis of the liability before assuming that only one entity is exposed.

Section 79 is the recovery mechanism; the underlying provision determines who is legally liable.

Do not use Section 79 in isolation to decide liability. First identify the substantive provision creating the liability; then determine the applicable recovery route.

13. Transfer of business and recovery

Where a business is transferred, GST law contains specific provisions concerning liabilities connected with the business. A buyer or transferee should therefore conduct GST due diligence before acquisition.

For an acquisition, finance and legal teams should review:

  • open GST notices;
  • assessment orders;
  • appeals;
  • outstanding demands;
  • electronic liability registers;
  • recovery communications;
  • tax periods under investigation; and
  • indemnity provisions in the transaction documents.

14. Representative persons

GST law contains separate provisions dealing with representative persons and liabilities of certain persons connected with a taxable person. Where such provisions apply, the recovery analysis must consider the precise statutory liability before recovery is initiated.

Section 79 should therefore be viewed as the enforcement mechanism, not the provision that independently creates every underlying liability.

15. Company liquidation and recovery

Where a company is under liquidation or insolvency-related proceedings, GST recovery has to be considered together with the applicable insolvency and corporate laws.

A finance team should not assume that Section 79 operates independently of every other statutory process. Priority, moratorium, claims and recovery rights can depend on the applicable insolvency/court framework and the facts.

For an actual insolvency case, the GST demand should be reconciled with the claims filed before the appropriate authority and the applicable legal orders.

16. Death and recovery

Where a taxable person dies, GST law contains specific provisions concerning the business carried on by legal representatives. The recovery position therefore depends on the applicable liability provision and the status of the business.

The important practical point is that death does not automatically erase an existing statutory tax liability. The applicable succession/legal-representative provisions must be examined before determining recovery.

17. Section 78 vs Section 79

Section 78Section 79
Primarily provides the payment period after an order.Provides recovery machinery for amounts that remain recoverable and unpaid.
Normal period is three months from service, subject to the statutory proviso.Allows one or more specified recovery modes.
Focus is payment opportunity.Focus is enforcement/recovery.

Easy way to remember: Section 78 asks, “How long do I get to pay?” Section 79 asks, “What can Government do to recover if the amount remains unpaid?”

18. Appeal and stay

Recovery analysis must always be done alongside the appeal status.

When a demand is challenged, record:

✓ Order date
✓ Service date
✓ Appeal deadline
✓ Appeal filed date
✓ Pre-deposit
✓ Stay status
✓ Appellate order
✓ Revised demand

Filing an appeal and obtaining protection against recovery are related but distinct concepts. The taxpayer should verify the actual legal effect of the appeal and any stay/pre-deposit provisions rather than assuming that recovery automatically stops.

19. Section 80 instalments

Section 80 provides a separate mechanism through which the Commissioner may, subject to prescribed conditions, allow payment of tax or other amounts due in instalments or grant additional time.

Rule 158 prescribes the procedural framework, including FORM GST DRC-20 for an application and FORM GST DRC-21 for an order allowing payment in instalments or additional time.

This is especially relevant for businesses that have a genuine liquidity problem but want to remain compliant rather than allowing recovery to escalate.

Do not wait until attachment: if instalment relief is legally available, examine it early and document the cash-flow reasons and proposed payment schedule.

20. DRC-07 and recovery notice

Rule 142 provides the electronic demand documentation framework. A summary of specified orders is uploaded in FORM GST DRC-07, specifying tax, interest and penalty payable. The Rules provide that the order referred to in the relevant sub-rule is treated as the notice for recovery. citeturn0search4

Therefore, DRC-07 is a major practical document in recovery management.

However, the finance team should always retain the underlying adjudication order because the DRC-07 summary should not be treated as a substitute for understanding the legal reasoning, tax period and components of the demand.

21. PMT-01 liability register

The electronic tax liability register is maintained in FORM GST PMT-01. CBIC's payment rules state that amounts payable under returns, orders and other statutory mechanisms are debited to the liability register. They also provide that demand is reduced to the extent of relief granted by an appellate authority, Appellate Tribunal or court. citeturn0search0

This makes PMT-01 reconciliation essential for a company with multiple GST disputes.

22. Bank account recovery

Bank-related recovery can be commercially disruptive because it can affect the company's ability to pay employees, vendors, lenders and project expenses.

A finance team should therefore have a protocol for immediately escalating any GST recovery communication received by a bank.

Do not confuse:

  • a normal GST payment made voluntarily by the taxpayer;
  • a bank attachment/recovery action by the department; and
  • a statutory payment made pursuant to an instalment or other authorised arrangement.

Each should be separately recorded and reconciled.

23. Customer/third-party receivable recovery

Third-party recovery can be particularly serious for contractors and infrastructure companies.

Suppose:

  • Company A has a GST demand of ₹50 lakh;
  • Company B owes Company A ₹70 lakh under a contract; and
  • a statutory recovery communication is issued to Company B.

The recovery can affect the cash flow expected by Company A. Therefore, GST demand monitoring should not be restricted to bank accounts.

Construction-sector control: Maintain a list of major receivables, especially government departments, PSUs and project owners, when a material GST demand is outstanding.

24. Property recovery process

Property recovery involves significantly greater procedural consequences than ordinary voluntary payment.

Finance/legal teams should identify:

  • what property is covered;
  • whether the property belongs to the taxpayer;
  • the attachment communication;
  • the applicable valuation/sale process;
  • any court or appellate protection;
  • secured lender interests;
  • co-owner issues; and
  • the amount actually recoverable.

The detailed procedure is governed by Section 79 and the relevant Rules. For a live attachment, the exact recovery notice and applicable Rules should be reviewed rather than relying only on a general summary.

25. Priority and other proceedings

Businesses can have multiple statutory liabilities at the same time: GST, income tax, customs, bank dues, employee dues and other statutory claims.

Section 79 provides GST recovery powers, but the practical priority of claims can depend on the nature of the asset, other statutory charges, court orders, insolvency proceedings and applicable law.

Therefore, a company should not assume that an asset is available for GST recovery simply because it appears in the fixed-asset register. Ownership, charge, security interest and other legal factors can matter.

26. Self-assessed tax under Section 75(12)

A very important recovery situation arises from Section 75(12).

Where an amount of self-assessed tax in a return under Section 39 remains unpaid, or interest payable on that tax remains unpaid, the law provides for recovery under Section 79 notwithstanding the ordinary determination provisions.

The explanation added by the Finance Act, 2021 clarified that “self-assessed tax” includes tax payable on outward supplies whose details have been furnished under Section 37 but which have not been included in the return under Section 39.

CBIC Instruction No. 01/2022-GST specifically addressed recovery under Section 79 for cases covered by this explanation. citeturn0search3

Practical lesson: A company should reconcile GSTR-1 outward tax with GSTR-3B tax payment. A mismatch can create a recovery issue even before a conventional adjudication demand is completed.

27. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Unpaid demand after Section 78

A company receives a ₹30 lakh demand. The Section 78 payment period expires without payment or effective recovery protection. Section 79 recovery can become relevant.

Example 2 — Customer owes contractor

A contractor has ₹1 crore receivable from a project owner and an outstanding GST demand. A third-party recovery mechanism can potentially affect that receivable.

Example 3 — Goods held by department

Goods belonging to the taxpayer are already under the control of the proper officer. The law provides a route for detention and sale for recovery, subject to the prescribed process.

Example 4 — Fixed asset exposure

A manufacturing company has a large unpaid GST demand and substantial machinery. The finance team should not wait for an attachment before escalating the issue to legal and treasury management.

Example 5 — Appeal filed

The company files an appeal and makes the required pre-deposit. It must separately determine the remaining recovery exposure and whether effective protection exists.

Example 6 — Instalment request

A business has a confirmed demand but temporary cash-flow constraints. It examines Section 80 and Rule 158 before the recovery position becomes critical.

Example 7 — DRC-07 mismatch

The order says ₹20 lakh but the portal liability appears different. The taxpayer should reconcile the order, DRC-07 and PMT-01 and seek correction through the appropriate mechanism.

Example 8 — Appellate relief

A ₹1 crore demand is reduced to ₹25 lakh. The company should ensure the electronic liability is reduced accordingly and that the recovery tracker reflects the revised balance.

Example 9 — GSTR-1 vs GSTR-3B

A company reports ₹10 lakh output tax in GSTR-1 but pays only ₹7 lakh in GSTR-3B. The unpaid self-assessed amount can create recovery exposure under the Section 75(12)/79 framework.

Example 10 — Multiple GSTINs

A group company has an outstanding demand in Telangana but substantial cash in Maharashtra. Each GSTIN is a separate taxable registration, so the recovery and payment position must be analysed GSTIN-wise.

Example 11 — Government receivable

A road contractor has a large receivable from a government department. A GST recovery communication affecting the receivable could materially disrupt project cash flow.

Example 12 — Insolvency situation

A company enters insolvency proceedings while GST recovery is outstanding. The GST team must coordinate the demand with the insolvency process and applicable court/NCLT orders rather than treating it as an ordinary standalone recovery.

Example 13 — Refund and demand

A company has a refund claim and a recoverable demand. It should monitor both separately and account for any statutory adjustment only after the competent authority acts under the refund/recovery framework.

Example 14 — Related-party receivable

A company has an outstanding GST demand and significant receivables from group entities. The recovery analysis should include receivables from related parties where legally relevant.

28. Construction and infrastructure examples

RA bill receivables

A contractor may have significant certified RA bills pending from project owners. A GST recovery action involving third-party receivables can therefore have a direct impact on project liquidity.

Government project receivables

For government contracts, finance teams should maintain a GST-demand risk register alongside the project receivables register.

Mobilisation advance

If a GST demand arises from mobilisation-advance treatment, maintain the advance ledger, invoices, tax payment evidence, contract clauses and reconciliation with RA bills.

Retention money

Where substantial retention is receivable, identify the legal owner of the receivable and monitor any recovery communication affecting the project owner or other debtor.

Equipment and machinery

Construction companies often own high-value cranes, batching plants, vehicles and project equipment. Material GST recovery exposure should therefore be escalated to treasury and legal teams before asset attachment becomes a risk.

Multi-State projects

Each GSTIN's demand should be tracked separately. A central group dashboard should show GSTIN, demand, appeal, stay, payment and recovery status.

32. Common mistakes

  • Waiting until recovery begins before involving treasury and legal teams.
  • Confusing Section 78 payment period with Section 79 recovery.
  • Assuming filing an appeal automatically stops recovery.
  • Failing to reconcile DRC-07 and PMT-01.
  • Ignoring GSTR-1 versus GSTR-3B self-assessed tax differences.
  • Not monitoring major customer receivables when a material demand exists.
  • Not considering Section 80 instalments where legally available.
  • Paying a demand from the wrong GSTIN.
  • Failing to update the demand after appellate relief.
  • Informally netting refund claims against demands.
  • Not preserving bank/third-party recovery communications.
  • Treating every attachment as if it were the same statutory recovery mechanism.

34. Section 79 GST FAQs

What is Section 79 of the CGST Act?

Section 79 provides the statutory machinery for recovery of amounts payable to Government that remain unpaid.

When does Section 79 apply?

It applies where an amount payable under the Act or Rules remains unpaid, subject to the applicable statutory conditions.

What is the difference between Section 78 and Section 79?

Section 78 provides the payment period after an order; Section 79 provides the recovery mechanisms if a recoverable amount remains unpaid.

Can GST be recovered from a customer's payment?

Section 79 contains a mechanism under which a person owing money to the taxpayer can be required to pay the Government instead, subject to the statutory conditions and procedure.

Can GST department attach bank accounts?

Recovery provisions can affect money and assets through prescribed mechanisms. For an actual bank attachment, the exact notice, legal authority and applicable recovery rules should be reviewed.

Can GST department attach property?

Section 79 provides recovery through attachment and sale of property subject to the prescribed statutory procedure.

Can GST department recover by selling goods?

Yes, Section 79 includes detention and sale of goods belonging to the taxpayer under the conditions and procedure prescribed by law.

Does Section 79 require a demand order?

Not every recovery situation should be analysed solely through a conventional demand order. Section 75(12), for example, provides a specific route for certain unpaid self-assessed tax and interest. The exact statutory basis must be identified.

What is DRC-07?

DRC-07 is the electronic summary of specified GST orders showing tax, interest and penalty payable. The Rules provide that the relevant order is treated as the notice for recovery.

What is PMT-01?

PMT-01 is the electronic tax liability register where GST liabilities and demand-related amounts are recorded under the payment Rules.

Can I appeal against a GST demand?

Where the Act provides an appellate remedy, the taxpayer can challenge the order subject to the applicable limitation, pre-deposit and procedural requirements.

Does filing appeal stop Section 79 recovery?

The taxpayer should not assume this automatically. The actual stay/pre-deposit and applicable statutory framework should be checked.

Can GST demand be paid in instalments?

Section 80 and Rule 158 provide an instalment/additional-time mechanism subject to conditions.

What is Section 75(12) and Section 79?

Section 75(12) provides that specified unpaid self-assessed tax and interest are recovered under Section 79. The 2021 explanation also covers tax on outward supplies reported under Section 37 but not included in the Section 39 return.

Can a GST recovery action affect construction-company receivables?

Yes. Because Section 79 includes third-party recovery mechanisms, large project receivables should be monitored where a material GST demand is outstanding.

Can the department recover GST from fixed assets?

Section 79 contains attachment and sale mechanisms for property subject to the prescribed process.

What should a company do after receiving a GST recovery notice?

Immediately identify the demand, service date, legal basis, amount, appeal/stay position, affected bank/receivables/assets and available payment or instalment options.

36. Key takeaways

  • Section 79 is the core GST recovery provision.
  • It applies when an amount payable to Government remains unpaid, subject to the statutory conditions.
  • Recovery can use more than one statutory mode.
  • Money owed to the taxpayer can be targeted through the prescribed third-party recovery mechanism.
  • Goods can be detained and sold through the prescribed recovery process.
  • Movable and immovable property can be subject to attachment and sale under the statutory framework.
  • Section 78 and Section 79 should always be read together: payment window first, recovery machinery after non-payment.
  • Section 75(12) is particularly important for unpaid self-assessed tax and interest.
  • DRC-07 and PMT-01 are essential documents for demand/recovery reconciliation.
  • Appeal filing and recovery protection are not concepts that should be automatically treated as identical.
  • Section 80 may provide an instalment/additional-time route subject to conditions.
  • Construction and infrastructure businesses should monitor project receivables, government dues, inventory and high-value assets when material GST demands are outstanding.
