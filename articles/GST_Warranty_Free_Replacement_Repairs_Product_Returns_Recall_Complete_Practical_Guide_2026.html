1. Quick decision framework: what actually happened?

A product is returned, repaired or replaced, but the GST result depends on the original contract, who owns the goods, whether the warranty was included in the original price, whether a separate amount is charged, and whether the event changes the original taxable value. “Free replacement” does not automatically mean “no GST issue”; nor does every replacement require a fresh taxable invoice.

Step 1 — identify the original promise

Was warranty included in the sale price, separately charged, sold by a dealer, or provided under a third-party service contract?

Step 2 — identify the event

Repair, replacement, return, refund, recall, exchange, upgrade, goodwill gesture or paid out-of-warranty service?

Step 3 — identify the movement

Who owns the old product, replaced product, defective part, scrap and goods in transit?

Step 4 — map GST records

Original invoice, credit/debit note, delivery challan, service invoice, warranty claim and GSTR-1/3B treatment.

Core principle: analyse the original supply and the later warranty/return event together, but do not assume every later event automatically changes the original invoice. Apply the specific provisions on supply, valuation, credit notes, invoice requirements and ITC to the facts.

4. Warranty included in the original sale price

Where a manufacturer sells a product with a standard warranty included in the agreed price, the customer normally pays one commercial price for the product and the promised warranty coverage. Later repair or replacement may be the manufacturer’s performance of that existing obligation rather than a new customer purchase. That conclusion must be supported by the contract and actual conduct.

Specific CBIC clarification: Circulars 195/07/2023-GST and 216/10/2024-GST

These circulars are especially important for warranty teams and should be considered before designing a replacement/ITC process.

Scenario clarifiedPractical treatment stated in the circularsControl for finance
OEM replaces parts or repairs goods for customer during warranty, with no additional chargeNo further GST is payable on the warranty replacement/repair because the original goods value included the likely warranty cost; the OEM is not required to reverse ITC on the replacement parts/repair service merely for this reason.Keep original invoice, warranty policy, claim and parts/service records.
Entire product replaced under warrantyCircular 216/10/2024 extends the earlier clarification to replacement of the goods as a whole, not only parts.Link old and replacement serial numbers and record return/dispatch.
Distributor replaces parts from own stock and invoices OEM for the partsWhere the distributor makes a supply to the manufacturer and invoices consideration, GST applies to that supply; OEM ITC is subject to normal conditions. The distributor need not reverse ITC merely because the part was used in warranty replacement.Match distributor tax invoice, claim approval and OEM ITC.
OEM supplies parts to distributor for warranty replacement at no separate chargeNo GST on that no-charge replenishment and no ITC reversal by OEM on those parts, subject to the facts covered by the clarification.Use a delivery challan and link the replenishment to the approved warranty claim.
Distributor provides repair service to OEM and charges OEMThe distributor supplies repair service to the manufacturer; GST applies on that service, and OEM ITC is subject to normal conditions.Raise a service invoice and preserve job cards and service evidence.
Extended warranty sold by same supplier at original saleThe consideration for extended warranty forms part of composite supply with goods as principal supply under the circular's stated circumstances.Verify that the supplier of the goods and extended warranty is the same person.
Extended warranty sold by a different supplier at original sale, or sold after original saleThe extended warranty is treated as a separate supply of services under Circular 216/10/2024-GST.Identify actual supplier, contract date, classification, rate and tax point.
Do not miss the distinction: no GST on a qualifying no-charge replacement to the customer does not mean every related transaction in the supply chain is non-taxable. A distributor's taxable service to the OEM, a charge collected from the customer, or a separate extended-warranty contract can have its own GST result.

Finance-team test

  1. Check whether the warranty was promised in the original quotation, invoice, product terms or sales agreement.
  2. Confirm whether the customer paid any additional amount for warranty coverage.
  3. Identify whether the warranty is provided by the same supplier or another entity.
  4. Trace whether a replacement is a new sale, a swap under warranty, or a return followed by a new order.
  5. Keep a claim reference linking the original invoice, serial number, defect report, replacement and inventory movement.
Practical position: do not raise a second customer invoice merely because a replacement product leaves the warehouse if it is genuinely supplied to fulfil the original warranty obligation and the documentation reflects that arrangement. Equally, do not assume that every “free” dispatch is covered by the original warranty: goodwill gifts, upgrades and unrelated supplies need separate review.

Example

A manufacturer sells a pump for ₹1,00,000 plus GST and promises a 2-year warranty. After 8 months, the pump fails and the manufacturer replaces it without collecting money. If the replacement is strictly under the original warranty, the file should link the original sale, warranty terms, defect report, old unit return/disposal and replacement dispatch. The replacement is not automatically a fresh sale for a second ₹1,00,000 merely because a new serial number is issued.

6. Repairs and service centres: labour, parts and reimbursements

Repairs may be performed by the manufacturer, an authorised dealer, an independent service centre or the customer’s own maintenance team. The GST treatment depends on who supplies the service, who is liable to pay, whether the customer is charged, and whether parts are supplied as part of the repair contract or separately.

Common models

ModelQuestions to resolve
Manufacturer repairs its own productIs it fulfilling the original warranty? Are parts/labour charged to the customer or reimbursed by another party?
Authorised service centre repairs for customerDoes the service centre invoice the customer, manufacturer or dealer? Is reimbursement a service fee under the agreement?
Paid out-of-warranty repairIdentify repair service, parts supplied, applicable classification/rate, invoice date and ITC eligibility.
Customer sends own goods for repairUse appropriate movement documentation; physical movement alone is not necessarily a sale of goods.
Manufacturer sends parts to repair centreEstablish ownership, consumption, unused parts returned, scrap ownership and stock reconciliation.

Illustration: an authorised service centre incurs ₹3,000 of parts and ₹1,500 labour on a covered repair. If the manufacturer reimburses the service centre ₹4,500 under a service arrangement, determine from the contract whether the reimbursement is consideration for a repair service supplied to the manufacturer. If so, the service centre should apply the appropriate invoice and tax treatment; “reimbursement” in the narration does not by itself make the amount non-taxable.

7. Manufacturer, dealer and third-party warranty arrangements

Warranty chains can involve three or four parties: customer, selling dealer, manufacturer, and an independent warranty administrator or insurer. The invoice chain and contract chain may differ. Map the actual obligations before posting GST.

Customer purchase→Dealer→Manufacturer→Service centre
  • Who promised the warranty to the customer?
  • Who approves the claim and decides whether to repair or replace?
  • Who owns the replacement part before installation?
  • Who pays the service centre, and what exactly does it receive in return?
  • Does the dealer act as principal or agent in the warranty process?
  • Is a third-party extended-warranty plan a separate service supplied for a separate price?
Risk: a company may book a dealer claim as a “warranty expense” and pay it without checking whether the dealer has supplied a taxable service. Conversely, it may demand an invoice for a pure contractual settlement without first identifying the legal nature of the payment. Contract review is essential.

8. Credit notes, debit notes and original invoice corrections

Section 34 must be considered where the original taxable value or tax charged needs adjustment due to returns, deficiencies or other qualifying circumstances. The GST effect of a credit note depends on the statutory conditions, reporting requirements and applicable time limit. A commercial credit note used for settlement may not necessarily reduce the supplier’s output tax.

DocumentUse carefully whenControl
GST credit noteOriginal supply’s taxable value/tax is being reduced under Section 34.Link original invoice, reason, recipient ITC impact, reporting and applicable statutory deadline.
Commercial credit noteCommercial settlement is made without reducing GST liability, or statutory conditions for GST reduction are not met.Clearly state commercial purpose and assess whether consideration/value is actually changing.
Debit noteAdditional taxable value/tax is required for an earlier supply.Link original invoice and disclose in the appropriate return period.
Replacement challan / movement documentGoods move under warranty or repair without representing a new sale.Describe reason, original invoice/claim reference, goods, quantity, serial numbers and destination.

Do not issue a GST credit note merely to clear a warranty expense or dealer claim. First decide whether the original sale price/tax needs statutory adjustment or whether the later payment is a separate service/contract settlement.

9. Customer returns and sales returns

A product returned because of damage, wrong specification, commercial rejection or customer cancellation may require a different process from a warranty replacement. Goods may return to saleable inventory, be repaired, be reworked, be scrapped or be returned to a supplier.

Return workflow

  1. Obtain a return request with original invoice, item/serial number, quantity and reason.
  2. Approve the return under the sales contract and record the date goods physically return.
  3. Inspect the goods and record whether they are saleable, repairable, scrap or under dispute.
  4. Decide whether a GST credit note is permitted under Section 34 or only a commercial adjustment is appropriate.
  5. Reconcile customer ledger, stock register, GSTR-1, output tax and the customer’s ITC where relevant.
  6. If replacement is supplied, link it to the return case and decide whether it is a new sale or warranty fulfilment.

Example: a distributor returns 20 unopened units worth ₹2,00,000 plus GST. If the seller accepts the return and statutory credit-note conditions are met, issue and report the appropriate credit note and update stock. If the parties only agree to a commercial rebate while the original goods remain with the buyer, do not treat that as a physical sales return without evidence.

10. Product recall, safety campaigns and defective batches

A recall can involve retrieving goods from distributors or end customers, replacing units, repairing products, destroying stock or selling recovered goods as scrap. Each flow should be documented separately. A recall notice or public announcement alone does not establish the GST treatment of every transaction in the recall process.

Recall eventPractical GST and control questions
Goods collected from dealersAre goods physically returned to the manufacturer? Which original invoices and batches are involved? Is a credit note issued?
Defective product repaired and returnedWho owns goods during repair? Is the repair under warranty or separately charged? Is movement documented?
Product replacedIs replacement performance of original obligation or a separate supply/settlement? Link old and new serial numbers.
Product destroyedMaintain destruction approval, batch/quantity evidence and review ITC reversal implications under applicable law.
Scrap sold after recallDetermine who owns the scrap and who supplies it; classify, value, invoice and report the sale as applicable.
Dealer compensationIs the payment a price adjustment, reimbursement or consideration for recall/handling services? Read the agreement.

Recall controls should be coordinated across quality, legal, supply chain, finance and GST teams. The inventory record should reconcile quantities recalled, returned, repaired, replaced, destroyed and scrapped.

11. ITC on replacement goods, parts and destroyed stock

Do not assume that ITC on every part or replacement item is automatically available or automatically blocked. Apply Sections 16 and 17 to the recipient, business use, nature of expense and relevant facts. Consider whether the goods are inventory, capital goods, repair parts, goods destroyed, or inputs used to provide a taxable repair service.

Expense / eventReview pointEvidence
Replacement product held as inventoryTrace purchase ITC, stock issue, warranty claim and final disposition.Purchase invoice, stock register, claim file, dispatch/return documents.
Repair parts used in taxable repair serviceTest ordinary Section 16 conditions and any specific Section 17 restriction.Vendor invoice, job card, consumption record, output service invoice.
Goods destroyed in recallExamine Section 17(5)(h) and any other applicable provisions; quantify affected ITC and period.Destruction certificate, quality report, batch records, ITC calculation.
Credit note received from supplierEnsure the accounting and ITC treatment reflect the document and applicable legal requirements.Original invoice, credit note, supplier ledger, GSTR-2B/IMS records as applicable.
Warranty claim paid to service centreCheck whether a taxable service has been supplied and whether invoice/ITC conditions are met.Agreement, claim approval, invoice, proof of service.
Control: use a warranty-specific ITC code and reconcile purchases, replacement issues, returns, destroyed stock, scrap and claim reimbursements. Avoid both duplicate ITC and unsupported blanket reversals.

12. Valuation, reimbursements and warranty claim payments

Section 15 starts with transaction value where its conditions are satisfied. A warranty reimbursement, handling fee, service-centre payment or dealer incentive must be analysed by identifying the supply and contractual consideration. The word “reimbursement” does not decide the tax result.

Questions to ask

  • Who is supplying what to whom?
  • Is payment linked to a distinct service, or is it a price adjustment relating to the original sale?
  • Does the manufacturer pay for actual labour/parts, a fixed claim amount, a handling fee or a performance incentive?
  • Are parties related, and if so, do valuation rules apply?
  • Are third-party expenses incurred as a pure agent, and are all statutory conditions actually met?
  • Does the agreement require a tax invoice, debit note, credit note or a non-tax settlement document?

Worked example: manufacturer reimburses a dealer ₹6,000 for a warranty repair and separately pays ₹500 handling fee. Do not automatically treat the entire ₹6,500 as a non-taxable reimbursement. Examine whether the dealer provides repair/claim administration services to the manufacturer, whether the amounts are consideration and what the agreement says. If a taxable service is supplied, the dealer’s invoice and tax treatment should reflect that supply.

13. Worked examples with solutions

Example 1 — standard warranty replacement

Facts: A company sells a machine for ₹5,00,000 plus GST with a 12-month warranty. At month 5, the machine is defective and replaced at no extra charge under the written warranty.

Solution: Treat the replacement as fulfilment of the documented original warranty obligation if the facts support that conclusion. Link original invoice, warranty clause, inspection report, old machine movement and replacement dispatch. Do not issue a second sale invoice for ₹5,00,000 simply because the replacement is a new unit. Document how the defective unit is returned, repaired or disposed of.

Example 2 — paid extended warranty

Facts: Customer buys an appliance and separately pays ₹8,000 for a 3-year extended warranty.

Solution: Analyse the extended warranty as a separately priced contractual supply. Determine classification, rate, invoicing and time of supply under the contract and applicable law. Do not automatically apply the same invoice logic as a standard warranty included in the product price.

Example 3 — dealer repair reimbursed by manufacturer

Facts: Dealer repairs a product for a customer under manufacturer warranty. The manufacturer reimburses ₹4,000 parts and labour plus ₹300 claim-processing fee.

Solution: Read the dealer agreement to identify the recipient of the repair/administration service. If the dealer supplies a taxable service to the manufacturer, determine the correct classification, invoice and rate. Reconcile the claim to the job card, parts issue and manufacturer approval.

Example 4 — customer returns goods for refund

Facts: Customer returns goods with taxable value ₹1,00,000. Seller accepts the return and refunds the price.

Solution: Confirm the goods physically returned and determine whether the Section 34 credit-note requirements and statutory time limit are satisfied. Issue/report the appropriate credit note where permitted, adjust stock and reconcile the recipient’s ITC position. If statutory conditions are not met, assess whether only a commercial settlement is possible.

Example 5 — goodwill replacement after warranty expiry

Facts: Warranty expired 3 months ago, but the company sends a replacement product free of charge to retain a major customer.

Solution: Do not automatically use the standard warranty process. Record the business reason and approval, establish whether there is any consideration or settlement of an existing obligation, identify the legal basis for dispatch and test the supply/valuation provisions. Document inventory movement and review ITC implications.

Example 6 — recall and destruction

Facts: Manufacturer recalls 500 defective units, destroys 320 and recovers 180 units for refurbishment or scrap.

Solution: Maintain a batch-wise reconciliation: recalled 500 = destroyed 320 + recovered 180. For the destroyed units, review Section 17(5)(h) and quantify any ITC reversal required under applicable law. For recovered units, determine whether they are refurbished and sold, used as spares or sold as scrap; document output tax and valuation as relevant.

Example 7 — replacement with a higher-value model

Facts: Original item worth ₹20,000 is replaced by a model priced at ₹28,000; customer pays ₹8,000.

Solution: Determine whether the agreement treats the transaction as a warranty upgrade with an additional charge or a return plus new sale. The ₹8,000 cannot be classified in isolation; consider the contract, original supply, return of the old unit and valuation of the new transaction. Issue appropriate documents for the chosen legal structure.

Example 8 — unused replacement parts returned to warehouse

Facts: Service centre receives parts for a warranty job but uses only 3 of 5 parts.

Solution: Record 3 parts consumed against the job card and 2 returned to inventory. Do not book all 5 as consumed warranty expense. Link dispatch/transfer documents, service centre acknowledgement, unused-part return and stock ledger.

15. Accounting and ERP controls

Accounting entries should reflect the legal event, not replace legal analysis. Configure separate transaction types for standard warranty cost, warranty provision, replacement inventory, repair parts, service-centre invoices, sales returns, GST credit notes, commercial credits, scrap and recall destruction.

16. Documents and evidence to retain

  • Original tax invoice, sales order, quotation and applicable terms and conditions.
  • Standard warranty policy, extended warranty contract and customer communication.
  • Defect report, inspection report, service job card and technical finding.
  • Customer return authorisation, RMA, delivery challan, e-way bill where applicable and proof of physical movement.
  • Original and replacement serial numbers, batch details and stock ledger.
  • Dealer/service-centre agreement, claim approval, reimbursement calculation and supporting invoice.
  • GST credit/debit note, commercial credit note, return filing evidence and customer ITC communication where relevant.
  • Recall notice, quality report, regulatory correspondence, destruction certificate and scrap sale records.
  • ITC computation for damaged/destroyed goods and documented legal conclusion.
  • Exception report for open claims, parts not returned, unreconciled claims and credit notes not reported.

17. Common mistakes that create GST exposure

  • Issuing a fresh taxable invoice for every warranty replacement without analysing the original obligation.
  • Treating every free dispatch as exempt from GST without checking its legal and commercial basis.
  • Calling all dealer payments “reimbursements” without deciding whether a service is supplied.
  • Issuing a GST credit note when only a commercial credit is intended, or using a commercial credit note to conceal a taxable adjustment.
  • Failing to trace the old product, replacement unit, spare parts and scrap.
  • Ignoring ITC implications for destroyed or written-off goods.
  • Mixing standard warranty, extended warranty, paid repair and goodwill cases in one ERP code.
  • Assuming the accounting provision for warranty is itself the GST tax point.
  • Not reconciling warranty claims paid by the manufacturer with service-centre invoices and job cards.
  • Using an outdated tax rate or notification without checking the date of supply.

18. Monthly warranty GST control checklist

  1. Download all warranty claims opened, approved, rejected and closed during the month.
  2. Reconcile claim IDs to original sales invoices and warranty terms.
  3. Reconcile goods issued, goods returned, repairs completed, replacement dispatches and scrap.
  4. Separate standard warranty from extended warranty, paid repairs and goodwill cases.
  5. Review dealer/service-centre reimbursements for invoice and tax treatment.
  6. Review customer returns and test Section 34 credit-note eligibility/time limit.
  7. Reconcile output tax adjustments with GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B.
  8. Review ITC on parts, destroyed stock, credit notes and related expenses.
  9. Resolve unmatched serial numbers, duplicate claims and negative stock.
  10. Prepare a reviewer sign-off with exceptions, legal conclusion and supporting documents.

19. Frequently asked questions

1. Is GST payable again when a product is replaced free under warranty?

Not automatically. If the replacement is genuinely the fulfilment of an existing warranty obligation included in the original supply, analyse and document it on that basis. A separate sale, upgrade or goodwill transaction may require a different result.

2. Should we issue a tax invoice for every replacement unit?

Do not apply a blanket rule. Determine whether the movement represents a new supply, warranty fulfilment or another transaction. Use the appropriate invoice or movement document and link it to the original sale and claim.

3. Is a separately charged extended warranty taxable?

Apply Circular 216/10/2024-GST: if the same supplier sells the goods and extended warranty together at original supply, the warranty consideration forms part of the composite supply with goods as principal supply. If a different person supplies the warranty, or the warranty is contracted after the original sale, it is treated as a separate supply of services. Confirm the actual supplier, contract and applicable rate.

4. Is a warranty reimbursement to a dealer taxable?

It depends on what the dealer supplies under the agreement. If the payment is consideration for a repair, claim administration or another service, the applicable service tax treatment must be considered. The word “reimbursement” alone does not settle the issue.

5. Can we issue a GST credit note for returned goods?

Only after checking Section 34 requirements, the reason for reduction, reporting and the applicable statutory time limit. A commercial credit note does not automatically reduce GST liability.

6. What if the warranty has expired but we replace the product for goodwill?

Analyse the contractual basis, consideration, business reason and exact transaction. Do not automatically apply the standard-warranty treatment. Keep a documented approval and tax analysis.

7. What happens to ITC on goods destroyed during a product recall?

Review Section 17(5)(h) and any other applicable provisions, identify the ITC attributable to the destroyed goods and retain reliable destruction and inventory evidence.

8. Does every repair involve a separate supply?

Identify the parties and obligations. A paid repair service may be a separate supply; a repair under the original warranty may be performance of the existing obligation. Service-centre arrangements require separate contract analysis.

9. What if the customer pays extra for an upgraded replacement?

Review the original warranty, return of the old product, new model value and additional payment. Decide whether the arrangement is an upgrade under contract or a return plus new sale, and document the invoice/credit-note trail.

10. Is a warranty provision in accounts subject to GST immediately?

Accounting recognition of an estimated provision does not by itself determine the GST tax point. Analyse the actual supply, consideration, invoice and time-of-supply rules separately.

11. Can a manufacturer claim ITC on spare parts used for warranty repairs?

Apply Sections 16 and 17 to the actual use and facts, and retain purchase invoices, job cards, stock consumption and warranty claim records. Do not assume automatic eligibility or blanket disallowance.

12. What should be done first when GST treatment is disputed in an audit?

Reconstruct the transaction using the original contract, invoice, warranty terms, serial-number movement, service records, claim settlement and returns. Then prepare a provision-wise written analysis and reconcile tax/ITC amounts.
