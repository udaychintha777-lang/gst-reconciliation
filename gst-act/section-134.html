Section 134 of the CGST Act, 2017

Cognizance of offences. A practical guide explaining why Commissioner sanction matters before a court takes cognizance of a GST offence and why GST offences can only be tried by a court of the prescribed judicial level or higher.

CGST Act Section 134Cognizance of OffencesGST ProsecutionCommissioner SanctionMagistrate First Class

Section 134 at a Glance

Core subjectWho can take cognizance of an offence under the CGST Act or Rules and which court can try it.
Previous sanctionNo court may take cognizance without the previous sanction of the Commissioner.
Court levelA court inferior to a Magistrate of the First Class cannot try the offence.
In simple words: Section 134 creates two important procedural conditions for GST criminal offences: first, the Commissioner’s previous sanction is required before the court takes cognizance; second, the trial cannot be conducted by a court below the level of a Magistrate of the First Class.

1. Statutory Text of Section 134

134. Cognizance of offences.

No court shall take cognizance of any offence punishable under this Act or the rules made thereunder except with the previous sanction of the Commissioner, and no court inferior to that of a Magistrate of the First Class, shall try any such offence.

Section 134 contains only one sentence, but that sentence performs two separate procedural functions: it controls cognizance and establishes a minimum court level for trial.

2. Section 134 Explained in Simple Language

Suppose a person is alleged to have committed a criminal offence under the CGST Act. The existence of an allegation does not mean that any court can automatically take up the matter and conduct a trial.

Section 134 adds two statutory requirements:

  1. Previous Commissioner sanction: before a court takes cognizance of the offence, the required previous sanction of the Commissioner must exist.
  2. Minimum court level: a court below the level of a Magistrate of the First Class cannot try the offence.
Easy way to remember: Section 134 answers two questions — “Can the court formally take cognizance?” and “Can this level of court conduct the trial?”

This is a procedural provision. It does not itself define the underlying GST offence or decide whether the accused is guilty.

3. What Does “Cognizance” Mean?

“Cognizance” is a legal term and is not the same as simply receiving a complaint or hearing about an allegation.

In broad practical terms, when a competent court takes cognizance, it judicially takes notice of an alleged offence for the purpose of proceeding under the criminal law framework. The exact procedural meaning can depend on the applicable criminal procedure and the circumstances of the case.

Why the distinction matters

EventSimple description
Investigation/enquiryAuthorities collect facts and evidence regarding suspected conduct.
Complaint/prosecution filingThe matter is placed before the court through the applicable legal process.
CognizanceThe court takes judicial notice of the offence in accordance with the applicable procedure.
TrialThe court adjudicates the criminal case according to the prescribed process.
Conviction/acquittalThe criminal court ultimately determines the case according to law and evidence.
Important: Section 134 does not say that Commissioner sanction is proof that an offence occurred. Sanction is a statutory procedural requirement before cognizance.

4. Why Is Previous Commissioner Sanction Required?

Section 134 expressly states that no court shall take cognizance of an offence punishable under the CGST Act or the Rules made under it except with the previous sanction of the Commissioner.

This creates an approval requirement before the judicial stage of cognizance.

What “previous” means in practical terms

The sanction is intended to exist before the court takes cognizance. It should therefore not be treated as a formality that can automatically be assumed to exist merely because an investigation has occurred.

What sanction does not mean

  • It is not a conviction.
  • It is not a finding that every allegation is proved.
  • It does not determine the final sentence.
  • It does not replace the criminal court's role.
  • It does not eliminate the accused person's legal rights and defences.
Practical takeaway: In a GST prosecution file, the chronology should clearly identify the alleged offence, investigation, prosecution decision, Commissioner sanction and court proceedings.

5. Which Court Can Try a GST Offence?

The second part of Section 134 states that no court inferior to that of a Magistrate of the First Class shall try a GST offence covered by the provision.

Therefore, the statutory minimum level is a Magistrate of the First Class. A court below that level cannot conduct the trial of such an offence.

QuestionSection 134 position
Can a court below Magistrate of First Class try the offence?No.
Does Section 134 itself establish guilt?No.
Does Section 134 prescribe the punishment?No. The punishment comes from the relevant offence provision, such as Section 132.
Does Section 134 remove all criminal procedure?No. It sets specific GST-law requirements within the broader criminal procedure framework.

6. Cognizance and Trial Are Two Different Questions

A common misunderstanding is to read the two halves of Section 134 as if they mean the same thing. They do not.

Part of Section 134Question it addressesPractical meaning
“No court shall take cognizance...”Can the court take judicial cognizance of the GST offence?Previous Commissioner sanction is required.
“No court inferior to that of a Magistrate of the First Class, shall try...”Which court has the minimum level of jurisdiction to conduct the trial?A court below Magistrate First Class cannot try the offence.

Keeping these concepts separate makes it easier to understand GST prosecution procedure.

9. Practical Examples

Example 1 – Prosecution complaint without prior sanction

A prosecution complaint is presented alleging a CGST offence, but the required previous Commissioner sanction is absent. Section 134 becomes directly relevant because the section says the court shall not take cognizance except with previous sanction.

Example 2 – Sanction does not equal conviction

The Commissioner grants sanction for prosecution. The accused argues that sanction itself proves guilt. That is incorrect. Sanction is a procedural prerequisite; the criminal court still has to deal with the prosecution according to law.

Example 3 – Wrong court level

A GST offence is placed before a court inferior to a Magistrate of the First Class for trial. Section 134 specifically states that such a court cannot try the offence.

Example 4 – Section 132 and Section 134 together

A person is alleged to have committed an offence under Section 132. Section 132 identifies the offence and punishment, while Section 134 governs the sanction/cognizance and minimum court-level aspects.

Example 5 – Departmental demand versus prosecution

A taxpayer receives a GST demand order. The taxpayer assumes Section 134 automatically applies to every demand proceeding. It does not. Section 134 concerns cognizance of an offence punishable under the Act or Rules; an ordinary tax determination is not automatically a criminal prosecution.

Example 6 – Multiple legal stages

An investigation identifies suspected fraudulent ITC. The department gathers records, considers prosecution, obtains the required sanction and approaches the court. Each stage has a different function; Section 134 specifically governs the court's cognizance and trial-level requirements.

Example 7 – Company prosecution

A company is alleged to have committed a GST offence. Section 137 may determine how company/officer liability is addressed, while Section 134 remains relevant to the court's cognizance and trial framework.

Example 8 – Sanction chronology

A prosecution file records that the court took cognizance first and Commissioner sanction was obtained later. Because Section 134 expressly requires previous sanction, the chronology can become legally important and should be examined carefully by the parties and court.

14. Common Misunderstandings

“Section 134 itself creates a GST offence.”

No. It is a procedural provision concerning cognizance and the minimum level of court that may try an offence.

“Commissioner sanction means the accused is guilty.”

No. Sanction is a statutory prerequisite to cognizance; it is not a conviction.

“Every GST notice requires Commissioner sanction.”

No. Section 134 is concerned with offences punishable under the Act or Rules and the criminal-court cognizance process.

“Any court can try a GST prosecution.”

No. Section 134 states that no court inferior to a Magistrate of the First Class shall try the offence.

“Cognizance and conviction are the same.”

No. Cognizance is a procedural judicial step; conviction is the final determination of guilt after the applicable criminal process.

“Section 134 replaces the Code of Criminal Procedure.”

No. Section 134 establishes specific GST-law requirements. The broader criminal procedural framework continues to be relevant to the extent applicable.

“A departmental tax order automatically means criminal prosecution.”

No. Tax determination and criminal prosecution are distinct legal processes, although the same factual background may sometimes give rise to both.

15. Practical Checklist for a GST Prosecution File

  1. Identify the exact offence provision.
  2. Identify the relevant tax/ITC/refund amount where the offence threshold depends on it.
  3. Preserve the investigation and evidence file.
  4. Confirm whether prosecution is being proposed or has already been initiated.
  5. Verify the date and authority of Commissioner sanction.
  6. Confirm that sanction preceded cognizance as required by Section 134.
  7. Identify the court that took cognizance.
  8. Confirm the trial court meets the statutory minimum level.
  9. Track any Section 137 company/officer liability separately.
  10. Track the evidentiary provisions under Sections 135 and 136 where relevant.
  11. Track compounding under Section 138 where legally available.
  12. Maintain a complete chronology of investigation, sanction, complaint, cognizance and court proceedings.

17. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 134 of the CGST Act?

It deals with cognizance of offences under the CGST Act or Rules and provides that a court cannot take cognizance without previous Commissioner sanction. It also says that a court below a Magistrate of the First Class cannot try the offence.

2. What does “cognizance” mean?

Broadly, it refers to the court taking judicial notice of an alleged offence so that the criminal proceeding can move forward according to law.

3. Is previous Commissioner sanction mandatory?

Section 134 expressly states that no court shall take cognizance of an offence punishable under the Act or Rules except with the previous sanction of the Commissioner.

4. Does Commissioner sanction prove guilt?

No. It is a procedural requirement and is not a conviction or judicial finding of guilt.

5. Which court can try a GST offence?

Section 134 states that no court inferior to that of a Magistrate of the First Class shall try such an offence.

6. Does Section 134 prescribe imprisonment?

No. Punishment is prescribed by the relevant offence provision, such as Section 132. Section 134 deals with cognizance and court level.

7. Does Section 134 apply to every GST tax demand?

No. It concerns offences punishable under the CGST Act or Rules and the criminal-court process for those offences.

8. How is Section 134 connected with Section 132?

Section 132 contains specified GST offences and punishments. Section 134 provides procedural requirements concerning cognizance and the minimum level of court that can try such offences.

9. What is the difference between sanction and cognizance?

Sanction is the prior approval required from the Commissioner. Cognizance is the court's judicial taking of notice of the offence under the applicable procedure.

10. What is the difference between cognizance and trial?

Cognizance is a procedural judicial step. Trial is the subsequent adjudicatory process in which the criminal case is heard and decided.

11. Does the court need Commissioner sanction for a prosecution under the CGST Rules?

Section 134 refers to offences punishable under the Act or the rules made thereunder and requires previous Commissioner sanction before cognizance.

12. Does Section 134 mean GST prosecution cannot be filed in any other court?

The section specifically prohibits a court inferior to a Magistrate of the First Class from trying the offence. The exact procedural forum should be verified under the applicable criminal procedure and jurisdiction.

13. How does Section 137 affect a company prosecution?

Section 137 addresses offences by companies and responsible persons. Section 134 separately addresses the cognizance and minimum trial court framework.

14. What should a company verify if prosecution has been initiated?

Verify the alleged offence, evidence, amount, Commissioner sanction, complaint chronology, cognizance order, court level and the persons proceeded against.

15. Can a tax demand and criminal prosecution arise from the same facts?

Different legal proceedings can arise from the same factual background where the respective statutory requirements are satisfied. Section 131 also makes clear that certain GST penalties or confiscation do not automatically prevent other punishment.

18. Quick Revision – Section 134

  • Section 134 = Cognizance of offences.
  • No court shall take cognizance of a GST offence under the Act or Rules without previous sanction of the Commissioner.
  • The sanction must be previous to cognizance.
  • Sanction is not a conviction and does not itself establish guilt.
  • No court inferior to a Magistrate of the First Class shall try the offence.
  • Section 134 is procedural; it does not itself prescribe the underlying punishment.
