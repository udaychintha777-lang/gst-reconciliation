Section 129 of the CGST Act, 2017 – Detention, Seizure and Release of Goods and Conveyances in Transit

A detailed, practical guide to GST detention of goods and vehicles in transit: when Section 129 applies, how tax and penalty are calculated, owner versus transporter consequences, notice and order timelines, security for release, sale of detained goods, e-way bill issues and the difference between Section 129 and confiscation under Section 130.

At a glance

What is Section 129?

In simple language: Section 129 is the GST provision dealing with detention or seizure of goods and the conveyance carrying them when goods are transported or stored while in transit in contravention of the CGST Act or Rules. It provides a statutory route for release on payment of the applicable amount or by furnishing prescribed security.

For businesses, this section is particularly important in road transportation, e-way bill compliance, invoice/document verification, movement of stock between branches, job-work movements, project-site movements, purchases and sales involving third-party transporters, and other situations where GST officers intercept a consignment.

The current Section 129 is materially different from the original 2017 version. Amendments effective from 1 January 2022 changed the payment formula, inserted the seven-day notice/order framework and changed the post-order recovery mechanism. Therefore, old articles that still describe Section 129 using the earlier 100% tax / 14-day / automatic Section 130 framework should not be treated as the current law.

Very important: Section 129 is not simply “e-way bill penalty.” It is a statutory detention/seizure and release mechanism that can apply when goods are transported or stored while in transit in contravention of the Act or Rules. The exact allegation and facts matter.

Law explained

1. Current statutory text of Section 129

Section 129 – Detention, seizure and release of goods and conveyances in transit.

(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where any person transports any goods or stores any goods while they are in transit in contravention of the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder, all such goods and conveyance used as a means of transport for carrying the said goods and documents relating to such goods and conveyance shall be liable to detention or seizure and after detention or seizure, shall be released—

(a) on payment of the applicable tax and penalty equal to two hundred per cent. of the tax payable on such goods and, in case of exempted goods, on payment of an amount equal to two per cent. of the value of goods or twenty-five thousand rupees, whichever is less, where the owner of the goods comes forward for payment of such tax and penalty;

(b) on payment of the applicable tax and penalty equal to the fifty per cent. of the value of the goods or two hundred per cent. of the tax payable on such goods, whichever is higher, and in case of exempted goods, on payment of an amount equal to five per cent. of the value of goods or twenty-five thousand rupees, whichever is less, where the owner of the goods does not come forward for payment of such tax and penalty;

(c) upon furnishing a security equivalent to the amount payable under clause (a) or clause (b) in such form and manner as may be prescribed.

Provided that no such goods or conveyance shall be detained or seized without serving an order of detention or seizure on the person transporting the goods.

(3) The proper officer detaining or seizing goods or conveyance shall issue a notice within seven days of such detention or seizure, specifying the penalty payable, and thereafter, pass an order within a period of seven days from the date of service of such notice, for payment of penalty under clause (a) or clause (b) of sub-section (1).

(4) No penalty shall be determined under sub-section (3) without giving the person concerned an opportunity of being heard.

(5) On payment of the amount referred to in sub-section (1), all proceedings in respect of the notice specified in sub-section (3) shall be deemed to be concluded.

(6) Where the person transporting any goods or the owner of such goods fails to pay the amount of penalty under sub-section (1) within fifteen days from the date of receipt of the copy of the order passed under sub-section (3), the goods or conveyance so detained or seized shall be liable to be sold or disposed of otherwise, in such manner and within such time as may be prescribed, to recover the penalty payable under sub-section (3).

Provided that the conveyance shall be released on payment by the transporter of penalty under sub-section (3) or one lakh rupees, whichever is less.

Provided further that where the detained or seized goods are perishable or hazardous in nature or are likely to depreciate in value with passage of time, the said period of fifteen days may be reduced by the proper officer.

The current text and amendment history are available on CBIC's GST Acts portal.

2. Section 129 explained in very simple language

Imagine a truck is carrying goods from Hyderabad to Bengaluru. During transit, a GST officer checks the consignment and finds a contravention of GST law or the GST Rules. Section 129 provides a mechanism under which the goods and the vehicle used to carry them can be detained or seized and then released through the statutory payment/security route.

The section is therefore about control of goods in transit. It is not limited to one particular type of paperwork mistake. The underlying question is whether the movement or storage of goods while they are in transit is in contravention of the Act or Rules.

Step 1 – Movement

Goods are being transported or are stored while in transit.

Step 2 – Contravention

The officer identifies a contravention of the GST Act or Rules.

Step 3 – Detention/seizure

The goods, conveyance and relevant documents can be subject to detention or seizure as provided by Section 129.

Step 4 – Release

Release occurs through payment under clause (a)/(b), or prescribed security under clause (c), subject to the statutory process.

3. When can Section 129 apply?

The opening words of Section 129 are important. It applies where a person transports goods or stores goods while they are in transit in contravention of the provisions of the Act or the Rules.

Therefore, the officer's case should be connected to the goods being in transit and to a contravention of GST law/rules.

Situations that can commonly lead to transit verification

  • Invoice/document discrepancies identified during movement.
  • E-way bill issues or inconsistencies.
  • Mismatch between goods physically carried and accompanying records.
  • Movement without the documentation required under applicable GST rules.
  • Incorrect or incomplete particulars in transport documentation.
  • Movement where the GST officer alleges a contravention of applicable provisions.
  • Goods moving between locations where the supporting records do not adequately establish the transaction/movement.
  • Other factual circumstances in which the Act or Rules governing transit have been contravened.
Important distinction: An officer's allegation and the taxpayer's defence are separate things. A detention does not by itself establish that every allegation is legally or factually correct. The person concerned should examine the exact discrepancy, evidence and provision relied upon.

4. What can be detained or seized?

Section 129 refers to:

ItemPractical meaning
GoodsThe consignment that is being transported or stored while in transit.
ConveyanceThe means of transport carrying the goods, such as a truck or other conveyance.
Documents relating to goods and conveyanceRelevant transport/transaction documents connected with the goods and conveyance.

The provision is therefore broader than simply holding a truck. It addresses the goods and the transport arrangement together.

CBIC's GST FAQ explains the broad distinction between detention and seizure: detention involves preventing access through a legal order/notice, while seizure involves taking actual possession in the circumstances authorised by law.

5. Owner comes forward vs owner does not come forward

This is one of the most important changes in the current Section 129.

SituationRelease/payment formula for taxable goodsKey idea
Owner comes forwardApplicable tax + penalty equal to 200% of the tax payable.Owner comes forward for payment of tax and penalty.
Owner does not come forwardApplicable tax + penalty equal to the higher of 50% of value of goods or 200% of tax payable.The penalty formula changes where the owner does not come forward.
SecuritySecurity equivalent to the amount payable under clause (a) or (b), in prescribed form and manner.Security can be used as the statutory release route instead of immediate payment, subject to prescribed procedure.

The current CBIC text uses 200% in both taxable-goods penalty calculations, with the owner/non-owner distinction determining the second formula. This is an important point because older versions of Section 129 used different amounts before the amendment effective 1 January 2022.

6. How to calculate the Section 129 amount for taxable goods

Case A – Owner comes forward

Release amount = Applicable tax + 200% of tax payable as penalty

Example: Tax payable on the detained taxable goods is ₹1,00,000.

  • Applicable tax = ₹1,00,000
  • Penalty = 200% × ₹1,00,000 = ₹2,00,000
  • Total = ₹3,00,000

This assumes the case is correctly within clause (a) and no other amount/condition affects the working.

Case B – Owner does not come forward

Penalty = higher of (50% of value of goods) or (200% of tax payable)

The applicable tax is then payable in addition to that penalty.

Example: Goods value = ₹10,00,000 and tax payable = ₹1,80,000.

  • 50% of value = ₹5,00,000
  • 200% of tax = ₹3,60,000
  • Higher amount = ₹5,00,000 penalty
  • Applicable tax = ₹1,80,000
  • Total = ₹6,80,000
Do not use the old formula from pre-2022 articles. Current Section 129 must be read in its amended form.

7. How are exempted goods treated?

Section 129 contains separate amounts for exempted goods.

Owner positionAmount for exempted goods
Owner comes forward2% of the value of goods or ₹25,000, whichever is less.
Owner does not come forward5% of the value of goods or ₹25,000, whichever is less.

Example – exempt goods, owner comes forward

Value of exempt goods = ₹8,00,000.

  • 2% of value = ₹16,000
  • ₹25,000 limit = ₹25,000
  • Amount = ₹16,000, because the provision says whichever is less.

Example – exempt goods, owner does not come forward

Value = ₹8,00,000.

  • 5% of value = ₹40,000
  • ₹25,000 limit = ₹25,000
  • Amount = ₹25,000.

8. Can goods be released against security?

Yes. Clause (c) of Section 129(1) provides release upon furnishing a security equivalent to the amount payable under clause (a) or clause (b), in the form and manner prescribed.

This is important for businesses because detention can stop the physical movement of goods and can create project, production and customer-delivery consequences. Security may provide a statutory route to obtain release without making the entire amount an immediate cash outflow, subject to the applicable procedure and acceptance of the prescribed security.

Cash/payment route

The applicable amount is paid according to the statutory formula.

Security route

Security equivalent to the amount under clause (a) or (b) is furnished in the prescribed form and manner.

Finance impact

Payment affects cash flow; a security/bond route may have different working-capital implications.

Control point

The finance team should document the basis of the amount and the security furnished.

The current law contains specific timelines after detention or seizure.

These are current Section 129 statutory timelines. They should not be confused with the older pre-2022 wording. CBIC's consolidated Act reflects the seven-day notice/order structure and fifteen-day post-order period.

10. Is an opportunity of hearing required?

Yes. Section 129(4) states that no penalty shall be determined under sub-section (3) without giving the person concerned an opportunity of being heard.

This means a taxpayer/transporter should take the notice seriously and present the relevant factual and legal explanation before the penalty is determined.

A practical hearing preparation list

  • Identify exactly what the officer says is wrong.
  • Identify the relevant Act/Rule provision.
  • Match the allegation with the invoice and e-way bill.
  • Check physical goods against the documents.
  • Prepare proof of movement, delivery and transaction.
  • Explain any genuine data-entry or documentation error.
  • Where appropriate, demonstrate that the alleged contravention did not occur.
  • Keep copies of all documents submitted.
Important: The hearing is not merely a formality for the taxpayer. It is the opportunity to put the factual record before the officer before the penalty is determined.

11. What happens after payment?

Section 129(5) provides that on payment of the amount referred to in sub-section (1), all proceedings in respect of the notice specified in sub-section (3) shall be deemed to be concluded.

In simple terms, once the statutory amount is paid under Section 129(1), the proceedings relating to that detention notice are treated as concluded under Section 129(5).

Businesses should nevertheless preserve the payment proof, release documents, notice, order and reconciliation entry because the incident may be relevant for internal compliance review, audit and future litigation.

12. What if the penalty is not paid?

Section 129(6) provides that where the transporter or owner fails to pay the amount of penalty under sub-section (1) within 15 days from receipt of the copy of the order, the detained or seized goods or conveyance become liable to be sold or otherwise disposed of in the prescribed manner and time to recover the penalty.

There is an important special rule for the conveyance: the conveyance is to be released on payment by the transporter of the penalty under sub-section (3) or ₹1,00,000, whichever is less.

For perishable or hazardous goods, or goods likely to depreciate in value with the passage of time, the proper officer may reduce the fifteen-day period.

Business priority: If goods are detained, do not wait until the end of the statutory period before starting the legal, finance and logistics response. The consequences can include disruption to delivery, production, customer commitments and ultimately sale/disposal proceedings.

13. What happens to the truck or other conveyance?

Section 129 applies not only to goods but also to the conveyance used for carrying them. The current sub-section (6) separately provides a release mechanism for the conveyance where the transporter pays the penalty under sub-section (3) or ₹1 lakh, whichever is less.

PartyPractical issue
Owner of goodsMay need to establish ownership/come forward and deal with tax and penalty.
TransporterMay face operational disruption and has a specific statutory route concerning release of the conveyance under Section 129(6).
Vehicle ownerShould preserve transport documents and evidence concerning knowledge, instructions and the movement.
Finance/legal teamShould coordinate payment/security, documents, hearing and release.

Do not assume that a dispute involving the goods automatically means the vehicle owner is legally liable for the entire tax amount. The exact facts, statutory provision and role of each person must be examined.

14. Section 129 and e-way bill compliance

E-way bill compliance is one of the most common areas associated with transit verification. Rules 138 onwards contain the e-way bill framework, while Rule 138A deals with documents and devices to be carried by the person in charge of a conveyance in the circumstances specified by the Rules.

CBIC has explained that, for road transportation, the person in charge is required to carry the applicable invoice/bill of supply/delivery challan and the e-way bill number or prescribed electronic form of the e-way bill, subject to the applicable rules.

Before dispatch

  • Verify invoice/date/value.
  • Verify supplier and recipient GSTIN.
  • Verify vehicle details where required.
  • Generate/verify e-way bill as applicable.

At dispatch

  • Match physical goods to invoice.
  • Check quantity/UQC.
  • Confirm transporter details.
  • Provide required documents.

During movement

  • Track vehicle changes.
  • Update relevant e-way bill details where required.
  • Keep documents accessible.

At interception

  • Do not provide inconsistent explanations.
  • Collect the detention order/records.
  • Immediately inform tax/finance/legal team.

16. Section 129 vs Section 130 – what is the difference?

This is one of the most important questions in GST transit enforcement.

PointSection 129Section 130
Main subjectDetention, seizure and release of goods/conveyances in transit.Confiscation of goods/conveyances and levy of penalty.
Typical contextContravention involving goods transported or stored while in transit.Specified circumstances including cases involving intent to evade tax and other grounds listed in Section 130.
Immediate focusRelease of detained/seized goods/conveyance through payment or security.Confiscation adjudication and option of fine in lieu of confiscation where applicable.
Current relationshipSince the 2022 changes, Section 129 proceedings were delinked from the old automatic progression to Section 130 merely because the Section 129 amount was not paid within the old period.Operates under its own statutory conditions.

The 2021 Finance Act changes effective from 1 January 2022 expressly delinked Section 129 detention/seizure proceedings from the earlier Section 130 progression mechanism. CBIC's amendment material describes this change.

Do not use outdated articles: Older material may say that failure to pay within 14 days automatically leads to Section 130 proceedings. That is not the current Section 129(6) wording.

17. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 – Taxable goods, owner comes forward

Goods have tax payable of ₹2,00,000. The owner comes forward.

  • Tax = ₹2,00,000
  • Penalty = 200% × ₹2,00,000 = ₹4,00,000
  • Total release amount = ₹6,00,000

Example 2 – Taxable goods, owner does not come forward

Goods value is ₹12,00,000 and tax payable is ₹1,50,000.

  • 50% of value = ₹6,00,000
  • 200% of tax = ₹3,00,000
  • Higher penalty = ₹6,00,000
  • Tax = ₹1,50,000
  • Total = ₹7,50,000

Example 3 – Owner does not come forward, tax is relatively high

Goods value = ₹8,00,000 and tax payable = ₹3,00,000.

  • 50% of value = ₹4,00,000
  • 200% of tax = ₹6,00,000
  • Higher penalty = ₹6,00,000
  • Tax = ₹3,00,000
  • Total = ₹9,00,000

Example 4 – Exempt goods, owner comes forward

Exempt goods value = ₹20,00,000.

  • 2% = ₹40,000
  • ₹25,000 is lower
  • Amount = ₹25,000

Example 5 – Exempt goods, owner does not come forward

Exempt goods value = ₹4,00,000.

  • 5% = ₹20,000
  • ₹25,000 is higher
  • Amount = ₹20,000

Example 6 – Security instead of immediate payment

If the amount payable under the applicable clause is ₹6,00,000, the statutory security route is based on security equivalent to ₹6,00,000 in the prescribed form and manner.

Example 7 – Invoice value differs from physical goods

A truck carries 100 units but the accompanying invoice records 80 units. The officer may investigate whether there is a contravention. The taxpayer should immediately reconcile purchase order, invoice, packing list, e-way bill, delivery documents and physical quantity before accepting or disputing the allegation.

Example 8 – Vehicle number error

A vehicle number was entered incorrectly in an e-way bill. The correct legal consequence depends on the exact facts, timing, applicable Rule and nature of the error. The taxpayer should not assume that every data-entry mistake automatically justifies the same Section 129 consequence.

Example 9 – Genuine clerical error

A transporter carries genuine goods with genuine tax invoice and tax transaction, but a document contains a clerical error. The person should present evidence establishing the genuineness of the transaction and explain the error. The legal issue should be determined based on the applicable rules and facts.

Example 10 – Goods belong to a customer

A logistics company is transporting goods owned by another party. The transporter should maintain the transport agreement, invoice/e-way bill and instructions received. The owner/transporter roles can materially affect the statutory payment formula and subsequent proceedings.

Example 11 – Stock transfer between company locations

A company moves material from one warehouse to a project site. The finance/logistics team should maintain the correct delivery challan or invoice as applicable, e-way bill where required, stock-transfer documentation and destination records.

Example 12 – Job-work movement

Goods are sent to a job worker. The company should verify the documentation and e-way bill requirements applicable to the movement. If intercepted, the company should be able to demonstrate the underlying job-work relationship and movement purpose.

Example 13 – Goods are perishable

Perishable goods are detained. Section 129(6) allows the proper officer to reduce the fifteen-day period because the goods may depreciate in value or perish. The taxpayer should therefore act immediately rather than waiting for the normal period to expire.

Example 14 – Goods are hazardous

Hazardous goods are detained. The same reduced-period provision can become relevant. Safety and logistics teams should coordinate with tax/legal teams immediately.

Example 15 – Section 129 versus Section 130

A taxpayer sees a Section 129 detention order and assumes confiscation has already occurred. That is incorrect. Detention/seizure under Section 129 and confiscation under Section 130 are distinct statutory mechanisms.

Example 16 – Payment concludes notice proceedings

After the applicable Section 129 amount is paid, Section 129(5) says the proceedings in respect of the notice are deemed concluded. The business should retain the payment and release records for future audit purposes.

18. Recommended business workflow when a truck is detained

  1. Immediately identify the location and officer. Record where the interception occurred and the officer/authority involved.
  2. Secure copies of the detention/seizure document. Do not rely only on verbal communication.
  3. Freeze the facts. Record vehicle number, driver statement, goods description, quantity and physical condition.
  4. Collect the complete document pack. Invoice, e-way bill, delivery challan, purchase order, LR/consignment note and other relevant documents.
  5. Check the exact discrepancy. Do not respond generically.
  6. Calculate the possible amount independently. Verify tax, value, owner status and formula.
  7. Decide payment vs security strategy. Consider the statutory route and business cash-flow implications.
  8. Prepare the hearing response. Explain factual and legal grounds with evidence.
  9. Track the seven-day notice deadline.
  10. Track the seven-day order period after service of notice.
  11. Track the fifteen-day post-order payment period.
  12. Escalate immediately for perishable/hazardous goods.
  13. Reconcile the accounting entry after release.
  14. Conduct a root-cause review. Correct the internal control that caused the incident.

19. How to respond to a Section 129 notice

A useful response should be fact-specific. A practical structure is:

PartWhat to explain
1. Case detailsGSTIN, vehicle number, date/time, place of interception, invoice and e-way bill details.
2. Transaction backgroundWhat goods were being moved, from where, to where and for what purpose.
3. Alleged contraventionState precisely what the officer has alleged.
4. Factual responseExplain the correct facts with documentary evidence.
5. Legal responseIdentify the relevant Act/Rule and explain why the allegation does or does not apply.
6. CalculationWhere payment is being considered, independently verify the tax and penalty calculation under Section 129(1).
7. HearingPresent documents and submissions and retain proof.
8. ReleaseComplete the applicable payment/security procedure and obtain release documentation.
Golden rule: Never allow the driver alone to handle a significant Section 129 dispute without promptly informing the company's GST/finance/legal team. The commercial value of the goods, penalty exposure and statutory deadlines can be significant.

21. Internal controls to prevent Section 129 problems

  • Make e-way bill validation part of the dispatch checklist.
  • Use system controls to compare invoice number, date, value and GSTIN with e-way bill data.
  • Verify vehicle number before dispatch.
  • Monitor e-way bill validity and extensions where legally applicable.
  • Maintain a process for vehicle/transporter changes.
  • Train drivers to carry/access required documents but instruct them to escalate legal disputes immediately.
  • Keep a 24×7 escalation contact for high-value or time-sensitive shipments.
  • For project companies, reconcile material movement from central warehouse to site.
  • For job-work movements, maintain job-work challans and supporting records.
  • For branch/stock transfers, document the commercial and GST nature of the movement.
  • Review repeated detention incidents by transporter and route.
  • Maintain a root-cause register for every Section 129 incident.

22. Common mistakes and misconceptions

“Section 129 is only for missing e-way bills.”

No. The statutory wording is broader and covers transportation or storage while in transit in contravention of the Act or Rules.

“Current penalty is always 100% of tax.”

No. The current taxable-goods owner-comes-forward formula uses penalty equal to 200% of tax payable. The older 100% formula belongs to an earlier version of the law.

“If owner does not come forward, penalty is always 50% of value.”

No. For taxable goods, the current formula is the higher of 50% of value or 200% of tax payable, plus applicable tax.

“Goods can be detained without any order.”

Section 129 contains a proviso requiring an order of detention or seizure to be served on the person transporting the goods.

“There is no hearing in Section 129.”

Section 129(4) expressly requires an opportunity of being heard before penalty is determined under sub-section (3).

“After 15 days, Section 130 automatically starts.”

That was associated with the earlier version of the provision. The current Section 129(6) deals with sale/disposal to recover penalty, while Section 130 has its own independent statutory conditions.

“Paying the amount means the goods can be ignored in accounting.”

No. The company should record the payment, release and root cause and preserve the entire case file.

“Vehicle owner is automatically liable for the goods tax.”

Liability depends on the statutory provision and facts. The roles of goods owner, transporter and conveyance owner should be separately analysed.

24. Frequently Asked Questions about Section 129

What is Section 129 of the CGST Act?

It deals with detention, seizure and release of goods and conveyances in transit when goods are transported or stored while in transit in contravention of the GST Act or Rules.

Can a GST officer detain a truck under Section 129?

Yes, where the statutory conditions are satisfied, the conveyance used for carrying the goods can be liable to detention or seizure.

Can goods be detained for an e-way bill issue?

E-way bill compliance is one area that can be relevant to transit enforcement, but the exact issue must be analysed against the applicable GST Rules and facts.

What is the current penalty when the owner comes forward?

For taxable goods, the current Section 129(1)(a) formula is applicable tax plus a penalty equal to 200% of the tax payable. Exempt goods have a separate 2%/₹25,000 formula.

What happens if the owner does not come forward?

For taxable goods, the penalty is the higher of 50% of the value of goods or 200% of tax payable, with applicable tax also payable. Exempt goods have the separate 5%/₹25,000 formula.

Can I get release by furnishing security?

Yes. Section 129(1)(c) provides release upon furnishing security equivalent to the amount payable under clause (a) or (b), in the prescribed form and manner.

How quickly must the notice be issued?

The proper officer is required to issue the Section 129(3) notice within seven days of detention or seizure.

How quickly must the order be passed?

The current Section 129(3) wording provides for the order within seven days from the date of service of the notice.

Is a hearing mandatory?

Yes. Section 129(4) says no penalty shall be determined under sub-section (3) without giving the person concerned an opportunity of being heard.

What happens after payment?

Under Section 129(5), proceedings in respect of the notice under sub-section (3) are deemed concluded on payment of the amount referred to in sub-section (1).

What happens if the amount is not paid?

After fifteen days from receipt of the order copy, the detained/seized goods or conveyance may become liable to sale or other disposal under the prescribed procedure to recover the penalty.

Can the fifteen-day period be reduced?

Yes, where the goods are perishable or hazardous or likely to depreciate in value with passage of time, the proper officer may reduce the period.

How can the transporter get the vehicle released?

Section 129(6) provides that the conveyance shall be released on payment by the transporter of the penalty under sub-section (3) or ₹1 lakh, whichever is less.

Is Section 129 the same as Section 130?

No. Section 129 deals with detention, seizure and release in transit. Section 130 deals with confiscation and has its own statutory conditions.

Did Section 129 change in 2022?

Yes. Significant amendments effective from 1 January 2022 changed the payment formula and procedure, including the seven-day notice/order framework and the current fifteen-day post-order recovery period.

Does Section 129 apply only to taxable goods?

No. The section contains separate release/payment amounts for exempted goods.

Can a genuine clerical mistake lead to detention?

The legal consequence depends on the precise mistake, applicable GST Rules and facts. The taxpayer should demonstrate the genuine transaction and correct documentation/evidence during the proceeding.

What documents should be kept in a detained-goods case?

Invoice, e-way bill, delivery challan where applicable, transport documents, purchase/sales records, GST details, detention order, notice, reply, hearing documents, order and payment/security proof should be preserved as relevant.

26. Quick revision – Section 129 in 60 seconds

  • Subject: Detention, seizure and release of goods and conveyances in transit.
  • Trigger: Goods transported/stored while in transit in contravention of the Act or Rules.
  • Owner comes forward: Tax + 200% of tax as penalty for taxable goods; exempt goods use 2% of value or ₹25,000, whichever is less.
  • Owner does not come forward: Tax + higher of 50% of value or 200% of tax as penalty for taxable goods; exempt goods use 5% of value or ₹25,000, whichever is less.
  • Security: Equivalent security can be furnished in prescribed form/manner.
  • Notice: Within 7 days of detention/seizure.
  • Order: Within 7 days from service of notice.
  • Hearing: Required before penalty is determined.
  • Payment: Concludes proceedings concerning the notice.
  • Non-payment: After 15 days from receipt of order, goods/conveyance can become liable to sale/disposal under prescribed procedure.
  • Vehicle: Transporter can obtain release under the special ₹1 lakh/penalty provision, whichever is less.
  • Remember: Current Section 129 must not be confused with the pre-2022 version.
