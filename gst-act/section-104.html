Section 104 CGST Act — Advance Ruling to be Void in Certain Circumstances

A practical guide to what happens when an advance ruling was obtained using fraud, suppression of material facts or misrepresentation of facts — and how finance, tax and compliance teams should respond.

1. Why Section 104 matters

An advance ruling is valuable because it gives a taxpayer clarity on specified GST questions relating to its proposed or undertaken supplies. But that protection depends on the integrity of the facts placed before the Authority or Appellate Authority.

Section 104 addresses the serious situation where the ruling itself was obtained through fraud, suppression of material facts, or misrepresentation of facts. In such a case, the Authority or Appellate Authority can declare the ruling void ab-initio. The law then operates as though that advance ruling had never been made.

Practical message: An advance ruling should never be treated as a permanent shield if the application did not present the transaction, contract, commercial arrangement and other material facts accurately and completely.

2. Section 104 in simple language

Think of Section 104 as a safeguard for the integrity of the advance-ruling system.

SituationWhat it means in practice
Correct facts + genuine applicationThe ruling can operate within its statutory scope, subject to Section 103 and later changes in law, facts or circumstances.
Important facts deliberately or materially withheldThe Authority may examine whether the ruling was obtained by suppression of material facts.
Facts presented inaccuratelyA misrepresentation issue may arise if the ruling was obtained on an incorrect factual foundation.
Fraud used to obtain the rulingThe ruling may be declared void ab-initio after the statutory process and hearing.

The important words are “void ab-initio”. This is stronger than simply saying that the ruling will stop applying from a future date.

3. The three statutory triggers

Section 104 focuses on three grounds: fraud, suppression of material facts, and misrepresentation of facts. The practical question for a tax team is not merely whether a document was omitted or an answer was imperfect. The real issue is whether the ruling was obtained on a legally significant and unreliable factual basis.

GroundPractical questionTypical risk area
FraudWas deception or dishonest conduct used to obtain the ruling?Fabricated documents, deliberately false facts, manipulated evidence
Suppression of material factsWas an important fact deliberately or materially withheld even though it could affect the question decided?Contract clauses, consideration, end-use, recipient, actual supply structure
Misrepresentation of factsWas a material fact represented incorrectly so that the ruling was based on an inaccurate factual picture?Incorrect description of service, wrong contractual role, incomplete transaction flow

4. What can “fraud” mean in an advance-ruling context?

Fraud is the most serious of the three grounds. In a practical tax file, the risk is not limited to a dramatic forged document. Any deliberate deceptive conduct that is material to obtaining the ruling can create serious exposure.

Examples of high-risk conduct

  • Submitting a document known to be false or manipulated.
  • Presenting a commercial arrangement as something materially different from the actual arrangement.
  • Creating a factual narrative designed to obtain a preferred tax result while withholding contradictory evidence.
  • Providing a description of the supply that knowingly omits a material component of the transaction.
Control point: The person signing an advance-ruling application should have a clear evidence trail showing who prepared the facts, who verified the contracts, and who approved the final factual statement.

5. Suppression of material facts

Not every omitted fact is automatically a “material fact”. The practical concern is whether the omitted information could affect the legal question on which the ruling was sought.

Facts that may become material

  • The actual nature and scope of the supply.
  • Key clauses in the work order, agreement or purchase contract.
  • Who owns or controls the goods involved in a service.
  • Who is the recipient and who ultimately benefits from the activity.
  • Whether the supplier performs one service or a bundled/composite arrangement.
  • Whether the transaction is principal-to-principal, agency, job work, subcontracting or another structure.
  • Commercial consideration, reimbursement, recovery or other amounts connected with the supply.
  • Facts about the place, end-use, movement or deployment of goods where those facts affect the GST issue.

Practical test: Ask, “If the Authority had known this fact before deciding the question, could the reasoning or conclusion reasonably have been different?” If yes, the fact deserves serious review.

6. Misrepresentation of facts

Misrepresentation is different from simply making a legal argument that later turns out to be unsuccessful. A taxpayer is allowed to take a legal position. The concern under Section 104 is the factual foundation on which that position was presented.

SituationRisk assessment
“We believe the supply is exempt because of X.”Normally a legal position requiring interpretation; disagreement alone does not establish misrepresentation.
“The contract contains only service X,” when the actual contract also contains a material supply component.Potential factual misrepresentation.
“Goods are never transferred,” when records show actual transfer as part of the transaction.Potentially material misrepresentation.
A minor clerical typo that has no bearing on the ruling.Not automatically a Section 104 issue; materiality matters.

7. What makes a fact “material”?

For business teams, materiality should be assessed by connecting the fact to the exact question referred for advance ruling.

Step 1Identify the question asked in the application.
Step 2List every factual assumption used to support the applicant's position.
Step 3Compare those assumptions with contracts, invoices, operations and accounting records.
Step 4Check whether any omitted or incorrect fact could change the GST analysis.

This is why an advance-ruling file should be maintained like a major tax-position file rather than as a single PDF order.

8. What does “void ab-initio” mean?

Void ab-initio means void from the beginning. Section 104 does not merely say that the ruling stops being effective from the date of the Section 104 order. Once the ruling is validly declared void under the section, the Act and rules apply as if that advance ruling had never been made.

Business impact: Tax teams should not assume that the earlier period is automatically protected merely because returns were filed consistently with the advance ruling. A Section 104 order can fundamentally change the legal basis on which that past treatment was adopted.

The exact tax demand, interest, ITC and penalty consequences will depend on the underlying transaction, the applicable provisions and the facts of the case. Section 104 itself is the mechanism that removes the ruling's protective effect.

9. The applicant or appellant must get an opportunity of being heard

Section 104 contains an express hearing safeguard. The Authority or Appellate Authority cannot pass the Section 104 order without giving the applicant or appellant an opportunity of being heard.

What should a business do when a Section 104 proceeding is initiated?

  1. Obtain and preserve the notice or communication initiating the issue.
  2. Identify the exact ruling and application documents involved.
  3. Prepare a fact-by-fact comparison between the original application and the actual transaction.
  4. Collect contracts, amendments, invoices, purchase orders, work orders, accounting entries and correspondence.
  5. Identify whether the alleged omission is actually material to the question ruled upon.
  6. Prepare a written factual response and legal submissions with documentary support.
  7. Ensure the authorised representative understands both the original ruling and the underlying business process.

10. What happens after the ruling is declared void?

Once the ruling is declared void under Section 104, the Act and rules apply to the applicant or appellant as if the advance ruling had never been made.

AreaWhat the finance/tax team should reassess
Output taxCorrect taxability, classification, rate, place/time/value of supply and applicable exemptions/concessions.
ITCWhether input tax credit was admissible and whether any credit requires reversal or adjustment under the applicable provisions.
InvoicesWhether invoices issued during the affected period reflect the correct tax treatment.
ReturnsWhether GSTR-1, GSTR-3B and other relevant records need reconciliation and corrective action.
Customer impactWhether tax needs to be recovered, credit notes issued, or customer positions reviewed.
AccountingWhether tax liabilities, provisions, contingencies or recoveries need accounting review.
AuditWhether statutory/internal/tax audit working papers need to be updated and the matter escalated.

11. The special limitation-period rule

Section 104 contains an important protection against a limitation problem created by reliance on the advance ruling. The period beginning with the date of the advance ruling and ending with the date of the Section 104 order is excluded while computing the periods specified in the relevant sub-sections of Sections 73 and 74. citeturn0search25

This means finance teams should not conclude that a tax period is automatically beyond the department's reach simply because time passed while the advance ruling was in force and before it was declared void.

Practical control: When a Section 104 order is received, prepare a period-wise limitation calculation immediately. Do not rely only on the original annual-return due date.

12. Records and evidence: the most important defence

For an advance ruling, the quality of the factual record is as important as the legal submission. A company should maintain a dedicated “Advance Ruling Evidence File”.

DocumentWhy it matters
Original application and annexuresShows exactly what facts and questions were presented.
Contracts/work ordersEstablishes the actual commercial arrangement.
Technical scope and specificationsCan establish what is actually supplied or performed.
Invoices and accounting entriesShows how the transaction was implemented.
GST returns and reconciliationsShows the tax treatment followed after the ruling.
Internal approvalsShows how the factual position was verified and adopted.
Contract amendmentsImportant where the business changed after the original ruling.

13. Returns, ITC and accounting: practical implementation

Suppose a company obtained an advance ruling and then built its invoicing and GST return process around it. If the ruling is later declared void ab-initio, the tax team should not simply change the ERP tax code and move on.

Use a transaction-impact matrix

PeriodTransactionOld treatmentPotential correct treatmentAction
Apr–JunService invoicesTax not chargedTaxability to be reassessedRecompute exposure
Jul–SepInput servicesITC claimedEligibility to be reassessedReview ITC

Then reconcile the result with the general ledger, sales register, purchase register, GSTR-1, GSTR-3B and relevant GST portal data.

14. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Omitted contract clause

A contractor seeks a ruling on the GST treatment of a project and submits the main work order. A separate contractual clause requires the contractor to provide additional equipment/services that materially changes the supply structure, but that clause is not disclosed.

Risk: If the omitted clause is material to the question decided, the taxpayer may face a Section 104 issue. The finance team should preserve the complete contract and explain the actual scope rather than relying only on the favourable ruling.

Example 2 — Incorrect description of service

A company describes an activity as pure manpower support in its application, while the actual agreement requires the supplier to perform an end-to-end operational function with responsibility for specified outputs.

Risk: The factual description may not match the contractual and operational reality. If the difference was material to the ruling, the ruling's validity can be questioned.

Example 3 — Different project after the ruling

A ruling is obtained for one project based on specific contractual terms. Later projects contain materially different obligations, consideration mechanisms or supply structures.

Lesson: Section 104 is not the only issue. Section 103 must also be considered because a ruling is binding subject to changes in law, facts or circumstances. Do not automatically extend the old ruling to a materially different project.

Example 4 — Group company assumption

Company A obtains a ruling and the group starts using the same tax treatment in Company B without analysing the separate legal entity, contract and factual position.

Lesson: The group should not treat another entity's ruling as universal protection. The scope of binding effect must be examined before adopting the position.

Example 5 — Minor clerical error

An application contains a typographical error in a non-material date but the contracts, transaction structure and question remain correctly described.

Lesson: A mistake should not automatically be labelled fraud or suppression. The relevance and materiality of the error need to be assessed carefully.

15. Construction & infrastructure example

Consider an infrastructure company executing a large works contract. It approaches the Authority for a ruling on the GST treatment of a particular project arrangement. The application describes the contract as a single construction service, but the actual agreement contains separate obligations for material supply, equipment deployment, design support and another recoverable component.

If those facts are relevant to the question on which the ruling was obtained, the tax team should not assume that the ruling safely covers every invoice raised under the project.

Recommended project-level control

  • Keep the exact contract version used in the advance-ruling application.
  • Map each material contract clause to the facts stated in the application.
  • Track amendments, variation orders and supplementary agreements.
  • Before using the ruling for a new project, compare the new contract with the original factual matrix.
  • Maintain a project-wise GST position paper.
  • Escalate any material change to the tax head before changing the ERP tax configuration.
Practical benefit: This creates an audit trail showing that the company did not blindly rely on an old ruling; it actively verified that the factual assumptions remained accurate.

16. Section 102 vs Section 103 vs Section 104

ProvisionCore purposePractical trigger
Section 102Rectification of an error apparent on the face of the record.Clear record-level error, subject to the statutory six-month framework.
Section 103Defines the binding effect of an advance ruling.Applicant/officer scope; binding effect continues unless law, facts or circumstances supporting the ruling change.
Section 104Deals with an advance ruling obtained through fraud, suppression of material facts or misrepresentation of facts.Integrity of the factual basis on which the ruling was obtained.

Easy memory rule: Section 102 asks “Is there an apparent error in the order?” Section 103 asks “Who is bound and for how long?” Section 104 asks “Was the ruling obtained on a materially dishonest or inaccurate factual basis?”

17. Common mistakes businesses make

  1. Submitting only the favourable facts. A tax application should present the transaction fairly, including facts that may complicate the preferred position.
  2. Ignoring contract amendments. The ruling was based on a factual matrix; later amendments can change that matrix.
  3. Allowing consultants to draft facts without business verification. The operations and commercial teams should validate factual statements.
  4. Using a ruling as a group-wide tax policy without analysis. Binding effect is not automatically universal.
  5. Not retaining the evidence used for the ruling. Years later, the company may need to demonstrate what was actually represented.
  6. Changing ERP tax codes without a transaction-impact study. A tax position should flow through invoices, returns, ledgers and reconciliations consistently.
  7. Ignoring limitation calculations after a Section 104 order. The special exclusion rule needs to be considered.

18. Practical response workflow

1. Freeze the factsPreserve contracts, applications, invoices and correspondence.
2. CompareMatch the original application with actual business records.
3. Assess materialityIdentify whether the alleged fact could affect the ruling.
4. QuantifyCalculate tax, ITC, interest and customer/accounting exposure.
5. Prepare responseUse documentary evidence for each disputed fact.
6. HearingEnsure the authorised representative addresses the factual and legal issues.
7. ReconfigureIf the ruling is declared void, update tax treatment only after proper review.
8. Close controlsUpdate SOP, ERP, tax position register and audit file.

20. Frequently Asked Questions

Can an advance ruling be declared void merely because the Authority later disagrees with the legal interpretation?

Not merely for that reason. Section 104 is directed at fraud, suppression of material facts or misrepresentation of facts in obtaining the ruling. A legal disagreement should not automatically be treated as one of those grounds.

Does every mistake in an application amount to suppression?

No. The practical significance, materiality and circumstances of the mistake matter. A harmless typographical error is very different from withholding a fact that could change the GST conclusion.

Does void ab-initio mean the ruling is treated as never having existed?

Yes. Section 104 expressly provides that, after such declaration, the Act and rules apply as if the advance ruling had never been made.

Can the company be heard before the ruling is declared void?

Yes. Section 104 contains a specific requirement that an opportunity of being heard be given before the order is passed. citeturn0search25

Does Section 104 automatically determine the amount of tax payable?

No. Section 104 removes the advance ruling's protective effect in the circumstances covered by the provision. The actual tax liability must then be determined under the applicable GST provisions based on the underlying transactions and facts.

What should a company do immediately after receiving a Section 104 notice?

Preserve the complete ruling file, compare the original factual submissions with actual transactions, identify the disputed material facts, quantify possible exposure, prepare documentary evidence and take professional legal/tax advice before responding.

Is Section 104 the same as Section 102 rectification?

No. Section 102 addresses an error apparent on the face of the record. Section 104 addresses the integrity of the factual basis on which an advance ruling was obtained.

22. Key takeaways

  • Section 104 protects the integrity of the advance-ruling process.
  • The three core grounds are fraud, suppression of material facts and misrepresentation of facts.
  • “Void ab-initio” means the ruling is treated as if it had never been made once validly declared void.
  • The applicant/appellant must receive an opportunity of being heard before the Section 104 order is passed.
  • The period between the advance ruling and the Section 104 order is excluded for the specified limitation calculations under Sections 73 and 74.
  • Businesses should maintain a complete factual and documentary file supporting every advance-ruling application.
  • When the ruling is declared void, tax, ITC, invoices, returns, accounting and customer impact should be reassessed systematically.
