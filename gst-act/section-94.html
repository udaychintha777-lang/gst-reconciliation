Section 94 CGST Act — GST Liability After Discontinuance or Change in Constitution

What happens to GST when a firm, association of persons or HUF stops business? What if a partnership is reconstituted by adding or removing partners? Can old GST dues still be recovered after the change?

Section 94 deals with these situations. This detailed guide explains discontinuance, reconstitution, joint and several liability, historical GST demands and the practical steps finance teams and partners should take when a business structure changes.

1. Why Section 94 matters

Closing or changing a business structure does not automatically erase historical GST liability.

A partnership firm may stop trading, an association of persons may discontinue its business, or an HUF may discontinue its business. Similarly, a firm or AOP may change its constitution by admitting or removing partners/members.

GST liabilities relating to periods before the event can surface later through scrutiny, audit, investigation or assessment. Section 94 prevents the structural change itself from becoming a shield against those historical dues.

Core idea: Section 94 preserves GST liability for the period before discontinuance or reconstitution and identifies the persons who remain liable.

3. Section 94 in simple language

Suppose a partnership firm operates a construction business and stops operations on 31 March. Two years later, GST authorities determine that the firm underpaid tax for the period before 31 March.

The firm cannot simply say: “We closed, so there is no one to pay.” Section 94 allows the earlier liability to be determined as though the discontinuance had not occurred, and the persons who were partners at discontinuance can remain jointly and severally liable.

Now consider a different situation: the firm changes its partners on 1 April. Section 94(2) can make the partners before and after reconstitution jointly and severally liable for GST dues relating to the period before reconstitution.

Business structure changes ≠ historical GST liability disappears

4. Complete statutory structure

PartCore rule
94(1)(a)Where a firm, AOP or HUF discontinues business, tax, interest or penalty payable up to discontinuance may be determined as if no discontinuance had taken place.
94(1)(b)Persons who were partners/members at discontinuance are jointly and severally liable for the tax, interest and penalty of the firm/AOP/HUF up to discontinuance.
94(2)Where a firm or AOP changes its constitution, partners/members before and after reconstitution are jointly and severally liable for dues relating to the period before reconstitution, without prejudice to Section 90.
94(3)The discontinuance rules in subsection (1) apply, so far as may be, to an association of persons or a Hindu Undivided Family that has discontinued business.

The operative statutory wording focuses on continuity of liability despite discontinuance and on liability surrounding reconstitution.

5. Discontinuance of firm, AOP or HUF

Discontinuance means the business stops. It does not mean that all historical GST matters are automatically closed.

For Section 94, first identify:

✓ Exact discontinuance date
✓ Entity type
✓ GSTIN
✓ Tax periods up to discontinuance
✓ Pending returns
✓ Outstanding demands
✓ Partners/members at discontinuance
✓ Business assets and records

6. Determining dues as if no discontinuance occurred

This is one of the most important phrases in Section 94(1)(a).

The department can determine the tax, interest or penalty payable up to the date of discontinuance as though the discontinuance had not happened.

In practical terms, stopping the business does not prevent the department from completing the normal GST determination process for the earlier period.

Example: Firm closes on 31 March 2027. A scrutiny in 2029 identifies under-reported taxable turnover for FY 2026-27. The closure date does not prevent determination of the earlier liability.

7. Partners/members after discontinuance

At the time of discontinuance, every person who was a partner of the firm or member of the AOP/HUF covered by the provision can be jointly and severally liable for the relevant tax, interest and penalty.

This is why a closure checklist should record the exact partners/members on the discontinuance date.

8. What if the demand is determined after discontinuance?

Section 94(1)(b) expressly covers liability whether the tax and interest were determined or the penalty was imposed before or after discontinuance.

Therefore, a firm should not assume that a demand issued after closure is automatically invalid merely because the entity had stopped business.

The tax period and statutory basis of the demand still need to be examined.

9. Reconstitution of a firm

Reconstitution occurs when the constitution of a firm changes. This can happen through admission of a partner, retirement of a partner or another change that alters the constitution.

Section 94(2) creates a special rule for historical GST dues. Partners as the firm existed before reconstitution and partners as it exists after reconstitution can both be jointly and severally liable for tax, interest or penalty due from the firm for a period before reconstitution.

Important: A partner joining after the disputed transaction can still be covered by Section 94(2) for pre-reconstitution dues. The subsection specifically includes partners before and after reconstitution.

10. Reconstitution of an AOP

Section 94(2) also covers a change in the constitution of an association of persons.

The same historical-liability principle applies: persons/members before and after reconstitution can be jointly and severally liable for the AOP's tax, interest or penalty relating to the period before reconstitution.

11. Before-and-after partners

PersonReconstitution scenarioSection 94 position

Incoming partnerJoins after reconstitutionCan be jointly and severally liable for dues for the period before reconstitution under Section 94(2).

This is one of the most important practical points to explain to a person entering an existing partnership.

12. Joint and several liability

“Jointly and severally” is stronger than simply saying each partner pays according to profit ratio.

The statutory liability can expose each covered person to the firm's relevant tax, interest and penalty. Internal arrangements between partners do not automatically change the department's statutory rights.

Professional point: Partnership agreements can allocate economic responsibility between partners, but that private allocation should not be confused with the GST department's statutory liability under Section 94.

13. Relationship with Section 90

Section 90 deals with liability of partners of a firm. Section 94(2) expressly operates without prejudice to Section 90 when the firm is reconstituted.

This means a reconstitution analysis should not stop at Section 94. The partner's status before and after the change and the specific liability provision must be considered together.

14. HUF and discontinuance

Section 94(1) and 94(3) cover discontinuance of business by a Hindu Undivided Family.

The practical issue is historical GST exposure up to discontinuance. A later departmental determination can still relate to the period when the HUF carried on the business.

HUF partition itself is dealt with separately under Section 93(2). Therefore, do not automatically use Section 94 for every HUF event; identify whether the event is discontinuance or partition.

15. GST registration consequences

Section 94 deals with liability; cancellation or amendment of GST registration is governed by separate provisions.

When a firm discontinues business, the GST registration should be reviewed and cancellation/final-return obligations handled according to the applicable registration framework.

When a firm is reconstituted, changes in constitution, partners and authorised persons should be properly reflected in the registration records where required.

Remember: GST registration cancellation or amendment does not by itself extinguish historical tax, interest or penalty liability.

18. Assessment and recovery

Section 94 identifies liability after discontinuance or reconstitution; it does not replace the normal GST provisions for determining and recovering tax.

When a demand is received, check:

  • Tax period
  • Date of discontinuance/reconstitution
  • Partners/members at the relevant date
  • Whether the demand relates to a pre-change period
  • Tax and interest calculation
  • Penalty
  • Payments already made
  • Appeal or stay
  • Statutory limitation applicable to the underlying proceeding

19. Construction and infrastructure examples

Construction firm closes after project completion

A partnership completes a ₹100 crore project and discontinues business. A later audit finds under-reported GST on RA bills and subcontractor transactions. Section 94 keeps the pre-discontinuance liability relevant.

New partner joins an infrastructure firm

A partner joins on 1 April after reconstitution. A GST demand in 2028 relates to the period before 1 April. Section 94(2) must be examined because the incoming partner is expressly within the before-and-after reconstitution framework.

Partner retires after a large contract

The retiring partner should obtain a detailed GST due-diligence statement because retirement does not automatically eliminate statutory exposure for earlier periods.

20. Detailed practical cases

Case 1 — Firm closes, demand later

Firm discontinues in March; demand is determined two years later. Section 94(1) applies to the historical period.

Case 2 — Partner retires

Retiring partner remains relevant for pre-reconstitution liability.

Case 3 — New partner joins

Incoming partner can be jointly and severally liable for pre-reconstitution dues under Section 94(2).

Case 4 — Firm changes profit ratio only

Determine whether the change actually constitutes a change in constitution; do not assume every commercial change has the same legal effect.

Case 5 — AOP constitution changes

Map the date and members before/after change and identify historical tax exposure.

Case 6 — HUF stops business

Historical GST exposure up to discontinuance remains relevant.

Case 7 — HUF partitions

Analyse Section 93(2), not merely Section 94.

Case 8 — Old ITC dispute

Preserve invoice, 2B and payment evidence for the historical tax period.

Case 9 — RCM liability

Historical RCM exposure should be included in the closure due-diligence statement.

Case 10 — Demand paid partly before closure

Reconcile the outstanding balance and the legal basis of any remaining demand.

Case 11 — Business transferred rather than discontinued

Analyse transfer-of-business provisions separately; Section 94 is focused on discontinuance/reconstitution.

Case 12 — Incoming partner due diligence

Review historical GST returns, notices, demands, litigation and reconciliations before accepting admission into an existing firm.

24. Common mistakes

  • Assuming closure automatically ends GST liability.
  • Assuming a demand cannot be raised after closure.
  • Ignoring the incoming partner's potential historical liability.
  • Assuming liability follows only profit-sharing ratio.
  • Failing to record the exact reconstitution date.
  • Failing to preserve old GST records.
  • Confusing reconstitution with dissolution.
  • Confusing HUF partition under Section 93 with HUF discontinuance under Section 94.
  • Ignoring Section 90 when analysing partners.
  • Failing to review GST notices before admitting a new partner.
  • Treating registration cancellation as settlement of tax dues.
  • Ignoring RCM and ITC issues in closure due diligence.

26. Legislative position

Section 94 is part of the original CGST Act framework dealing with liability in special business-structure situations. Its practical role is to prevent discontinuance or reconstitution from defeating GST liability relating to earlier periods.

The current operative structure continues to focus on three situations: discontinuance of a firm/AOP/HUF, change in constitution of a firm/AOP, and application of the discontinuance framework to AOP/HUF. The section also expressly covers tax and interest determined, and penalties imposed, after discontinuance.

27. Section 94 GST FAQs

What is Section 94 of the CGST Act?

Section 94 deals with GST liability when a firm, AOP or HUF discontinues business and when a firm or AOP changes its constitution.

Can GST demand be raised after a firm closes?

Yes. Section 94 expressly allows historical liability to be determined after discontinuance.

Are partners liable after firm closure?

Persons who were partners at discontinuance can be jointly and severally liable for the firm's relevant tax, interest and penalty.

Can an incoming partner be liable for old GST dues?

Yes. Section 94(2) expressly includes partners as the firm existed before and after reconstitution for dues relating to the period before reconstitution.

What happens when a partner retires?

Retirement does not automatically eliminate historical GST exposure for periods before reconstitution. Section 90 and Section 94 should be considered together.

Does Section 94 apply to AOP?

Yes. It covers discontinuance and change in constitution of an association of persons in the circumstances specified by the section.

Does Section 94 apply to HUF?

It applies to discontinuance of business by an HUF. HUF partition is separately addressed under Section 93.

What does jointly and severally liable mean?

It means the statutory liability is not automatically limited to each person's profit share. The department's rights operate according to the statutory liability provision.

Does GST registration cancellation remove old dues?

No. Cancellation is a registration process and does not by itself extinguish historical tax, interest or penalty liability.

What should a new partner check before joining?

Review GST returns, GSTR-2B reconciliations, tax payments, RCM, notices, assessments, demands, appeals and litigation for historical periods.

What if the demand is determined after reconstitution?

The date of determination does not by itself remove the pre-reconstitution liability. Section 94(2) specifically addresses dues for periods before reconstitution.

What if a business is transferred instead of discontinued?

Transfer-of-business provisions should be examined separately. Section 94 is specifically focused on discontinuance and reconstitution.

Is Section 94 the same as Section 93?

No. Section 93 addresses events such as death, partition, dissolution and termination of specified guardianship/trust arrangements. Section 94 focuses on discontinuance and reconstitution.

29. Key takeaways

  • Closing a firm, AOP or HUF business does not automatically erase historical GST liability.
  • Tax, interest and penalty up to discontinuance can still be determined after the business stops.
  • Partners/members at discontinuance can be jointly and severally liable.
  • On reconstitution, partners/members before and after the change can be liable for pre-reconstitution dues.
  • An incoming partner should therefore conduct GST due diligence before joining an existing firm.
  • Private agreements between partners do not automatically override statutory GST liability.
  • Section 90 must be considered alongside Section 94 for partnership matters.
  • HUF partition is primarily a Section 93 issue, while HUF business discontinuance is addressed by Section 94.
  • Registration cancellation does not settle historical GST dues.
  • Historical records should be preserved even after closure or reconstitution.
