GST ACT — SECTION 82

Section 82 CGST Act — First Charge on Property for GST Tax, Interest & Penalty

Section 82 creates a statutory first-charge framework over the property of a person for amounts payable to Government on account of GST tax, interest or penalty. It is an important recovery provision for businesses, property owners, lenders, buyers and insolvency professionals. This detailed guide explains the section in simple language, its relationship with GST recovery, the special Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code exception, property transactions, secured lending and practical due-diligence controls.

1. Why Section 82 matters

Searches such as “Section 82 CGST Act”, “Section 82 GST”, “GST first charge on property”, “GST tax first charge”, “GST dues against property”, “GST property recovery”, “GST lender priority”, “GST and insolvency”, “GST charge over property” and “does GST have first charge on property” are directly connected with this provision.

Section 82 is important because GST recovery is not limited to the taxpayer's cash balance. Where tax, interest or penalty is payable, the CGST Act creates a statutory first-charge rule over the property of the person liable to pay.

For a finance team, this means a large GST demand can affect the way property transactions, financing, refinancing and asset disposals should be evaluated. For a buyer or lender, GST due diligence can become important when acquiring property from a person with significant GST exposure.

Simple idea: Section 82 gives the Government a statutory first-charge claim over the relevant property for GST tax, interest or penalty, but the section itself expressly makes the rule subject to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code.

2. Section 82 in very simple language

Imagine a company owes ₹5 crore to the Government towards GST-related tax, interest and penalty. The company owns valuable property.

Section 82 says that, subject to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code exception, the amount payable to Government becomes a first charge on the company's property.

GST tax / interest / penalty payable → statutory first charge on property → subject to IBC

This is different from Section 81. Section 81 deals with certain fraudulent transfers made to defeat Government revenue. Section 82 establishes a statutory charge over property.

3. What the law actually says

Section 82 provides that, notwithstanding anything contrary contained in any law for the time being in force, save as otherwise provided in the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, any amount payable by a taxable person or any other person on account of tax, interest or penalty which that person is liable to pay to Government shall be a first charge on the property of that taxable person or such person.

PhrasePractical meaning
Notwithstanding anything contraryThe statutory charge is intended to operate despite conflicting provisions of other laws, subject to the express IBC saving.
Save as otherwise provided in IBCThe CGST Act itself recognises that the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code can determine the applicable position in insolvency.
Any amount payableThe section is concerned with amounts that are legally payable.
Tax, interest or penaltyAll three are expressly mentioned.
First chargeGovernment's statutory claim receives priority according to the applicable legal framework.
Property of such personThe charge attaches to property of the person liable, subject to the Act and other applicable law.

4. What “first charge” means

In simple terms, a first charge means that the law gives a statutory priority claim over the property for the specified Government dues.

It is important not to reduce this to the statement “Government automatically takes ownership of the property”. Section 82 creates a charge; it does not itself transfer ownership to Government.

The actual enforcement process is connected with the separate GST recovery machinery, including Section 79 and the relevant Rules.

Important distinction: Charge ≠ ownership. Section 82 creates a statutory priority claim; recovery/attachment/sale are governed by the applicable recovery provisions and Rules.

5. Whose property is covered?

The section covers property of the taxable person or any other person liable to pay the specified tax, interest or penalty.

This wording matters because GST law contains situations where persons other than the original taxable person can become liable for amounts. The exact liability must therefore be identified before applying Section 82.

QuestionWhat to verify
Who is liable?Identify the person legally liable for the amount.
What property?Identify property belonging to that person.
What amount?Confirm tax, interest or penalty legally payable.
Is there insolvency?Check IBC because Section 82 expressly saves the Code.

6. Tax, interest and penalty

Section 82 expressly covers three categories:

Tax
GST tax legally payable to Government.
Interest
Interest legally payable under the GST law.
Penalty
Penalty legally payable under the Act.
Other amounts?
Do not automatically expand Section 82 beyond its statutory wording. The precise legal basis of each amount should be identified.

This is important because Section 79 uses broader recovery language in places, whereas Section 82 specifically identifies tax, interest and penalty.

7. When does the charge matter?

The practical issue is not simply whether a taxpayer has ever received a GST notice. The finance/legal team should establish whether the relevant amount is legally payable.

A proper chronology should identify:

Liability
How was the amount created?
Determination
Was an order issued where required?
Payment status
How much remains unpaid?
Recovery
Has recovery been initiated?

In a transaction involving property, these dates should be compared with the acquisition, mortgage, sale or other property events.

8. “Notwithstanding anything”

The opening words of Section 82 are an overriding clause. They are intended to give the first-charge provision effect notwithstanding anything contrary contained in another law.

However, the next words are equally important: “save as otherwise provided in the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016.”

Therefore, it is legally inaccurate to describe Section 82 as an unlimited first charge that always defeats every other claim. The statutory IBC exception must be considered.

9. Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code exception

The IBC exception is the most important qualification to Section 82.

Where insolvency proceedings are involved, the priority and distribution of claims are governed by the applicable provisions of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code and the facts of the particular proceeding.

A 2026 appellate decision discussing Section 82 emphasised the significance of the express IBC saving clause and distinguished Section 82 from state VAT provisions that did not contain the same wording.

Do not use Section 82 as a standalone insolvency conclusion. If a company is in CIRP/liquidation or another IBC process, the GST claim must be analysed together with the Code, the insolvency stage, applicable orders and the relevant priority provisions.

10. Priority over other claims

The phrase “first charge” is significant because it is a statutory priority mechanism. But priority disputes can become complex when property is already subject to mortgages, statutory charges, insolvency proceedings, court orders or other competing claims.

11. Banks and secured lenders

A lender should not assume that a mortgage automatically eliminates GST-related statutory risks.

Where a borrower has material GST exposure, lender due diligence can include:

✓ GST registration details
✓ Outstanding demand report
✓ Tax/interest/penalty status
✓ Recovery proceedings
✓ Property title
✓ Existing statutory charges
✓ Mortgage creation date
✓ IBC status
✓ Court/tribunal proceedings
✓ Legal opinion for material exposure

The timing of the security and the statutory position should be reviewed by appropriate legal advisers in a significant financing transaction.

12. Property buyers

A purchaser buying property from a person with substantial GST dues should conduct appropriate due diligence.

The buyer should distinguish between:

  • the seller's title to the property;
  • existing registered encumbrances;
  • statutory tax claims;
  • GST demand/recovery status; and
  • IBC or court proceedings.

Section 82 makes GST due diligence relevant even where the seller produces a conventional title document.

13. Sale of property

Section 82 does not itself say that every sale of charged property is automatically cancelled. The section creates the statutory first-charge framework.

Where a property sale is proposed, the parties should establish whether GST dues exist and how the charge/recovery framework affects the transaction.

Example

A company owes ₹3 crore in GST tax, interest and penalty and owns a commercial building. It proposes to sell the building. The buyer, seller, lender and legal advisers should not treat the transaction as an ordinary property sale without checking the outstanding Government claim and the applicable recovery/charge position.

14. Mortgage and security

A mortgage gives a lender a security interest in property. Where GST dues exist, Section 82 can become important because it creates a statutory first-charge rule.

The review should include:

  • date of mortgage;
  • date of GST liability/demand;
  • amount legally payable;
  • notice/recovery status;
  • IBC status;
  • security enforcement stage; and
  • applicable court/tribunal orders.

The correct priority cannot be determined from the word “mortgage” alone.

15. Section 81 vs Section 82

Section 81Section 82
Transfer of property to be void in certain cases.Tax, interest or penalty becomes a first charge on property.
Targets specified transfers/charges intended to defraud Government revenue.Creates statutory first-charge protection.
Contains a proviso protecting certain genuine transfers.Expressly saves the IBC.
Fraudulent-intent analysis is central.Focus is statutory charge/priority.

Easy memory: Section 81 protects against a deliberate property transfer designed to defeat recovery. Section 82 establishes the Government's first-charge position over property.

16. Section 82 vs Section 83

Section 82Section 83
First charge on property for specified payable amounts.Provisional attachment to protect revenue during specified proceedings.
Operates as a statutory charge.Operates through a provisional attachment order.
Express IBC saving.Has its own statutory conditions and duration.

Do not use the terms “first charge” and “provisional attachment” interchangeably. They are legally different concepts.

17. Section 82 vs Section 79

Section 79 is the principal GST recovery machinery. Section 82 establishes a first-charge position that supports the Government's claim over property.

Liability → payment becomes due → unpaid amount → Section 79 recovery machinery + Section 82 property-charge implications

The actual recovery method depends on the facts and applicable Rules.

18. Section 78 connection

Section 78 provides the payment period after an order before recovery proceedings are initiated, subject to its statutory framework.

For a property owner with a large demand, the finance team should therefore monitor the Section 78 payment window closely and not wait until recovery becomes operational before considering lawful payment options.

19. Section 80 instalments

Section 80 can, subject to its conditions, permit extension of time or payment by monthly instalments for eligible amounts. It is therefore an important alternative to unmanaged default where the liability is eligible.

However, Section 80 specifically excludes liability self-assessed in a return and is subject to Rule 158 restrictions.

For a business with valuable property and a large GST demand, the finance team should consider the available statutory payment mechanisms before allowing the situation to move unnecessarily into recovery.

20. Section 84 appeals/revisions

Section 84 deals with continuation and validation of certain recovery proceedings after an appeal, revision or other proceedings affecting Government dues.

This is relevant to Section 82 analysis because a demand can change after appellate proceedings.

OutcomeGeneral effect
Dues enhancedAdditional demand/recovery can be continued under the statutory framework.
Dues reducedRecovery proceeds for the reduced amount and the taxpayer is informed as prescribed.

Therefore, property-charge analysis should be updated after a material appellate/revisional order.

21. Insolvency practical analysis

When a company enters an IBC process, Section 82 should be read together with the Code rather than treated as an independent answer to priority.

Checklist for a GST claim in insolvency

✓ Date of GST liability
✓ Nature of tax/interest/penalty
✓ Assessment/adjudication order
✓ Proof of claim
✓ CIRP/liquidation stage
✓ Moratorium status
✓ Resolution plan, if relevant
✓ Secured creditor position
✓ Liquidation waterfall, if applicable
✓ NCLT/NCLAT/Supreme Court orders

The express IBC exception in Section 82 means that an insolvency professional should not assume that the phrase “first charge” alone determines the distribution of proceeds.

22. Construction and infrastructure examples

Project land

A construction company owns land and has a large GST demand. Before selling or mortgaging the land, the company should establish the GST demand/recovery position and obtain legal review.

Commercial building

A developer proposes to sell a completed commercial building while GST dues remain unpaid. The buyer's due diligence should include GST recovery exposure, not only title documents.

Heavy machinery

A contractor owns cranes, batching plants and other equipment. Where such assets are relevant to a recovery situation, the finance team should maintain a clear asset register and understand the applicable recovery process.

Bank financing

A road contractor seeks additional working-capital finance against project property while facing a substantial GST demand. The lender should evaluate GST statutory claims together with the security documentation and applicable insolvency law.

Project transfer

A company proposes transferring a project business and its assets to another entity. Section 82 should be considered along with Section 85, recovery provisions and the transaction's legal structure.

23. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Unencumbered property

A company has an unpaid GST demand of ₹2 crore and owns a warehouse. Section 82 can create the statutory first-charge position over the property, subject to the IBC exception and applicable recovery framework.

Example 2 — Bank mortgage already exists

The warehouse is already mortgaged to a bank. The existence of the mortgage does not by itself answer the priority question. Timing, applicable law, GST charge and IBC status must be analysed.

Example 3 — New mortgage after GST demand

A company creates a mortgage after a substantial GST amount becomes payable. The bank should conduct enhanced due diligence before relying solely on the mortgage documentation.

Example 4 — Sale to unrelated buyer

A company sells property for genuine consideration while GST dues are outstanding. The transaction should be examined against the charge and recovery framework, and the buyer should obtain appropriate legal clearance.

Example 5 — Sale to group company

A company sells property to a related entity. The related-party relationship does not itself determine Section 82 priority, but it makes valuation, consideration and transaction purpose especially important.

Example 6 — Demand reduced on appeal

An appellate authority reduces a GST demand from ₹4 crore to ₹1.5 crore. The property/recovery analysis should be updated to reflect the legally effective reduced Government dues.

Example 7 — Insolvency begins

A company enters CIRP after GST dues arise. Section 82's express IBC saving becomes critical; claim treatment must be analysed under the Code and applicable orders.

Example 8 — Section 80 approved

A taxpayer obtains an instalment arrangement for an eligible amount. The finance team should comply with the approved schedule and monitor the remaining liability rather than assuming that the charge issue has disappeared.

Example 9 — Government project contractor

A contractor has government receivables but significant GST dues and owns project equipment. The finance team should model collections, payment obligations and recovery exposure before entering asset transactions.

Example 10 — Property purchaser

A buyer purchases a commercial property from a GST-defaulting company. The buyer should investigate GST dues and recovery status before closing and document the due-diligence process.

Example 11 — Refinancing

A lender refinances an existing loan secured by property. The new lender should review GST claims, existing charges and insolvency status rather than assuming that refinancing resets priority.

Example 12 — Liquidation

A company enters liquidation with GST dues and secured property. The GST claim and asset proceeds must be handled within the applicable IBC framework because Section 82 expressly saves the Code.

27. Accounting and finance perspective

Section 82 is primarily a legal/recovery provision, but it has important finance implications.

  • Maintain a separate GST demand schedule.
  • Reconcile tax, interest and penalty balances.
  • Track property subject to potential recovery exposure.
  • Disclose material contingencies according to applicable accounting standards and professional advice.
  • Coordinate tax, treasury, legal and secretarial teams before major asset transactions.
  • Do not treat a GST charge as an accounting ownership transfer.
Management point: A large GST demand can affect borrowing, asset sales, project financing and insolvency strategy even before physical recovery action takes place.

28. Common mistakes

  • Assuming Section 82 means Government automatically owns the property.
  • Ignoring the express IBC exception.
  • Assuming every GST notice creates the same property-charge consequence.
  • Ignoring interest and penalty while assessing exposure.
  • Failing to update the demand after an appellate order.
  • Assuming a bank mortgage automatically defeats GST priority.
  • Assuming GST always defeats every secured creditor in every insolvency situation.
  • Confusing Section 82 with Section 81.
  • Confusing Section 82 with provisional attachment under Section 83.
  • Allowing property transactions without GST due diligence.
  • Failing to coordinate GST and insolvency professionals.

30. Judicial development and current legal caution

Section 82 has become particularly important in disputes involving statutory priority, secured creditors and insolvency.

A 30 June 2026 appellate decision specifically analysed the words “save as otherwise provided in the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016” and treated that saving clause as materially significant when comparing Section 82 with a state VAT first-charge provision that lacked the same wording.

This is an important current-law caution: older discussions of “tax first charge” cannot automatically be applied to CGST Section 82 without considering its express IBC exception and subsequent Supreme Court/appellate jurisprudence.

For insolvency cases: Do not rely on a generic statement that “GST is first charge”. Determine the applicable IBC provisions, stage of insolvency, nature of the GST claim, security position and relevant judicial orders.

31. Section 82 GST FAQs

What is Section 82 of the CGST Act?

Section 82 provides that, subject to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code exception, GST tax, interest or penalty payable by a person is a first charge on that person's property.

What does first charge mean in GST?

It means the CGST Act creates a statutory priority claim over the relevant property for the specified Government dues.

Does Section 82 transfer ownership to Government?

No. A first charge is not the same as ownership. Recovery and enforcement are governed by the applicable recovery framework.

Does Section 82 cover interest?

Yes. Interest is expressly included in the statutory wording.

Does Section 82 cover penalty?

Yes. Penalty is expressly included.

Does Section 82 cover tax?

Yes. Tax is expressly included.

Does Section 82 apply to land and buildings?

Property is covered by the section. The precise enforcement and priority implications depend on the facts and applicable law.

Does GST have first charge over property?

Section 82 creates a first-charge framework, but it expressly saves situations governed by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code.

Can GST priority defeat a bank mortgage?

The answer cannot be given from Section 82 alone. The timing, nature of security, applicable law, recovery status and especially any IBC proceedings must be examined.

What is the difference between Section 81 and 82?

Section 81 deals with specified fraudulent property transfers/charges intended to defeat Government revenue. Section 82 establishes a statutory first charge.

What is the difference between Section 82 and Section 83?

Section 82 concerns first charge; Section 83 concerns provisional attachment during specified proceedings.

What is the difference between Section 82 and Section 79?

Section 79 contains the GST recovery machinery, while Section 82 establishes the statutory first-charge position over property.

Does Section 82 apply during insolvency?

Section 82 expressly says “save as otherwise provided” in the IBC. Therefore, insolvency claims must be analysed under the Code and applicable orders.

Can a company sell property when GST dues exist?

The existence of GST dues makes due diligence important. The effect of a particular sale depends on the charge, recovery status, transaction facts and applicable law.

Should a property buyer check GST dues?

For material transactions involving a taxpayer with significant GST exposure, appropriate GST and recovery due diligence is prudent.

Should banks check GST before lending against property?

Yes, where material GST exposure exists, lenders should consider GST dues, recovery, statutory charges and insolvency status as part of appropriate legal due diligence.

Does Section 82 itself provide the auction procedure?

No. Section 82 establishes the first-charge principle. Detailed recovery methods are dealt with through the recovery provisions and Rules.

Can Section 80 instalments remove the Section 82 issue?

An approved instalment arrangement changes the payment position according to its terms, but the underlying liability and applicable legal consequences should be monitored until fully resolved.

33. Key takeaways

  • Section 82 creates a statutory first charge on property for GST tax, interest and penalty payable to Government.
  • The charge is not the same as Government ownership of the property.
  • The provision begins with an overriding clause, but expressly saves the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code.
  • IBC can therefore materially affect priority and distribution in insolvency.
  • Section 82 is different from Section 81's fraudulent-transfer rule and Section 83's provisional attachment.
  • Section 79 provides the broader recovery machinery.
  • Banks, buyers and businesses should conduct GST due diligence for material property transactions where GST exposure exists.
  • Appeals and revisions can change the amount legally payable, so the property/recovery analysis should be updated.
  • Construction and infrastructure businesses should include GST recovery exposure in major asset-sale, financing and project-transfer decisions.
  • In insolvency, never rely on the phrase “GST is first charge” without analysing the IBC and current judicial position.
