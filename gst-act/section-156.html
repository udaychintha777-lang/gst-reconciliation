Section 156 of the CGST Act, 2017

Persons deemed to be public servants. Understand what this provision means for persons discharging functions under GST, why it refers to public-servant status, and how Section 156 fits into the wider framework of GST administration, confidentiality and accountability.

Section 156 at a Glance

Who?All persons discharging functions under the CGST Act.
StatusThey are deemed to be public servants for the statutory purpose stated in Section 156.
ReferenceThe provision refers to section 21 of the Indian Penal Code.
In simple language: Section 156 gives persons who discharge functions under the CGST Act the legal status of public servants for the purpose specified by the provision. It is an important status provision within the GST administration framework, but it should not be read as saying that every person connected with a GST business is a public servant.

1. Statutory Text of Section 156

156. Persons deemed to be public servants.—

All persons discharging functions under this Act shall be deemed to be public servants within the meaning of section 21 of the Indian Penal Code.
Current-law position: Current India Code and CBIC materials reproduce Section 156 in this form. The provision has been in force from 1 July 2017.

2. Section 156 Explained in Simple Language

GST administration involves officers and other persons performing statutory functions under the CGST Act. Section 156 establishes that all persons discharging functions under the Act are deemed to be public servants within the meaning of the statutory reference contained in the section.

The provision is therefore about the legal status attached to persons performing GST functions. It is not a provision that creates a new GST tax, penalty or registration obligation for taxpayers.

Simple example: A person performing a function under the CGST Act in an official capacity falls within the statutory public-servant deeming provision. The section is concerned with the person's statutory function, not with whether the person works for a private business that merely deals with GST.

3. Who Is Covered?

The wording is deliberately broad: “All persons discharging functions under this Act”.

CategoryHow Section 156 should be understood
GST officers performing statutory functionsClearly within the ordinary scope of the provision.
Other persons formally discharging functions under the ActThe wording is not limited only to one particular designation; the relevant question is whether the person is discharging functions under the Act.
Taxpayer / company employeeMerely complying with GST does not make a taxpayer or private employee a public servant under Section 156.
Private professional advising a taxpayerProviding private professional advice does not by itself mean the person is discharging functions under the CGST Act for Section 156.
Important: “All persons” must be read together with the words “discharging functions under this Act.” The provision is not a declaration that every person who interacts with GST is a public servant.

4. What Does “Discharging Functions Under This Act” Mean?

The key connection is the performance of functions under the CGST Act.

Examples of statutory GST functions can include exercising powers of scrutiny, inquiry, investigation, assessment, adjudication, inspection, issuing statutory communications, processing applications or performing other duties assigned under the Act.

ActivityWhy it may fall within the concept
Scrutiny of returnsPerformed as part of statutory GST administration.
Assessment / adjudicationInvolves statutory decision-making under GST law.
InvestigationExercises statutory investigative functions.
Inspection/search-related functionsPerformed under powers provided by the Act, subject to their statutory conditions.
Processing statutory applicationsMay constitute an official function under the Act depending on the role and authority.
The exact status of a particular individual should be determined from the person's statutory appointment, authorisation and function. Section 156 should not be used to assume public-servant status merely from a job title.

5. What Does “Deemed to Be a Public Servant” Mean?

The expression “deemed to be” is a legal deeming mechanism. The Act does not merely describe the person as an ordinary government employee; it directs that persons covered by the provision are treated as public servants within the statutory framework referred to by Section 156.

This matters because public-servant status can carry legal consequences under laws dealing with public servants, including laws concerning official conduct and offences involving public servants, subject to the exact requirements of the relevant law.

Important distinction: Section 156 establishes a statutory status. It does not by itself create an independent offence, penalty or prosecution against a taxpayer.

6. Reference to Section 21 of the Indian Penal Code

Section 156 expressly refers to section 21 of the Indian Penal Code. The original CGST text uses this reference to identify the statutory concept of “public servant.”

For historical legal reading, the CGST Act was enacted in 2017 and Section 156 has been reproduced with the IPC section 21 reference. Current consolidated sources continue to show that wording.

Legal-reference caution: The Indian criminal-law framework has undergone statutory changes in recent years. When analysing a current criminal-law consequence, do not rely on Section 156 alone or assume that the 2017 cross-reference answers every present-day criminal-law question. Check the criminal law applicable to the relevant date and conduct.

8. Accountability and Official Functions

Public-servant status should not be understood as unlimited immunity or unlimited power. GST officers and other persons exercising statutory functions remain subject to the governing law and the conditions attached to the relevant statutory power.

For example, a power of inspection, search, summons, assessment or adjudication must be exercised under the applicable provision and procedure. Section 156 does not enlarge each individual power merely because the person has public-servant status.

Why this distinction matters

  • Status answers who is treated as a public servant.
  • Power comes from the specific GST provision conferring that power.
  • Procedure comes from the Act, Rules, notifications, circulars and applicable legal principles.
  • Accountability depends on the relevant law and the person's conduct.

9. Connection with Confidentiality and Disclosure

Section 156 sits immediately before Section 157 and Section 158 in Chapter XXI. Section 158 separately deals with disclosure of information by a public servant. These provisions should therefore be read as part of a wider statutory framework.

ProvisionBroad subjectPractical relevance
Section 156Persons deemed to be public servants.Defines statutory status for persons discharging GST functions.
Section 157Protection of action taken under the Act.Deals with specified protection for acts done or intended to be done in good faith.
Section 158Disclosure of information by a public servant.Provides rules and exceptions relating to disclosure of GST information.
Section 159Publication of information in respect of persons in certain cases.Deals with a separate publication power.
Key point: Section 156 does not itself set out the confidentiality rules. For disclosure questions, the relevant provisions such as Section 158 and Section 159 must be examined separately.

10. Section 156 vs Section 157

These two sections are frequently confused because they appear next to each other.

Section 156Section 157
Persons deemed to be public servants.Protection of action taken under the Act.
Deals primarily with statutory status.Deals with specified protection against suits, prosecution or other legal proceedings for acts done or intended to be done in good faith, subject to the statutory wording.
Contains one principal deeming provision.Contains separate provisions for specified persons and acts.
Do not treat Section 156 as a blanket immunity provision. The protection in Section 157 has its own wording and conditions and must be analysed separately.

11. Detailed Practical Examples

Example 1 – GST officer conducting scrutiny

An officer examines a registered person's return under the statutory GST framework. The officer is discharging a function under the Act and falls within the public-servant deeming provision.

Example 2 – Investigation

A competent GST officer performs an investigation under the Act. Section 156 applies to the person's covered statutory function; it does not itself create the investigative power.

Example 3 – Taxpayer's accountant

A company's accountant prepares GST returns and reconciliations for the company. The accountant does not become a public servant merely because the work relates to GST.

Example 4 – Chartered Accountant advising a client

A CA provides private professional advice to a taxpayer. Private advisory work does not, by itself, bring the person within Section 156.

Example 5 – Statutory function vs job title

A person's designation alone should not be used to determine Section 156 status. The relevant question is whether the person is discharging functions under the CGST Act.

Example 6 – Search-related function

Where an authorised officer performs a search-related function under the CGST Act, the officer's statutory function is part of the GST administration framework. Section 156 addresses public-servant status, while the search power comes from the applicable provision.

Example 7 – Confidential information

A person performing GST functions may handle taxpayer information. The confidentiality and disclosure consequences are governed by the relevant provisions, including Section 158, rather than by Section 156 alone.

Example 8 – Good-faith protection

An officer believes a statutory action is required and acts under the applicable provision. Whether legal protection applies is a separate Section 157 question and depends on its conditions; Section 156 alone does not answer it.

Example 9 – Private consultant assisting department

A private specialist may assist a department in a technical matter. Whether that person is covered by Section 156 depends on whether the person is actually discharging functions under the Act within the statutory framework; private employment or consultancy alone is not enough.

Example 10 – GST practitioner

A GST practitioner assisting taxpayers with filings does not become a public servant merely because the person works within the GST compliance system.

Example 11 – Adjudication

An officer conducts statutory adjudication. The adjudicatory function is performed under GST law, while the person's public-servant status follows from Section 156.

Example 12 – Taxpayer dealing with an officer

A company receiving a statutory GST communication should identify the issuing authority and legal provision. Section 156 does not eliminate the need to check whether the particular action is authorised under the relevant GST provision.

12. What Section 156 Means for Taxpayers

For taxpayers, Section 156 is mainly an administrative/legal-status provision. It does not impose a new filing, payment or reconciliation obligation.

Practical implications

  • Identify the statutory authority behind a GST notice or proceeding.
  • Keep communications and submissions properly documented.
  • Respond through the applicable statutory process.
  • Do not assume that Section 156 itself gives an officer a particular power; identify the specific enabling provision.
  • For confidentiality concerns, examine Section 158 and other applicable provisions.
  • For legal protection of official acts, examine Section 157 separately.
Practical rule: When a GST authority takes an action, ask two separate questions: “Who is the officer/person?” and “What provision authorises this particular action?” Section 156 primarily addresses the first question.

15. Common Misunderstandings

Misunderstanding 1 – Everyone dealing with GST is a public servant

No. The statutory wording is tied to persons discharging functions under the CGST Act.

Misunderstanding 2 – Section 156 gives unlimited power to officers

No. The specific statutory power for an action must come from the relevant GST provision.

Misunderstanding 3 – Section 156 is a protection from prosecution

Section 156 concerns deemed public-servant status. Section 157 separately deals with specified protection for good-faith acts.

Misunderstanding 4 – Section 156 itself imposes a taxpayer penalty

No. It is a status provision and does not itself prescribe a taxpayer penalty.

Misunderstanding 5 – A private CA or accountant becomes a public servant

Private professional work for a taxpayer does not automatically fall within Section 156.

Misunderstanding 6 – Public-servant status means every action is valid

No. The legality of an action depends on the specific statutory authority and applicable procedure.

Misunderstanding 7 – Section 156 alone answers criminal-law consequences today

The section contains a reference to IPC section 21. Current criminal-law consequences should be checked against the criminal law applicable to the relevant date.

18. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 156 of the CGST Act?

Section 156 provides that all persons discharging functions under the CGST Act shall be deemed to be public servants within the meaning of section 21 of the Indian Penal Code.

2. Are GST officers public servants?

Persons discharging functions under the CGST Act are covered by the statutory deeming provision in Section 156.

3. Does Section 156 apply to every person working in a company?

No. A private employee does not become a public servant merely because the employee handles GST compliance.

4. Is a GST practitioner a public servant?

Not merely because the person is a GST practitioner. Section 156 turns on discharging functions under the CGST Act, not simply on assisting taxpayers with GST compliance.

5. Is a Chartered Accountant automatically a public servant under Section 156?

No. A CA providing private professional services does not become a public servant merely because the work relates to GST.

6. What does “deemed to be” mean?

It is a statutory deeming mechanism that treats persons within the provision as public servants for the purpose specified by the law.

7. Does Section 156 give an officer power to conduct a search?

No. The specific search power must come from the applicable provision, such as Section 67 where its conditions are met. Section 156 addresses public-servant status.

8. Does Section 156 provide immunity to GST officers?

No blanket immunity follows from Section 156. Section 157 separately contains specified protection for certain good-faith acts and must be read on its own terms.

9. What is the connection between Section 156 and Section 157?

Section 156 concerns persons deemed to be public servants, while Section 157 concerns protection of specified actions taken under the Act.

10. What is the connection between Section 156 and Section 158?

Section 158 deals separately with disclosure of information by a public servant. The provisions form part of the wider miscellaneous framework but perform different functions.

11. Does Section 156 impose any tax on taxpayers?

No. It is a legal-status provision and does not itself impose a tax.

12. Does public-servant status make every GST action valid?

No. Each action must be supported by the relevant statutory power and applicable procedure.

13. Does Section 156 apply to persons assisting the department?

The statutory wording focuses on persons discharging functions under the Act. Whether a particular assisting person falls within the provision depends on the person's actual statutory function and authority.

14. Is Section 156 still in force?

Yes. Current India Code and CBIC references continue to show Section 156 in force.

15. When did Section 156 come into force?

Section 156 came into force on 1 July 2017.

16. Why does Section 156 mention IPC section 21?

The CGST Act uses section 21 of the Indian Penal Code as the statutory reference for the concept of public servant in Section 156.

17. Should current criminal-law questions be answered only from Section 156?

No. Because Section 156 contains a criminal-law cross-reference, the criminal law applicable to the relevant date and conduct should also be checked.

18. What should a taxpayer check when dealing with a GST officer?

Identify the officer, the proceeding, the specific statutory provision authorising the action, applicable procedural requirements and the relevant response deadline.
