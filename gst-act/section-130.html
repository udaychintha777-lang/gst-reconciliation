Section 130 of the CGST Act, 2017 – Confiscation of Goods or Conveyances and Levy of Penalty

A detailed, practical and easy-to-understand guide to GST confiscation proceedings: the five situations in Section 130(1), the importance of intent to evade tax, unaccounted goods, unregistered supplies, conveyance liability, redemption fine, penalty, hearing, government ownership and disposal of confiscated goods or vehicles.

At a glance

What is Section 130?

In simple language: Section 130 deals with cases in which goods or a conveyance become liable to confiscation because specified serious GST contraventions exist. It also makes the person liable to penalty under Section 122. Where confiscation is authorised, the adjudicating officer gives the owner an option to pay a fine in lieu of confiscation, subject to the statutory limits and conditions.

Section 130 is therefore different from an ordinary tax-demand provision. It deals with a more serious consequence involving the potential transfer of ownership of goods or a conveyance to the Government.

The most important words in the section are not merely “confiscation.” In several clauses, the law requires a connection with intent to evade payment of tax. The exact facts, evidence, documents and conduct therefore become extremely important.

Important: Section 130 should not be treated as an automatic consequence of every e-way bill error or every detention under Section 129. The current Section 129 and Section 130 are separate statutory mechanisms, and the 2022 amendment materially changed their relationship.

Law explained

1. Current statutory text of Section 130

Section 130 – Confiscation of goods or conveyances and levy of penalty.

(1) Where any person—

(i) supplies or receives any goods in contravention of any of the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder with intent to evade payment of tax; or

(ii) does not account for any goods on which he is liable to pay tax under this Act; or

(iii) supplies any goods liable to tax under this Act without having applied for registration; or

(iv) contravenes any of the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder with intent to evade payment of tax; or

(v) uses any conveyance as a means of transport for carriage of goods in contravention of the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder unless the owner of the conveyance proves that it was so used without the knowledge or connivance of the owner himself, his agent, if any, and the person in charge of the conveyance,

then, all such goods or conveyances shall be liable to confiscation and the person shall be liable to penalty under section 122.

(2) Whenever confiscation of any goods or conveyance is authorised by this Act, the officer adjudging it shall give to the owner of the goods an option to pay in lieu of confiscation, such fine as the said officer thinks fit:

Provided that such fine shall not exceed the market value of the goods confiscated, less the tax chargeable thereon.

Provided further that the aggregate of such fine and penalty leviable shall not be less than the penalty equal to one hundred per cent. of the tax payable on such goods.

Provided also that where any such conveyance is used for the carriage of the goods or passengers for hire, the owner of the conveyance shall be given an option to pay in lieu of confiscation of the conveyance a fine equal to the tax payable on the goods being transported thereon.

(3) Omitted with effect from 1 January 2022.

(4) No order for confiscation of goods or conveyance or for imposition of penalty shall be issued without giving the person an opportunity of being heard.

(5) Where any goods or conveyance are confiscated under this Act, the title of such goods or conveyance shall thereupon vest in the Government.

(6) The proper officer adjudging confiscation shall take and hold possession of the things confiscated and every officer of Police, on the requisition of such proper officer, shall assist him in taking and holding such possession.

(7) The proper officer may, after satisfying himself that the confiscated goods or conveyance are not required in any other proceedings under this Act and after giving reasonable time not exceeding three months to pay fine in lieu of confiscation, dispose of such goods or conveyance and deposit the sale proceeds thereof with the Government.

The current India Code text reflects the 2022 changes: sub-section (1) no longer begins with the earlier “notwithstanding anything contained in this Act” wording, the old sub-section (3) has been omitted, and the former Section 129 linkage has been removed.

2. Section 130 explained in very simple language

Confiscation is more serious than simply asking a taxpayer to pay tax, interest or penalty. If goods or a vehicle are confiscated under the GST Act, the title to the confiscated goods or conveyance vests in the Government under Section 130(5).

However, the law provides an important route for the owner: when confiscation is authorised, the adjudicating officer must give the owner an option to pay a fine in lieu of confiscation. In ordinary language, this means the owner can retain the goods or conveyance by paying the prescribed/adjudicated redemption fine instead of losing ownership through confiscation, subject to the statutory limits and procedure.

Tax consequence

The underlying tax position must be identified according to the applicable provisions.

Penalty consequence

The person becomes liable to penalty under Section 122 where Section 130(1) applies.

Confiscation consequence

Goods or conveyance may become liable to confiscation.

Redemption option

The owner is given an option to pay fine in lieu of confiscation, subject to statutory limits.

3. The five grounds under Section 130(1)

Section 130(1) identifies five circumstances. Understanding each separately is essential.

ClauseWhat it coversKey feature
(i)Supplying or receiving goods in contravention of the Act/Rules with intent to evade tax.There must be a contravention plus the specified intent to evade tax.
(ii)Failure to account for goods on which the person is liable to pay tax.The goods are not properly accounted for.
(iii)Supplying taxable goods without having applied for registration.Unregistered supply of goods liable to tax.
(iv)Contravention of the Act/Rules with intent to evade tax.Again, intent to evade tax is expressly part of the clause.
(v)Use of a conveyance to carry goods in contravention of the Act/Rules.The conveyance owner gets a specific defence if the owner proves lack of knowledge or connivance by the owner, agent and person in charge.

Notice that clauses (i) and (iv) expressly contain the words “with intent to evade payment of tax.” A serious allegation under these clauses therefore requires attention to the evidence supporting the alleged intent.

4. What does “intent to evade payment of tax” mean in practical terms?

The phrase is important because Section 130 does not make every contravention automatically confiscable under clauses (i) and (iv). The statute connects the contravention with an intent to evade tax.

In a real proceeding, the department may rely on a combination of facts and documents to allege intent. The taxpayer, in turn, can explain the commercial transaction, accounting records, GST returns, payment trail, movement documents, correspondence and any error or omission that occurred.

Evidence that may become relevant

  • Whether the goods actually existed and were delivered.
  • Whether the transaction appears in the books.
  • Whether tax invoices were issued.
  • Whether tax was charged/paid as applicable.
  • Whether returns contain the relevant transaction.
  • Whether the supplier/recipient identities are genuine.
  • Whether there is a pattern of repeated unexplained discrepancies.
  • Whether documents were created or altered after interception.
  • Whether the movement and commercial purpose can be independently established.
  • Whether the explanation is consistent across invoice, e-way bill, books, transport records and statements.
Practical point: “There was an error” is not always enough. The taxpayer should explain what the error was, how it happened, whether the transaction was genuine, what tax treatment occurred, and what documents prove the explanation.

5. Clause (ii) – goods not accounted for

Clause (ii) applies where a person does not account for goods on which the person is liable to pay tax.

For a business, this makes inventory controls particularly important. If physical stock, purchase records, production records, sales records and GST records do not reconcile, the business may face difficult questions during investigation.

Example

A manufacturing company has physical finished goods worth ₹50 lakh. Its books and stock records cannot explain a substantial quantity of those goods. If the goods are taxable and the person is liable to pay tax on them, the department may examine whether clause (ii) applies.

Preventive control

Perform regular physical-vs-book stock reconciliation.

Evidence

Maintain GRNs, production records, stock registers, sales invoices and dispatch records.

6. Clause (iii) – taxable goods supplied without applying for registration

Clause (iii) covers a person who supplies goods liable to tax without having applied for registration.

This is different from an ordinary case where a registered taxpayer makes a documentation error. The clause focuses on a taxable supply made by a person who has not applied for registration.

Example

A person carries on a taxable goods business in circumstances where GST registration is required, but continues supplying taxable goods without having applied for registration. If the facts satisfy Section 130(1)(iii), confiscation consequences can arise in addition to the applicable penalty framework.

Important: Registration liability itself depends on the applicable law, threshold, nature of supply and other statutory conditions. Section 130 should not be applied merely because a person has no GSTIN without first examining whether registration was legally required.

7. Clause (v) – when can a vehicle/conveyance be confiscated?

Clause (v) addresses a conveyance used to transport goods in contravention of the Act or Rules.

But the section contains an important protection for the owner of the conveyance. The owner can avoid confiscation under this clause if the owner proves that the conveyance was used in the contravention without the knowledge or connivance of:

  • the owner himself;
  • the owner's agent, if any; and
  • the person in charge of the conveyance.

Why this matters to transport companies

A transport company may carry goods for many customers. It may not own or control the underlying goods. Therefore, transport documentation, customer instructions, driver records, trip sheets and contractual terms can become important evidence.

Practical example: A logistics company proves that it received genuine transport instructions, the driver followed the documents supplied, the company had no knowledge of the alleged contravention and there was no connivance by the owner, agent or person in charge. That evidence can be relevant to the defence contemplated by Section 130(1)(v).

8. What is the penalty under Section 130?

Section 130(1) says that where the specified circumstances exist, the goods/conveyance are liable to confiscation and the person is liable to penalty under Section 122.

This is important because Section 130 itself is not a standalone universal penalty amount. The penalty consequence is connected to Section 122.

The applicable clause of Section 122, tax period, facts and circumstances must therefore be identified rather than simply assuming one fixed penalty figure for every Section 130 case.

Do not confuse: the penalty under Section 122 and the fine in lieu of confiscation under Section 130(2) are separate components of the confiscation framework.

9. Fine in lieu of confiscation – what is redemption fine?

When confiscation is authorised, Section 130(2) requires the adjudicating officer to give the owner of the goods an option to pay a fine in lieu of confiscation.

This is commonly referred to as a redemption fine. It is not simply another name for GST tax or penalty.

Maximum limit for goods

Maximum fine = Market value of confiscated goods − tax chargeable thereon

The statute says the fine cannot exceed this amount.

Example

Suppose the market value of confiscated goods is ₹20,00,000 and tax chargeable is ₹3,60,000. The statutory ceiling for the fine is ₹16,40,000.

This is a maximum ceiling, not an instruction that the officer must always impose exactly ₹16,40,000. Section 130(2) says the officer gives an option to pay “such fine as the said officer thinks fit,” subject to the statutory limit and the other provisos.

10. Minimum aggregate of fine and penalty

Section 130(2) contains another important safeguard/limit: the aggregate of the fine and penalty leviable shall not be less than a penalty equal to 100% of the tax payable on the goods.

Fine + penalty  ≥  100% of tax payable on the goods

This does not mean that the fine itself must always equal 100% of tax. The wording concerns the aggregate of fine and penalty.

Illustrative example

Tax payable on goods = ₹2,00,000. Suppose the applicable penalty under Section 122 is ₹1,50,000. The aggregate of fine and penalty cannot be below ₹2,00,000. Therefore, the fine component would need to be sufficient to meet the statutory minimum, subject to the maximum fine ceiling.

Calculation caution: The actual Section 122 penalty must first be determined from the applicable clause and facts. Do not automatically insert an assumed penalty merely to calculate the redemption fine.

11. Special rule for a conveyance used for carriage for hire

Where a confiscated conveyance is used for carriage of goods or passengers for hire, Section 130(2) gives the owner of the conveyance an option to pay, in lieu of confiscation of the conveyance, a fine equal to the tax payable on the goods being transported on it.

This is separate from the maximum-fine formula for the goods themselves.

Example

A transporter's truck is proposed for confiscation under Section 130(1)(v). The goods being carried have tax payable of ₹4,00,000. Where the statutory conditions for the special conveyance provision apply, the conveyance owner is given an option to pay a fine equal to ₹4,00,000 in lieu of confiscation of the conveyance.

Transporter control: Transport businesses should maintain customer declarations, booking records, driver instructions, vehicle logs and contractual protections because evidence concerning knowledge and connivance can become important under Section 130(1)(v).

12. Is a hearing mandatory before confiscation?

Yes. Section 130(4) expressly states that no order for confiscation of goods or conveyance, or for imposition of penalty, shall be issued without giving the person an opportunity of being heard.

This is a critical procedural protection because confiscation can have consequences far beyond a routine tax adjustment. The person should be given an opportunity to explain the facts, documents, ownership, transaction, tax position and circumstances surrounding the alleged contravention.

A practical hearing file should contain

  • Notice and all annexures.
  • Invoice and underlying commercial documents.
  • E-way bill and transport records.
  • Books of account and stock records.
  • GST returns and tax payment evidence.
  • Purchase/sales agreements.
  • Bank/payment evidence where relevant.
  • Transporter/driver records.
  • Explanation for any discrepancy.
  • Evidence addressing any allegation of intent to evade tax.

13. What happens to ownership after confiscation?

Section 130(5) is very significant: when goods or a conveyance are confiscated under the Act, the title to those goods or conveyance vests in the Government.

In simple terms, confiscation is not merely a temporary detention. Once confiscation legally occurs, ownership changes according to the statute.

Detention

Goods/conveyance are held under the applicable enforcement process.

Confiscation

The statutory consequence is much more serious; title vests in the Government.

Fine in lieu

The owner is given an option to pay the redemption fine instead of confiscation, subject to the law.

Disposal

If the statutory conditions are met and fine is not paid within the allowed period, the officer may dispose of the confiscated goods/conveyance.

14. Who takes possession of confiscated goods or vehicles?

Section 130(6) provides that the proper officer adjudging confiscation shall take and hold possession of the confiscated goods or conveyance.

It further provides that every police officer shall, on the requisition of the proper officer, assist in taking and holding possession.

This shows why confiscation is fundamentally different from a normal tax demand. There is a physical-property consequence and a formal possession mechanism.

15. What happens after confiscation?

Section 130(7) allows the proper officer, after satisfying himself that the confiscated goods or conveyance are not required in any other proceedings under the Act, and after giving reasonable time not exceeding three months to pay the fine in lieu of confiscation, to dispose of the goods or conveyance and deposit the sale proceeds with the Government.

Why this provision matters

  • The officer must consider whether the property is required in another proceeding.
  • The owner gets reasonable time to pay the fine in lieu of confiscation, subject to the maximum three-month period stated in the provision.
  • If the property is disposed of, the sale proceeds are deposited with the Government.
Urgency: Once confiscation has been ordered, a business should not delay its legal response. The consequences can progress from physical possession to disposal of the property.

The Finance Act, 2021 changes effective from 1 January 2022 removed the old Section 129-to-Section 130 progression language and omitted Section 130(3). The current India Code and CBIC amendment material reflect this.

17. Why the 2022 amendment matters

Older GST articles can be confusing because they describe the original Section 129/130 structure. Before the 2022 changes, Section 129 contained language under which failure to pay the amount within the specified period led to further proceedings under Section 130.

With effect from 1 January 2022, the relevant Section 129 wording was replaced. The current Section 129(6) deals with sale/disposal of detained or seized goods/conveyance to recover the penalty after the specified post-order period. Section 130 was correspondingly amended and its sub-section (3) was omitted.

Public-friendly takeaway: If an online article says “non-payment of Section 129 amount within 14 days automatically starts Section 130,” check its date. That describes the earlier framework, not the current wording of Section 129(6).

18. Practical Section 130 procedure – from notice to disposal

  1. Department identifies the alleged Section 130 ground. The exact clause—(i), (ii), (iii), (iv) or (v)—should be identified.
  2. Evidence is examined. This can include invoices, e-way bills, stock records, books, transport documents and statements.
  3. Tax and penalty position is determined. The Section 122 penalty consequence is considered.
  4. Confiscation is adjudicated. The authority determines whether the statutory conditions are satisfied.
  5. Opportunity of hearing is provided. Section 130(4) requires a hearing before confiscation/penalty order.
  6. Fine in lieu is offered. Where confiscation is authorised, the owner receives the statutory option to pay fine instead of confiscation.
  7. Fine limits are applied. The goods-fine ceiling and aggregate minimum must be respected; conveyances used for hire have their special rule.
  8. If confiscation occurs, title vests in Government.
  9. Officer takes possession. Police assistance may be requisitioned.
  10. Reasonable time to pay fine is allowed. Under Section 130(7), the time does not exceed three months.
  11. Property may be disposed of. This can happen after the officer is satisfied it is not required in another proceeding and the statutory conditions are met.

19. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 – Supply with alleged intent to evade tax

A business transports taxable goods using documents that allegedly conceal the true nature/value of the supply. The department alleges a contravention with intent to evade tax. If the evidence satisfies Section 130(1)(i), the goods can become liable to confiscation and the person can become liable to penalty under Section 122.

Example 2 – Genuine transaction but document mismatch

A company sells genuine goods and has accounted for the transaction, but an e-way bill contains a discrepancy. The company should not automatically assume that Section 130 applies. It should demonstrate the genuine transaction, tax accounting, documents and circumstances and address whether the statutory conditions for confiscation are actually satisfied.

Example 3 – Unaccounted inventory

A warehouse contains taxable goods that the business cannot reconcile to its books or stock records. The department may examine Section 130(1)(ii). The company should immediately prepare a physical-to-book reconciliation and identify whether the goods are purchased, produced, returned, transferred, job-work material or otherwise explainable.

Example 4 – Unregistered taxable supply

A person makes taxable supplies of goods without applying for registration where registration is legally required. Clause (iii) can become relevant. The person should examine registration liability and all applicable facts.

Example 5 – Conveyance owner has no knowledge

A transporter carries a customer's goods. The customer allegedly violates GST law. The transporter produces the transport contract, customer instructions, invoice/e-way bill supplied by the customer, driver records and evidence showing no knowledge or connivance. This evidence can be relevant to the defence in Section 130(1)(v).

Example 6 – Market value and tax

Market value of goods = ₹30 lakh. Tax chargeable = ₹5.4 lakh. The maximum fine for the goods under the first proviso to Section 130(2) is ₹24.6 lakh.

Example 7 – Aggregate minimum

Tax payable = ₹4 lakh. If the applicable Section 122 penalty is ₹2.5 lakh, the aggregate of fine and penalty cannot be below ₹4 lakh. Subject to the statutory maximum, at least ₹1.5 lakh of fine would be needed to reach the minimum aggregate.

Example 8 – Fine is not the same as penalty

A company may have both a Section 122 penalty and a redemption fine. Paying one component does not automatically mean the other component is satisfied.

Example 9 – Conveyance used for hire

A truck used for commercial carriage is proposed for confiscation. Tax payable on the goods being transported is ₹3 lakh. The conveyance owner receives the statutory option to pay a fine equal to ₹3 lakh in lieu of confiscation of the conveyance, where the provision applies.

Example 10 – Confiscation changes ownership

Once goods are actually confiscated under Section 130, Section 130(5) says title vests in the Government. This is why the fine-in-lieu option is commercially important.

Example 11 – Three-month window

After confiscation, the officer may give reasonable time not exceeding three months to pay the fine in lieu of confiscation. If the conditions of Section 130(7) are met and the fine is not paid, disposal can follow.

Example 12 – Goods needed in another proceeding

Section 130(7) requires the proper officer to satisfy himself that confiscated goods or conveyance are not required in any other proceeding under the Act before disposal. Therefore, disposal is not simply an immediate step after confiscation.

Example 13 – High-value project material

A construction company moves expensive machinery/material to a project site and the department alleges a serious GST contravention. The company should immediately involve its finance, tax, legal, logistics and project teams because the commercial consequences of confiscation can be substantial.

Example 14 – Multiple parties

In a supply chain there may be a supplier, buyer, transporter, driver and vehicle owner. Their roles should not be merged into one. Each person's statutory position and evidence should be separately examined.

Example 15 – Repeated discrepancies

If a business repeatedly moves goods with similar unexplained documentation discrepancies, the department may examine the overall facts more closely. The business should perform a root-cause review rather than treating every incident as isolated.

Example 16 – Physical stock versus ERP

An ERP migration causes old inventory to be recorded incorrectly. Physical stock appears higher than the new ERP balance. The business should preserve the migration files, opening balances, old ERP reports and reconciliation so it can explain the discrepancy rather than allowing it to appear as unexplained stock.

20. How to respond to a Section 130 notice

A Section 130 proceeding requires a much more detailed response than a simple payment confirmation. A useful reply should be structured around the exact statutory clause.

1. Case detailsGSTIN, notice number, date, officer, goods, vehicle and relevant tax period.
2. Exact allegationIdentify whether the department relies on clause (i), (ii), (iii), (iv) or (v).
3. Factual chronologyOrder date, dispatch, invoice, e-way bill, transport, interception and subsequent events.
4. Transaction evidenceInvoice, purchase/sales order, books, bank records and delivery proof.
5. GST evidenceReturns, tax payment, e-way bill and relevant GST records.
6. Intent issueWhere applicable, address specifically why the evidence does or does not establish intent to evade tax.

Best practice: Address the exact allegation rather than replying only that “there was no intention to evade tax.” Show the commercial transaction, accounting trail, GST compliance and documentary evidence that supports the explanation.

22. Internal controls to reduce Section 130 risk

  • Maintain complete invoice-to-e-way-bill matching before dispatch.
  • Reconcile physical inventory with ERP and books regularly.
  • Keep separate records for stock transfers, job work, returns and project-site movements.
  • Ensure registration status is reviewed before commencing taxable supplies.
  • Maintain transporter agreements defining document responsibilities.
  • Obtain proper customer declarations where appropriate.
  • Train drivers not to alter or create documents after interception.
  • Maintain an escalation mechanism for GST enforcement incidents.
  • Preserve original ERP records when systems are migrated.
  • Review repeated e-way bill/document errors by branch, employee, route and transporter.
  • For high-value goods, maintain a dispatch compliance checklist signed before movement.
  • For transport companies, document procedures proving that drivers and owners follow customer-provided statutory documents.

23. Common mistakes and misconceptions

“Every e-way bill mistake means confiscation.”

No. Section 130 has specific statutory grounds. The facts and applicable clause must be established.

“Section 130 is automatically triggered whenever Section 129 is not paid.”

No. The current law after the 2022 amendment removed the old Section 129 progression language.

“Confiscation is the same as detention.”

No. Detention is a holding mechanism. Confiscation results in title vesting in the Government under Section 130(5).

“Redemption fine and penalty are the same amount.”

No. Fine in lieu of confiscation and penalty under Section 122 are distinct components.

“The maximum redemption fine is automatically payable.”

No. The statute sets a maximum ceiling for the goods fine. The adjudicating officer determines the fine subject to the statutory framework.

“The transporter is automatically responsible for the owner's goods.”

Not necessarily. Section 130(1)(v) contains a specific defence for the conveyance owner based on lack of knowledge or connivance.

“No hearing is required because the goods were already seized.”

Section 130(4) expressly requires an opportunity of being heard before the confiscation/penalty order.

“Once confiscated, the Government can immediately sell everything.”

Section 130(7) contains conditions, including satisfaction that the property is not required in another proceeding and reasonable time, not exceeding three months, to pay fine in lieu of confiscation.

24. Section 129, 130 and 122 – understand the connection

ProvisionMain roleKey consequence
Section 122Penalties for specified offences/contraventions.Specified penalty framework.
Section 129Detention, seizure and release of goods/conveyances in transit.Payment/security-based release and current post-order recovery process.
Section 130Confiscation of goods/conveyances and levy of penalty.Confiscation, Section 122 penalty and fine in lieu of confiscation.

Section 130 expressly says that the person is liable to penalty under Section 122 when the Section 130(1) grounds are satisfied. Therefore, the provisions should be read together rather than treating Section 130 as an isolated penalty rate.

25. Management checklist when a Section 130 notice is received

  1. Escalate the notice to the GST/tax head and legal team immediately.
  2. Identify the exact Section 130(1) clause cited.
  3. Prepare a complete chronology.
  4. Identify the owner of goods and owner of conveyance.
  5. Secure original invoices, e-way bills and transport records.
  6. Reconcile goods physically with books/ERP.
  7. Review GST returns and tax payment.
  8. Analyse any allegation of intent to evade tax separately.
  9. For a transporter, document knowledge/connivance facts.
  10. Calculate the Section 122 penalty using the applicable provision.
  11. Calculate the statutory ceiling for fine in lieu of confiscation.
  12. Check the 100% tax aggregate minimum.
  13. Prepare the hearing submission.
  14. Track the confiscation order and fine-payment period.
  15. Preserve all documents for appeal or future proceedings.

26. Frequently Asked Questions about Section 130

What is Section 130 of the CGST Act?

It deals with confiscation of goods or conveyances in specified circumstances and makes the person liable to penalty under Section 122.

When can goods be confiscated under GST?

Section 130(1) lists five circumstances, including specified contraventions with intent to evade tax, unaccounted taxable goods, taxable supplies by a person who has not applied for registration, and specified misuse of a conveyance.

Is intent to evade tax required in every Section 130 case?

Not in every clause. Intent to evade tax is expressly part of clauses (i) and (iv). Clause (ii), clause (iii) and clause (v) have their own statutory wording and conditions.

What is confiscation under GST?

It is a statutory consequence under which, once confiscation occurs, title to the confiscated goods or conveyance vests in the Government.

What is redemption fine under Section 130?

It is the fine offered to the owner in lieu of confiscation. If paid under the statutory framework, the owner can avoid confiscation of the relevant property.

What is the maximum fine for confiscated goods?

The fine cannot exceed the market value of the confiscated goods less the tax chargeable thereon.

What is the minimum aggregate of fine and penalty?

The aggregate of fine and penalty cannot be less than a penalty equal to 100% of the tax payable on the goods.

Is the Section 130 penalty itself 100% of tax?

Not necessarily. Section 130 connects the penalty to Section 122. The 100% wording in Section 130(2) is a minimum for the aggregate of fine and penalty, not necessarily the penalty alone.

Can a truck be confiscated under Section 130?

Yes, in the circumstances specified in Section 130(1)(v), subject to the conveyance owner's statutory defence concerning lack of knowledge or connivance.

What defence does the vehicle owner have?

The owner can prove that the conveyance was used for the contravention without the knowledge or connivance of the owner, the owner's agent, if any, and the person in charge of the conveyance.

Is hearing mandatory before confiscation?

Yes. Section 130(4) expressly requires an opportunity of being heard before an order of confiscation or penalty is issued.

When does title vest in Government?

When goods or a conveyance are confiscated under the Act, Section 130(5) provides that title vests in the Government.

Can confiscated goods be sold?

Yes, Section 130(7) permits disposal after the statutory conditions are satisfied, including checking that the property is not required in another proceeding and giving reasonable time not exceeding three months to pay fine in lieu of confiscation.

What happened to Section 130(3)?

It was omitted with effect from 1 January 2022.

Is Section 130 automatically triggered by non-payment under Section 129?

No. The 2022 amendment removed the earlier Section 129 language that expressly initiated Section 130 proceedings after non-payment within the earlier period.

Can Section 130 apply to an e-way bill mistake?

An e-way bill issue can be relevant to GST transit enforcement, but confiscation requires the facts to satisfy one of the grounds in Section 130(1). A mere assumption that an e-way bill error automatically equals confiscation is not enough.

What documents are useful in defending a Section 130 case?

Invoices, purchase/sales orders, books, stock records, e-way bills, transport records, GST returns, payment evidence, customer instructions and evidence concerning ownership and knowledge can all be relevant depending on the allegation.

What is the difference between fine and penalty?

Penalty is imposed under the applicable penalty provision, with Section 130 referring to Section 122. Fine in lieu of confiscation is the amount offered to avoid confiscation of the goods/conveyance.

28. Quick revision – Section 130 in 60 seconds

  • Subject: Confiscation of goods or conveyances and levy of penalty.
  • Five grounds: Sections 130(1)(i) to (v).
  • Intent: Expressly required in clauses (i) and (iv).
  • Unaccounted goods: Covered by clause (ii).
  • Unregistered taxable supply: Covered by clause (iii), subject to its statutory conditions.
  • Vehicle: Clause (v) includes a defence where the owner proves lack of knowledge/connivance as specified.
  • Penalty: Person is liable to penalty under Section 122.
  • Redemption fine: Owner must be given an option to pay fine in lieu of confiscation.
  • Goods fine ceiling: Market value less tax chargeable.
  • Aggregate minimum: Fine + penalty cannot be below 100% of tax payable on the goods.
  • Hearing: Mandatory before confiscation/penalty order.
  • Ownership: After confiscation, title vests in Government.
  • Disposal: Possible under Section 130(7) after the statutory conditions and reasonable time not exceeding three months.
  • 2022 change: Old Section 129 automatic progression language was removed and Section 130(3) was omitted.
