Section 136 of the CGST Act, 2017

Relevancy of statements under certain circumstances. Understand when a statement made and signed in response to a GST summons under Section 70 can become relevant for proving the truth of facts in a GST prosecution.

Section 136 at a Glance

Core subjectRelevancy of a statement made and signed after appearance in response to a Section 70 summons.
Where relevant?For proving, in a prosecution for an offence under the CGST Act, the truth of facts contained in the statement.
Two statutory situationsThe maker is unavailable in specified circumstances, or the maker is examined as a witness and the court considers admission in the interest of justice.
In simple language: Section 136 deals with a specific type of statement: a statement made and signed by a person who appears in response to a summons issued under Section 70 during GST inquiry or proceedings. The section says that such a statement can be relevant in a GST prosecution in the circumstances specified by the law.

1. Statutory Text of Section 136

136. Relevancy of statements under certain circumstances.—

A statement made and signed by a person on appearance in response to any summons issued under section 70 during the course of any inquiry or proceedings under this Act shall be relevant, for the purpose of proving, in any prosecution for an offence under this Act, the truth of the facts which it contains,—

(a) when the person who made the statement is dead or cannot be found, or is incapable of giving evidence, or is kept out of the way by the adverse party, or whose presence cannot be obtained without an amount of delay or expense which, under the circumstances of the case, the court considers unreasonable; or

(b) when the person who made the statement is examined as a witness in the case before the court and the court is of the opinion that, having regard to the circumstances of the case, the statement should be admitted in evidence in the interest of justice.

IndiaCode records Section 136 as in force as of September 2026. The provision specifically cross-references Section 70, which is the GST provision dealing with summons to give evidence and produce documents. citeturn0search1

2. Section 136 Explained in Simple Language

During a GST investigation or inquiry, an authorised officer can issue a summons under Section 70 requiring a person to appear, give evidence or produce documents. A person who appears may make a statement, and the statement may be signed.

Section 136 addresses a later and different stage: GST prosecution before a criminal court.

The section identifies circumstances in which the signed statement from the earlier GST proceeding can be relevant for proving the truth of the facts contained in that statement.

The key point: Section 136 does not say that every statement made during a GST inquiry automatically proves every fact in a criminal prosecution. It specifies when a particular signed statement is relevant for proving the truth of the facts it contains.

This distinction matters because an inquiry before a tax authority and a criminal prosecution before a court are not the same proceeding.

4. The Two Circumstances Specifically Mentioned in Section 136

Section 136 identifies two situations in which the statement is relevant for the purpose described by the section.

SituationWhat the law saysSimple explanation
(a) Maker unavailableThe person is dead, cannot be found, is incapable of giving evidence, is kept out of the way by the adverse party, or cannot be brought before the court without unreasonable delay or expense.The law recognises specified circumstances where the person who made the statement is not practically available to give evidence.
(b) Maker examinedThe person is examined as a witness before the court and the court considers, having regard to the circumstances, that the statement should be admitted in evidence in the interest of justice.The person appears as a witness in the prosecution and the court decides whether the earlier statement should be admitted.
Important: The two situations are alternatives. The statutory wording uses “or”. A business should therefore not assume that every signed statement will be treated identically in every prosecution.

5. Situation (a): The Person Who Made the Statement Is Unavailable

The first situation covers several different forms of unavailability. The statutory wording is deliberately specific.

5.1 The person is dead

If the person who made the statement has died, the statute recognises that the person cannot appear as a witness. Subject to the requirements of the provision and the court's assessment, the statement can be relevant for proving the truth of the facts it contains.

5.2 The person cannot be found

This covers circumstances where the maker cannot be located. It is not the same as merely saying that the person is difficult to contact; the actual facts and court's assessment matter.

5.3 The person is incapable of giving evidence

The provision also addresses situations where the maker is incapable of giving evidence. The reason and circumstances must be considered in the actual case.

5.4 The person is kept out of the way by the adverse party

The section expressly mentions a situation where the person is kept out of the way by the adverse party.

5.5 Presence cannot be obtained without unreasonable delay or expense

Even where the person is not in one of the earlier categories, the court may consider whether obtaining the person's presence would involve an amount of delay or expense that is unreasonable in the circumstances.

Simple takeaway: Situation (a) is about genuine unavailability of the person who made the statement. It is not simply a general rule that an earlier GST statement is automatically admissible in every prosecution.

6. Situation (b): The Person Is Examined as a Witness

The second situation is different. Here, the person who made the statement is examined as a witness in the case before the court.

Even then, Section 136 does not state that the earlier statement must automatically be admitted. The court must consider the circumstances of the case and be of the opinion that the statement should be admitted in the interest of justice.

Why this matters

  • The person who made the earlier statement is before the court as a witness.
  • The court considers the circumstances of the case.
  • The court determines whether the earlier statement should be admitted in evidence in the interest of justice.
Do not confuse “relevant” with “automatic conviction”. Section 136 addresses the relevance of a specified statement for proving the truth of facts it contains in a prosecution. The court still determines the evidentiary issues and ultimately decides the criminal case according to law.

7. What Does “For Proving the Truth of the Facts Which It Contains” Mean?

This phrase is important because the section is not merely concerned with the fact that a person once made a statement.

The statutory wording says the statement shall be relevant for the purpose of proving, in a prosecution, the truth of the facts which it contains, when one of the specified situations exists.

QuestionIllustration
What was stated?A person stated that certain invoices were created by a particular process.
Who made it?The person appeared in response to a Section 70 summons and signed the statement.
Under what proceeding?The statement was made during an inquiry/proceeding under the CGST Act.
What later happens?A prosecution for an offence under the Act is brought before a court.
Does Section 136 apply?The court examines whether one of the statutory situations in clause (a) or (b) exists.

The provision therefore creates a statutory route for the relevance of a particular category of earlier statement in specified circumstances.

8. What Section 136 Does Not Mean

It does not mean every GST statement is automatically admissible.

The statement must fit the statutory description: it must be made and signed by a person on appearance in response to a summons issued under Section 70 during an inquiry or proceeding under the Act, and one of the circumstances in Section 136 must apply.

It does not mean every statement proves every allegation.

The section refers to proving the truth of the facts contained in the statement. The exact facts, their relevance and their evidentiary significance remain matters for the court.

It does not itself create a criminal offence.

Section 136 is an evidentiary provision. The underlying offence must come from another provision of the Act.

It does not replace Section 70.

Section 70 is the provision concerning summons. Section 136 deals with the relevance of statements made in response to such summons.

It does not make an inquiry and prosecution the same proceeding.

A GST investigation/inquiry and a criminal prosecution have different procedural stages and purposes.

9. From GST Summons to Criminal Prosecution: How Section 136 Can Become Relevant

1. GST inquiryAn inquiry or proceeding is conducted under the CGST Act.
2. Section 70 summonsA person is summoned to appear, give evidence or produce documents.
3. Signed statementThe person appears and makes a statement that is signed.
4. Later prosecutionA prosecution for an offence under the CGST Act is brought before the court.
5. Section 136 testThe court examines whether clause (a) or clause (b) applies.
6. Evidentiary assessmentThe court determines the relevance and evidentiary effect of the statement in the prosecution.

This sequence shows why Section 136 is important for both investigating authorities and taxpayers: statements made during the earlier inquiry can have significance at a later prosecution stage.

10. Practical Examples

Example 1 – Supplier's authorised person gives a signed statement

An authorised person of a supplier appears in response to a Section 70 summons and signs a statement about the issuance of certain invoices. A prosecution is later initiated. If the statutory conditions of Section 136 are satisfied, the statement may be relevant for proving the truth of facts contained in it.

Example 2 – Statement maker has died

A person who made and signed a Section 70 statement subsequently dies. In a later prosecution, the court considers whether the circumstances fall within Section 136(a). The death of the maker is expressly one of the situations mentioned in the section.

Example 3 – Maker cannot be found

A person who made the statement cannot subsequently be located. The court considers the statutory requirements and whether Section 136(a) applies.

Example 4 – Maker is examined in court

The maker appears as a witness in the prosecution. The court considers the earlier signed statement and decides, having regard to the circumstances, whether it should be admitted in evidence in the interest of justice under Section 136(b).

Example 5 – Statement concerning fake invoices

A person states during the GST inquiry that certain invoices were issued without corresponding supplies. If a prosecution later relies on those facts, Section 136 may become relevant subject to its statutory conditions.

Example 6 – Statement about accounting records

An accounts employee explains how particular entries were created and signs a statement in response to a summons. If a later prosecution concerns those entries, the statement's evidentiary relevance can become an issue under Section 136.

Example 7 – Statement is not the only evidence

A prosecution contains a signed statement along with invoices, bank records, ERP logs and other documents. Section 136 concerns the statutory relevance of the statement; the court assesses the case and evidence as a whole according to law.

Example 8 – Genuine transaction dispute

A statement concerns a genuine commercial transaction where the parties disagree about the classification or documentation. The existence of a statement does not by itself establish that a criminal offence occurred. The underlying offence and all relevant evidence must still be considered.

Example 9 – Statement later clarified

A person gives a statement during an inquiry and later provides clarification when examined in court. The court considers the statement, testimony and circumstances under the applicable evidentiary rules.

Example 10 – Company investigation

A finance employee gives a signed statement concerning a company's GST records. A later prosecution names the company and certain individuals. Section 136 may become relevant to the statement, while Section 137 and the specific facts determine issues relating to company and individual liability.

12. Practical Approach When a GST Summons Is Received

A summons under Section 70 should not be ignored. At the same time, the recipient should avoid treating the response casually.

  1. Verify the summons, issuing authority, subject matter and date of appearance.
  2. Identify the GSTIN, tax period and transactions covered.
  3. Inform the appropriate finance/tax/legal authority within the organisation.
  4. Collect the records relevant to the specific subject matter.
  5. Prepare a factual chronology before appearance.
  6. Separate facts personally known to the person appearing from assumptions or information obtained from others.
  7. Answer accurately and avoid guessing where the person genuinely does not know a fact.
  8. Maintain copies of documents submitted and appropriate internal records of the response.
  9. Where the matter may lead to prosecution, obtain professional legal advice.
Practical principle: “I don't know” or “I need to check the records” can be more accurate than guessing. A person giving evidence should distinguish personal knowledge from information merely assumed or remembered incorrectly.

13. Why the Signature Matters

The statutory wording specifically refers to a statement “made and signed” by the person on appearance in response to a Section 70 summons.

Therefore, the nature of the document and circumstances in which it was made matter. A casual internal email, an unsigned note, or an unrelated commercial document should not simply be described as a Section 136 statement without examining the statutory requirements.

For businesses, this is another reason to maintain proper records of appearances, documents produced and the statements made during formal GST inquiry proceedings.

15. Common Misunderstandings

“Every statement given to a GST officer is covered by Section 136.”

No. The provision specifically concerns a statement made and signed on appearance in response to a summons issued under Section 70 during an inquiry or proceeding under the Act.

“A Section 70 statement automatically proves the criminal case.”

No. Section 136 identifies circumstances in which the statement is relevant for proving the truth of facts it contains. The court still decides the prosecution according to law.

“Section 136 applies only if the statement maker dies.”

No. Death is one circumstance under clause (a). The provision also covers other specified forms of unavailability and the separate situation in clause (b) where the maker is examined as a witness.

“If the maker is available, the earlier statement can never be relevant.”

That is also incorrect. Clause (b) specifically contemplates the maker being examined as a witness and the court considering admission in evidence in the interest of justice.

“A statement is relevant simply because it is signed.”

Signature is one part of the statutory description. The statement must also arise in response to a Section 70 summons during an inquiry/proceeding under the Act, and the statutory circumstances must be considered.

“Section 136 creates a new GST offence.”

No. It concerns evidentiary relevance in prosecution. The underlying offence is created elsewhere in the Act.

“A mismatch in GST records means the person's statement proves fraud.”

No. A mismatch can have many causes. The criminal offence charged and the evidence relevant to its ingredients must be considered.

18. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 136 of the CGST Act?

It deals with the relevancy of a statement made and signed by a person on appearance in response to a Section 70 summons during an inquiry or proceeding under the CGST Act, in specified circumstances.

2. What type of statement does Section 136 cover?

The statutory provision covers a statement made and signed by a person on appearance in response to a summons issued under Section 70 during an inquiry or proceeding under the Act.

3. Is every statement before a GST officer covered?

No. The statutory requirements must be satisfied, including the Section 70 summons and the circumstances specified in Section 136.

4. What are the two circumstances under Section 136?

Clause (a) covers specified circumstances in which the maker is unavailable. Clause (b) covers the situation where the maker is examined as a witness and the court considers admission of the statement in the interest of justice.

5. What if the person who gave the statement has died?

Death is expressly mentioned in Section 136(a) as one of the circumstances in which the statement may be relevant, subject to the operation of the provision in the actual case.

6. What if the person cannot be found?

That is also expressly mentioned in clause (a). The court must consider the circumstances and statutory requirements.

7. What if the person is examined as a witness?

Clause (b) applies where the person is examined as a witness before the court and the court considers that the statement should be admitted in evidence in the interest of justice.

8. Does Section 136 automatically prove the facts stated?

It provides that the statement shall be relevant for the purpose of proving the truth of the facts it contains when the specified circumstances apply. The court remains responsible for evaluating the prosecution and evidence according to law.

9. Is Section 136 connected to Section 70?

Yes. Section 136 expressly refers to a summons issued under Section 70.

10. Does Section 136 create a criminal offence?

No. It is an evidentiary provision concerning statements in prosecution.

11. Can a statement be relevant even when the maker is alive?

Yes. Clause (b) expressly contemplates the maker being examined as a witness before the court.

12. Does signing the statement make it automatically admissible?

No. The statutory description and the applicable circumstances must be satisfied, and the court determines the evidentiary issues.

13. Should a company keep records of GST summons?

Yes. A central summons register and organised records can help the business manage formal inquiries and preserve an accurate chronology.

14. Can a GST statement be the only evidence in a prosecution?

Section 136 does not prescribe a general rule that a prosecution must or must not have other evidence. The evidentiary assessment depends on the offence, facts and applicable law.

15. How is Section 136 different from Section 135?

Section 135 concerns presumption of culpable mental state. Section 136 concerns the relevance of specified statements made in response to Section 70 summons.

16. How is Section 136 different from Section 134?

Section 134 deals with cognizance of GST offences and the required Commissioner sanction/court level. Section 136 concerns evidentiary relevance of specified statements.

19. Quick Revision – Section 136

  • Section 136 = Relevancy of statements under certain circumstances.
  • The statement must be made and signed by a person appearing in response to a Section 70 summons.
  • The statement must arise during an inquiry or proceeding under the CGST Act.
  • Its relevance is for proving, in prosecution, the truth of the facts it contains.
  • Clause (a): specified circumstances where the maker is unavailable.
  • Clause (b): maker is examined as a witness and the court considers admission in evidence in the interest of justice.
  • Section 136 does not itself create an offence.
  • It does not mean every GST statement automatically proves a prosecution.
  • Section 70 should be read with Section 136.
  • Sections 132, 134, 135, 137 and 138 provide important surrounding context in GST prosecution matters.
