CGST Act Section 165 – Power to Make Regulations

Section 165 gives the Board power to make regulations, by notification, for carrying out the provisions of the CGST Act, subject to consistency with the Act and the rules made under it.

1. Section 165 at a Glance

Who can make regulations?The Board, by notification.
What must regulations be consistent with?The CGST Act and the rules made under the Act.

In simple language: Section 165 is a delegated-law provision that allows the Board to make regulations for implementing the CGST Act. The regulations cannot simply operate independently of the parent law: Section 165 expressly requires them to be consistent with the Act and the rules made under it.

3. Statutory Text of Section 165

165. Power to make regulations.— The Board may, by notification, make regulations consistent with this Act and the rules made thereunder to carry out the provisions of this Act.
Core rule: Section 165 contains one operative sentence. Its practical importance comes from the regulations made under the provision and from the requirement that those regulations remain consistent with both the CGST Act and the rules made under the Act.

4. Meaning in Simple Language

The CGST Act is the primary legislation. Section 164 separately gives the Government power to make rules. Section 165 then gives the Board a power to make regulations for carrying out the Act, but those regulations must be consistent with the Act and with the rules made under it.

This distinction matters because GST compliance often involves several layers of legal material. A finance professional may encounter the Act, CGST Rules, regulations, notifications, circulars, instructions, forms and portal procedures. These instruments do not all have the same legal source or function.

Easy way to remember: Section 164 = Government's rule-making power. Section 165 = Board's regulation-making power. Section 166 deals with laying rules, regulations and notifications before Parliament.

5. Who Is the Board?

In the CGST framework, Section 165 refers to the Board. The Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC) is the central tax administration body associated with the central indirect tax framework.

The important point for reading Section 165 is not merely the name of the institution but the legal function assigned by Parliament: the Board may, by notification, make regulations consistent with the Act and the rules made thereunder to carry out the provisions of the Act.

6. Three Core Requirements Under Section 165

1. The Board may make regulations

The provision creates the enabling authority. It is not itself a detailed compliance procedure for taxpayers.

2. Regulations are made by notification

The statutory text specifically states that the Board may make regulations “by notification”. When reviewing a regulation, identify the underlying notification and its effective date.

3. Regulations must be consistent with the Act and rules

This is an express statutory condition. A regulation should therefore be read with the parent CGST Act and the CGST Rules rather than in isolation.

Important: Do not assume that every circular, instruction, portal message or departmental clarification is a “regulation” under Section 165. First identify the legal instrument and the statutory provision under which it was issued.

7. What Can Regulations Do?

Section 165 says that regulations may be made “to carry out the provisions of this Act.” The phrase is broad enough to support operational implementation, but the power remains tied to the parent Act and the rules.

Operational implementationRegulations can provide a framework for carrying out statutory functions where regulation-making authority exists.
Technical administrationRegulatory instruments may support the administration of GST processes, subject to the statutory framework.
Consistency requirementThe regulation cannot be read as a free-standing source overriding the Act or the rules.

For a taxpayer, the practical task is therefore to identify the legal source of a requirement. If a compliance instruction is said to arise from a regulation, check the notification, the regulation itself, the parent Act and the relevant rules.

9. Practical Examples

Example 1 – Board creates an implementation regulation

Suppose the Board issues a regulation through notification to operationalise a statutory GST administration requirement. The finance team should retain the notification and regulation as the legal source.

Example 2 – Regulation conflicts with the Act

If a taxpayer believes a regulation imposes something inconsistent with the parent Act, the Act, the regulation and the enabling provision must be examined together. Section 165 expressly requires consistency with the Act.

Example 3 – Regulation conflicts with a CGST Rule

Section 165 expressly refers not only to the Act but also to the rules made thereunder. Therefore, the relationship with the relevant rule should be analysed rather than reading the regulation alone.

Example 4 – Finance team receives a circular

A circular may explain an administrative position, but the team should not automatically label it a Section 165 regulation. Identify the instrument first.

Example 5 – Historical audit

During an audit of FY 2018-19, the team should use the legal instruments applicable to that period. A later regulatory change should not automatically be inserted into historical workings.

Example 6 – GST software configuration

A software team should record the effective date and legal source of a compliance rule instead of hard-coding one procedure for all financial years.

Example 7 – Multiple GSTINs

A company with registrations across States should identify whether the requirement is under central GST law, State GST law, or another applicable framework before configuring a common control.

Example 8 – Legal notice response

If a notice cites a regulation, the taxpayer's response should verify the exact regulation, notification, effective date and parent statutory authority.

Example 9 – Regulation amended later

Where a regulation has been amended, maintain both the amendment date and the effective date so historical transactions can be tested against the correct version.

Example 10 – Act says one thing, operational portal shows another

A portal error or interface behaviour should not automatically be treated as a change in the law. The underlying Act, Rules, regulations and applicable notifications should be checked.

Example 11 – Internal compliance manual

A company's SOP can incorporate regulatory requirements, but the SOP itself is not a GST regulation. Keep the legal source separately documented.

Example 12 – External consultant advice

Professional advice may help interpret a regulation, but the finance team should retain the actual legal instrument on which the advice is based.

Example 13 – Rule change versus regulation

If the Government changes a CGST Rule under Section 164, that is different from a Board regulation under Section 165. The source and authority should be recorded separately.

Example 14 – Parliament laying requirement

Section 166 separately deals with laying rules, regulations and notifications before Parliament. Therefore, Section 165 should be read with Section 166 when studying the statutory framework.

10. Finance & Compliance Workflow

  1. Identify the requirement: Determine exactly what GST compliance action is required.
  2. Locate the parent Act provision: Find the statutory section connected to the requirement.
  3. Identify the instrument: Determine whether the source is a rule, regulation, notification, circular, instruction or portal procedure.
  4. For regulations, verify Section 165: Check the Board's notification and the regulation itself.
  5. Check consistency: Read the regulation with the CGST Act and applicable CGST Rules.
  6. Check effective date: Establish the date from which the requirement applies.
  7. Map to business process: Connect the legal requirement to invoice, ITC, return, payment, reconciliation or reporting controls.
  8. Configure ERP: Where appropriate, store effective dates and legal-source references.
  9. Retain evidence: Save the notification/regulation used for material positions.
  10. Review amendments: Before filing or responding to a notice, check whether the regulation has changed.

12. Important Legal Limits

Section 165 should not be read as giving the Board unlimited legislative authority. The section itself contains an important condition: regulations must be consistent with the CGST Act and the rules made thereunder.

  • A regulation should be traced to the statutory power under which it is made.
  • The notification making the regulation should be identified.
  • The regulation should be read together with the parent Act and applicable rules.
  • The effective date should be verified for historical transactions.
  • A circular or instruction should not automatically be described as a regulation.
  • Where a legal conflict is alleged, the exact statutory text and applicable judicial interpretation should be examined.
Historical-law caution: GST legislation is amended regularly. A conclusion about a regulation must be tied to the relevant date. The current text should not automatically be assumed to be the text applicable to an older transaction.

13. Common Mistakes

  • Confusing Section 164 rules with Section 165 regulations.
  • Assuming every notification is a regulation under Section 165.
  • Calling a circular a regulation without checking its legal source.
  • Ignoring the requirement that regulations be consistent with the Act and Rules.
  • Not checking the notification that introduced or amended the regulation.
  • Using the current version of a regulation for an old tax period.
  • Ignoring effective dates when preparing historical GST workings.
  • Configuring software without maintaining legal version history.
  • Relying only on a portal screen instead of checking the underlying legal instrument.
  • Failing to retain the legal source used for a material tax position.

15. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 165 of the CGST Act?

Section 165 gives the Board power, by notification, to make regulations consistent with the CGST Act and the rules made under it to carry out the provisions of the Act.

2. Who can make regulations under Section 165?

The section says that the Board may make regulations.

3. How are regulations made under Section 165?

Section 165 expressly says that the Board may make them by notification.

4. What must a Section 165 regulation be consistent with?

It must be consistent with the CGST Act and the rules made thereunder.

5. Is Section 165 the same as Section 164?

No. Section 164 concerns the Government's power to make rules, while Section 165 concerns the Board's power to make regulations.

6. Is a GST circular a regulation under Section 165?

Not automatically. The legal character of the instrument must be identified from its issuing authority, wording and statutory source.

7. Why is the notification important?

Because Section 165 expressly provides that the Board may make regulations by notification. The notification also helps establish the instrument and relevant dates.

8. Can a regulation override the CGST Act?

Section 165 expressly requires regulations to be consistent with the Act. Any alleged conflict therefore requires careful legal analysis of the parent statute and the regulation.

9. Can a regulation override a CGST Rule?

Section 165 expressly requires consistency with the rules made under the Act as well.

10. What does “to carry out the provisions of this Act” mean?

It describes the implementation purpose of the regulation-making power. Regulations are intended to support the operation and administration of the statutory framework.

11. What is the relationship between Section 165 and Section 166?

Section 165 provides the regulation-making power. Section 166 separately deals with laying rules, regulations and notifications before Parliament.

12. Should finance teams track regulations separately?

Yes. A legal register should identify whether a requirement arises from an Act, rule, regulation, notification, circular or another instrument.

13. Should historical GST workings use today's regulation?

Not automatically. The legal instrument and effective date applicable to the relevant period should be checked.

14. Does Section 165 itself prescribe a penalty?

No. Unlike Section 164(4), Section 165 itself does not contain a specific penalty ceiling. It is a power to make regulations.

15. Is Section 165 still in force?

India Code records Section 165 as an in-force provision of the CGST Act. The current statutory text is the single-sentence provision reproduced above.

16. What is the practical importance of Section 165 for taxpayers?

It helps explain the legal source of Board-made regulations used in GST administration and why those regulations must be read consistently with the Act and Rules.

17. Should GST software maintain regulation versions?

For historical compliance and audit support, maintaining legal effective dates and source references is a useful control.

18. Is this article legal advice?

No. It is an educational explanation. For a specific dispute, notice or transaction, verify the law, rules, regulations, notifications, circulars and judicial interpretation applicable to the relevant date.
