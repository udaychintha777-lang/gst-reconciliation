CGST Act Section 164 – Power of Government to Make Rules

Section 164 is the core rule-making provision of the CGST Act. It explains how the Government can make rules to carry out the Act, the matters for which rules may be prescribed, retrospective rule-making and the possible penalty for contravention of such rules.

3. Statutory Text of Section 164

164. Power of Government to make rules.—
(1) The Government may, on the recommendations of the Council, by notification, make rules for carrying out the provisions of this Act.
(2) Without prejudice to the generality of the provisions of sub-section (1), the Government may make rules for all or any of the matters which by this Act are required to be, or may be, prescribed or in respect of which provisions are to be or may be made by rules.
(3) The power to make rules conferred by this section shall include the power to give retrospective effect to the rules or any of them from a date not earlier than the date on which the provisions of this Act come into force.
(4) Any rules made under sub-section (1) or sub-section (2) may provide that a contravention thereof shall be liable to a penalty not exceeding ten thousand rupees.
Key point: Section 164 is an enabling provision. It gives the Government rule-making power; the detailed operational requirements appear in the CGST Rules and other instruments made under the Act.

4. Meaning in Simple Language

The CGST Act cannot practically contain every form, procedure, technical requirement, calculation method and operational detail in the primary legislation itself. Section 164 permits the Government to make rules for carrying out the Act.

For example, the Act may say that a return is to be furnished in the prescribed manner. The detailed form, manner, procedure, time-related mechanics and other operational requirements can then be provided through the rules, where the Act authorises rule-making.

Think of it this way: The Act establishes the statutory framework; rules provide many of the practical operating details within the authority delegated by the Act.

Section 164 should therefore be read together with the substantive section of the CGST Act that authorises or contemplates prescription by rules.

6. Scope of the Rule-Making Power

Sub-section (2) makes the scope explicit. Without limiting the general power in sub-section (1), the Government may make rules for matters which the Act requires to be prescribed, matters which may be prescribed, or matters in respect of which the Act provides that rules may be made.

Forms and proceduresWhere an Act provision requires a form or manner to be prescribed, the rules can provide the operational framework.
Compliance mechanicsReturns, payments, records, applications and other GST processes often rely on detailed rules.
Residual prescribed mattersSub-section (2) supports rule-making for matters that the Act expressly leaves to prescription.

As an illustration, the original legislative material itself identified numerous areas for detailed rules, including registration, invoices, returns, input tax credit, refunds, appeals, information returns and the fee for copies under Section 163. The enacted Act and subsequent amendments must always be checked for the current framework.

7. Retrospective Effect of GST Rules

Section 164(3) expressly says that the rule-making power includes the power to give retrospective effect to rules or any of them, but with an important outer limit: the retrospective date cannot be earlier than the date on which the provisions of the CGST Act came into force.

Important: “Retrospective power exists” does not mean that every GST rule can automatically be applied to every earlier period. The rule must itself validly exercise the power, and its legal effect has to be considered with the Act, the notification, the relevant dates and applicable judicial interpretation.
SituationSection 164(3) implication
Rule operates prospectivelyIt applies from its effective date according to the notification/rule.
Rule expressly given retrospective effectThe notification may specify a retrospective date, subject to the statutory lower limit in Section 164(3).
Attempt to go earlier than commencement of the ActSection 164(3) itself does not permit retrospective effect from a date earlier than the Act's commencement.

8. Penalty for Contravention of Rules

Section 164(4) allows rules made under sub-section (1) or (2) to provide that contravention of those rules may attract a penalty not exceeding ₹10,000.

Important distinction: Section 164 does not itself say that every breach of every GST rule automatically carries a ₹10,000 penalty. It permits the rules to provide for such a penalty, subject to the wording of the relevant rule and the broader penalty provisions of the Act.

9. Practical Examples

Example 1 – Return procedure

The Act requires a return to be furnished in a prescribed manner. Rules can provide the detailed procedural framework for filing.

Example 2 – Tax invoice details

Where the Act leaves invoice particulars or manner to be prescribed, the rules can supply those details.

Example 3 – ITC conditions

The Act may establish entitlement to ITC while rules provide procedural conditions or documentation mechanics where authorised.

Example 4 – Refund application

The Act provides the statutory refund framework; prescribed forms and procedural requirements can be contained in the rules.

Example 5 – Registration procedure

The Act establishes registration requirements and the rules can prescribe the application process, forms and related mechanics where authorised.

Example 6 – Retrospective amendment

A rule may be notified with retrospective effect where Section 164 permits it and the notification validly specifies the retrospective date.

Example 7 – Rule-based penalty

A rule made under Section 164 may specify a penalty for contravention, subject to the ceiling stated in Section 164(4) for such a rule-based penalty.

Example 8 – Section 163 fee

Section 163 says the fee for a copy of an order/document shall be prescribed. Section 164 is the general rule-making provision through which such prescribed details can be supported.

Example 9 – Accounts and records

Where the Act requires prescribed particulars or procedures for records, the rules can establish operational requirements within the authority delegated by the Act.

Example 10 – Multiple GST registrations

A finance team should identify whether a compliance requirement comes from the Act itself, a rule made under the Act, a notification, a circular/instruction or a portal procedure.

Example 11 – Rule versus internal instruction

A company should not treat every departmental communication as equivalent to a rule made under Section 164. Identify the legal instrument before relying on it.

Example 12 – Historical period review

When reviewing an old tax period, verify the rule and its effective date applicable to that period instead of applying today's rule mechanically.

Example 13 – Litigation

Where a dispute concerns validity or application of a rule, the taxpayer's legal team should examine the parent Act provision, the rule, notification, effective date and relevant judicial decisions together.

Example 14 – Compliance software

A GST ERP should store the legal effective date of a rule or procedure where historical reporting is required, rather than assuming one static rule applies to every period.

10. Compliance Workflow for Finance Teams

  1. Identify the legal requirement: locate the relevant section of the CGST Act.
  2. Check whether it is prescribed: look for the corresponding rule and current amendment history.
  3. Check effective date: determine when the relevant rule or amendment applies.
  4. Check notifications: identify whether a notification modifies commencement, exemption, procedure or applicability.
  5. Check circulars/instructions: use them for administrative clarification without treating them as substitutes for the Act or rules.
  6. Configure ERP: map the legal requirement to the applicable period, GSTIN and transaction type.
  7. Retain evidence: preserve the rule/notification reference used for significant compliance decisions.
  8. Review historical periods separately: do not automatically apply current rules to old transactions.
Good GST legal-control practice: For each major compliance rule, maintain a small legal register containing Act section, rule number, notification number, effective date, subject, business process affected and source document.

12. Important Legal Limits and Interpretation

Section 164 is a delegated-legislation provision. The rule-making power operates within the parent Act. A finance professional should therefore distinguish between:

  • Primary legislation: the CGST Act passed by Parliament.
  • Rules: made under the authority delegated by the Act.
  • Notifications: statutory instruments issued under specific enabling provisions.
  • Circulars and instructions: administrative clarifications or directions that must be read according to their legal character.
  • Portal procedures: technical implementation steps that do not by themselves replace the statutory text.
Retrospective-rule caution: When analysing a retrospective rule, check the exact notification, effective date, parent provision and the legal issue involved. Section 164(3) provides an enabling power, but it should not be treated as a blanket answer to every retrospective-law question.

13. Common Mistakes

  • Assuming every GST procedure comes directly from the CGST Act.
  • Ignoring the corresponding CGST Rule when an Act provision says “as prescribed”.
  • Applying a current rule to an old tax period without checking its effective date.
  • Assuming Section 164 automatically imposes a ₹10,000 penalty for every rule violation.
  • Confusing rules made under Section 164 with Board regulations under Section 165.
  • Treating a circular as if it were itself the parent Act.
  • Ignoring the exact notification that brought a rule or amendment into force.
  • Using an outdated GST compliance manual after a rule amendment.
  • Failing to preserve legal-source evidence for material tax positions.

15. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 164 of the CGST Act?

It empowers the Government, on the recommendations of the GST Council and by notification, to make rules for carrying out the provisions of the CGST Act.

2. Who makes rules under Section 164?

The Government makes the rules, on the recommendations of the Council, by notification.

3. Why are CGST Rules needed?

The Act provides the primary statutory framework, while many operational details are required to be prescribed or provided through rules.

4. Can Section 164 rules be retrospective?

Yes, Section 164(3) expressly permits retrospective effect, but not from a date earlier than the date on which the provisions of the CGST Act came into force.

5. Does every GST rule apply retrospectively?

No. Retrospective effect must be specifically and validly provided. The effective date and legal instrument must be checked.

6. What is the maximum penalty mentioned in Section 164(4)?

Rules made under sub-section (1) or (2) may provide for a penalty for contravention not exceeding ₹10,000.

7. Is ₹10,000 automatically payable for every rule violation?

No. Section 164(4) permits the rules to provide such a penalty; the relevant rule and applicable penalty provisions must be examined.

8. What does “on the recommendations of the Council” mean?

It is an express condition in Section 164(1) connecting the Government's rule-making power with recommendations of the GST Council.

9. What does “by notification” mean?

The rule-making power is exercised through the statutory notification mechanism.

10. Does Section 164 allow rules on any subject whatsoever?

No. The power is for carrying out the Act and for matters that the Act requires, permits or authorises to be prescribed or dealt with by rules.

11. What is the difference between a GST Act and a GST Rule?

The Act is the primary legislation. A rule is delegated legislation made under authority granted by the Act and operates within that statutory framework.

12. What is the difference between Section 164 and Section 165?

Section 164 concerns Government rule-making; Section 165 concerns the Board's power to make regulations consistent with the Act and rules.

13. Is a circular the same as a rule?

No. Their legal character is different. A finance team should identify the actual statutory instrument being relied upon.

14. Can rules change GST compliance procedures?

Rules can provide operational requirements where the Act authorises or permits prescription, but they operate within the parent Act.

15. How should old GST periods be reviewed?

Check the law, relevant rule, amendments, notifications and effective dates applicable to that period.

16. Is Section 164 relevant to Section 163 fees?

Yes, as a general rule-making provision. The original legislative material specifically identified the fee for taking a copy of an order or document under Section 163 as a matter for rules.

17. Should an ERP store rule effective dates?

For systems that support historical GST compliance, maintaining effective dates is useful because legal requirements can differ by period.

18. Is this page legal advice?

No. It is an educational explanation. Verify the current Act, rules, notifications, circulars and judicial interpretation applicable to the relevant issue and period.

16. Quick Revision – Section 164

  • Subject: Power of Government to make rules.
  • Authority: Government, on the recommendations of the Council.
  • Method: By notification.
  • Purpose: Carry out the provisions of the CGST Act.
  • Sub-section (2): Covers matters required or permitted to be prescribed or dealt with by rules.
  • Sub-section (3): Permits retrospective effect, but not from a date earlier than commencement of the Act.
  • Sub-section (4): Rules may provide a penalty for contravention not exceeding ₹10,000.
  • Compliance lesson: Always check the Act, relevant rule, notification and effective date together.
