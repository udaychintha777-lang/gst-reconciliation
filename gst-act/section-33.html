1. Why Section 33 matters

Searches such as “Section 33 CGST Act”, “Section 33 GST”, “amount of tax to be indicated in tax invoice”, “GST tax amount on invoice”, “how should CGST and SGST be shown on invoice?” and “Rule 46 GST invoice” all point to one practical compliance question: how must the GST amount be disclosed in the documents issued for a taxable supply?

Section 33 is a short provision, but it is important because it converts tax disclosure into a statutory requirement. It requires the person liable to pay tax for a supply made for consideration to prominently indicate the amount of tax in documents relating to assessment, the tax invoice and other like documents.

Simple rule: the customer should be able to identify the tax component from the document. The tax amount should not be hidden inside the total consideration where the GST law requires it to be indicated separately.

Section 33 should be read together with Section 31, which governs tax invoices and other GST documents, and Rule 46, which prescribes the detailed particulars of a tax invoice. The current India Code text identifies Section 33 as the provision dealing with the amount of tax to be indicated in tax invoices and other documents. citeturn0search0

2. What Section 33 says

Section 33 is titled “Amount of tax to be indicated in tax invoice and other documents.”

The operative principle is:

Where a supply is made for a consideration, every person who is liable to pay tax for that supply must prominently indicate in documents relating to assessment, the tax invoice and other like documents, the amount of tax which forms part of the price at which the supply is made.

The provision therefore focuses on tax disclosure. It does not independently decide whether the supply is taxable, what rate applies or whether the supplier is registered. Those questions are determined under other provisions of the GST law.

3. Complete statutory requirement

ElementMeaning
SupplyThere must be a supply to which the provision applies.
ConsiderationThe section refers to a supply made for consideration.
Person liable to pay taxThe disclosure obligation is connected with the person who is liable to pay tax for that supply.
DocumentsThe provision covers documents relating to assessment, tax invoices and other like documents.
Prominent indicationThe amount of tax must be clearly indicated rather than concealed in the overall price.
Tax amountThe document should identify the tax amount forming part of the price under the GST framework.

The statutory text does not turn Section 33 into a general rule that every commercial document must show GST. The document and transaction must first be examined under the applicable GST provisions.

4. What does “prominently indicate” mean?

Section 33 uses the expression “prominently indicate”. In practical invoice design, this means the tax component should be readily visible and distinguishable.

For a normal taxable invoice, a clear presentation may look like:

DescriptionAmount
Taxable value₹1,00,000
CGST @ 9%₹9,000
SGST @ 9%₹9,000
Total invoice value₹1,18,000

For an inter-State supply, the corresponding tax presentation would normally identify IGST rather than separately presenting CGST and SGST.

Do not hide GST: an invoice stating only “Total ₹1,18,000” without identifying the applicable tax component may not satisfy the detailed invoice requirements applicable to a tax invoice.

5. Section 33 and Section 31

Section 31 determines the circumstances in which a tax invoice, bill of supply, receipt voucher, payment voucher and other prescribed documents are issued. Section 33 addresses the identification of the tax amount in the relevant documents.

Section 31
When should the GST document be issued?
Section 32
Who may lawfully collect GST?
Section 33
How should the tax amount be indicated?
Section 34
How are later increases/decreases in taxable value or tax documented?

This sequence is useful during an invoice audit. First determine whether the document is the correct document under Section 31; then confirm the supplier is legally entitled to collect tax under Section 32; then check that the tax amount and other particulars are properly disclosed under Section 33 and the Rules.

7. How should CGST, SGST, UTGST and IGST be shown?

Section 33 requires the tax amount to be indicated; the applicable tax head is determined by the charging and place-of-supply provisions.

TransactionTypical tax presentation
Intra-State supply in a StateCGST + applicable State GST.
Intra-State supply in a Union Territory where UTGST appliesCGST + UTGST.
Inter-State supplyIGST.
Zero-rated supplyDepends on whether supply is made with payment of IGST or under the applicable LUT/bond route.
Reverse chargeInvoice/document must identify the reverse-charge treatment and the recipient's liability as prescribed.
Important: Section 33 does not permit a supplier to choose the tax head simply based on convenience. The correct tax type follows the GST charging and place-of-supply rules.

8. Taxable value vs tax amount

One of the most common invoice mistakes is confusing the taxable value with the GST amount.

TermExample
Taxable value₹5,00,000
GST rate18%
GST amount₹90,000
Total invoice value₹5,90,000

The invoice should make the relationship between these figures understandable. Rule 46 separately requires the total value, taxable value, rate of tax and amount of tax charged. citeturn0search2

This is particularly important in ERP systems where users sometimes enter the total contract value as taxable value or calculate GST on a figure that already includes tax.

9. Discounts and tax disclosure

Where discounts or abatements affect the taxable value under the applicable provisions, the invoice should reflect the correct taxable value and resulting tax amount.

For example:

ParticularAmount
Gross value₹10,00,000
Eligible discount₹1,00,000
Taxable value₹9,00,000
GST @ 18%₹1,62,000
Total₹10,62,000

The exact treatment of discounts depends on Section 15 and the conditions applicable to the particular discount. Section 33 then requires the resulting tax amount to be properly indicated in the relevant document.

10. Reverse charge invoices

Reverse charge requires special attention because the person who ultimately pays the tax to Government may be the recipient rather than the supplier.

Section 31 contains specific invoice/document requirements for reverse charge, while the Rules prescribe the particulars. The current invoice rules require the reverse-charge treatment to be identified in applicable documents. citeturn0search2

Do not confuse “tax shown on a document” with “supplier is liable to pay the tax.” Reverse charge changes who is liable, so the invoice and accounting treatment must be aligned with the applicable RCM provision.

11. Bill of supply vs tax invoice

A bill of supply is used in situations prescribed under Section 31, such as specified exempt supplies and composition taxpayers. It is not the same as a tax invoice.

Therefore, a supplier issuing a bill of supply should not simply insert a GST amount as though it were a normal tax invoice.

DocumentGeneral purpose
Tax invoiceTaxable supply where tax is chargeable.
Bill of supplySpecified supplies where tax invoice is not required under the applicable framework.
Receipt voucherReceipt of advance in specified circumstances.
Payment voucherSpecified reverse-charge payment situations.
Credit/debit notePost-invoice adjustment under Section 34.

12. Receipt vouchers and advances

A receipt voucher is governed by Section 31(3)(d) and the applicable Rules. The current CBIC invoice-rules page lists the receipt-voucher particulars, including supplier GSTIN, serial number, date, recipient details, description, advance amount, tax rate, tax amount, place of supply where relevant and reverse-charge indication. citeturn0search2

Where GST becomes payable on an advance under the applicable time-of-supply provisions, the document must correctly disclose the tax treatment. The accounting team should not confuse the commercial advance amount with the GST amount.

13. Credit notes, debit notes and revised invoices

Section 34 governs credit and debit notes, while the Rules prescribe their particulars. The document should identify the corresponding invoice and the taxable value/rate/tax adjustment as required.

This matters because an incorrect original tax amount should not be corrected merely by changing the accounting ledger. The correction should be supported by the prescribed GST document and corresponding return/reporting treatment.

Rule 46 and the invoice rules also contain special provisions for revised invoices and specified tax documents. citeturn0search2

14. E-invoice and electronic documents

Section 33 is technology-neutral in principle: the tax amount must be properly indicated in the applicable document. Where e-invoicing is applicable, the invoice data, tax values and tax-head information must be consistent with the e-invoice/IRN framework.

This means ERP validation should occur before the invoice is generated or transmitted for e-invoicing.

ERP
Correct customer, supply type, taxable value and rate.
Tax calculation
Correct CGST/SGST/IGST/cess calculation.
E-invoice
Invoice data should agree with the required e-invoice schema.
GST return
Reported taxable value and tax should reconcile with the invoice.

15. Construction, works contract and RA-bill examples

Section 33 becomes particularly important in construction and infrastructure businesses because invoices often contain multiple commercial components such as mobilisation advance adjustment, retention, deductions, recoveries, taxable value, GST and total payable amount.

Example — RA bill

ParticularAmount
Gross certified work₹1,00,00,000
Eligible taxable deductions/adjustments₹5,00,000
Taxable value₹95,00,000
GST @ 18%₹17,10,000
Gross invoice including GST₹1,12,10,000

Commercial deductions such as retention, mobilisation recovery or other contract deductions must be analysed under the applicable valuation provisions before deciding the taxable value. Once the correct taxable value and rate are determined, the tax amount must be clearly shown.

Mobilisation advance

Where an advance has GST consequences under the applicable time-of-supply rules, the receipt voucher and later tax invoice/adjustment should be reconciled so that tax is not duplicated or omitted.

16. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Intra-State supply

Taxable value ₹2,00,000 at 18%. The invoice should clearly identify CGST ₹18,000 and SGST ₹18,000, with total ₹2,36,000.

Example 2 — Inter-State supply

Taxable value ₹2,00,000 at 18%. The invoice should identify IGST ₹36,000 rather than CGST and SGST.

Example 3 — Hidden tax in total price

A supplier writes only “Invoice total ₹1,18,000” for a taxable supply. The invoice should be examined against the detailed tax-invoice requirements; simply hiding the tax component in the total does not satisfy the purpose of clear tax disclosure.

Example 4 — Wrong tax head

A supplier charges CGST and SGST on an inter-State supply. Section 33 does not cure the underlying place-of-supply error. The tax-head correction provisions must be examined.

Example 5 — Wrong rate

A supply correctly taxable at 12% is invoiced at 18%. The invoice shows a tax amount, but the amount is not necessarily legally correct. Section 33 is not a substitute for determining the correct rate.

Example 6 — Exempt supply

An exempt supply is billed with GST because the ERP default rate is 18%. Registration and ERP configuration should be corrected; the supplier should not treat the default rate as authority to collect tax.

Example 7 — Reverse charge

A service is subject to RCM. The supplier should not treat the invoice as an ordinary forward-charge invoice merely because a GSTIN is available. The RCM document and recipient accounting must be aligned.

Example 8 — Credit note

A supplier discovers that the taxable value was overstated. A credit note may be required under Section 34, subject to its conditions and reporting implications. The adjustment should clearly identify the relevant tax component.

Example 9 — Construction retention

A contractor's RA bill includes retention. The finance team should determine whether and when the retention affects taxable value under the applicable valuation/time-of-supply rules instead of automatically reducing GST.

Example 10 — ERP rounding

An ERP calculates CGST and SGST with inconsistent rounding and the total tax does not reconcile with the invoice total. The invoice master should apply a consistent calculation and rounding policy and the output should reconcile to the return.

17. Common invoice errors under Section 33

  • Showing only a gross invoice amount without separately identifying applicable GST.
  • Showing taxable value correctly but calculating the wrong tax amount.
  • Using CGST + SGST where IGST is applicable.
  • Using IGST where an intra-State supply requires CGST + SGST/UTGST.
  • Showing a tax amount on an exempt supply.
  • Using the wrong GST rate because the ERP rate master is outdated.
  • Failing to indicate reverse-charge treatment where required.
  • Incorrectly treating a bill of supply as a tax invoice.
  • Failing to reconcile credit/debit notes with the original invoice tax.
  • Mismatch between invoice tax, e-invoice data and GST return.
  • Rounding differences that cause the tax amount to disagree with the taxable value and rate.
  • Continuing to use an old invoice template after GST rate or compliance changes.

18. Recipient ITC connection

Section 33 is important for the recipient because clear tax disclosure supports invoice verification, but Section 33 by itself does not grant input tax credit.

ITC eligibility is governed principally by Section 16 and the related Rules. The recipient must satisfy the statutory conditions and possess the prescribed document.

The current CBIC ITC rules identify an invoice issued by the supplier in accordance with Section 31 as one of the documents on which ITC may be availed, subject to the applicable conditions. citeturn0search3

Practical lesson: a correctly formatted tax amount helps invoice compliance, but it does not cure an invalid supply, wrong GST registration, wrong tax liability or failure of Section 16 conditions.

20. GST Council background

The practical architecture surrounding Section 33 developed alongside the GST invoice framework. The original GST Council legislative material placed the tax-disclosure requirement within the tax-invoice chapter.

Revenue control

Technology alignment

The detailed tax-invoice framework has evolved through amendments to the CGST Rules and the development of electronic invoicing, while the basic Section 33 principle remains concise.

24. Section 33 GST FAQs

What is Section 33 of the CGST Act?

Section 33 requires the amount of tax forming part of the price of a taxable supply to be prominently indicated in documents relating to assessment, tax invoices and other like documents, where the section applies.

What does “prominently indicate” mean in GST?

It means the tax component should be clearly identifiable in the applicable document rather than being hidden inside the total price.

Does Section 33 require CGST and SGST to be shown separately?

For an intra-State taxable supply where both taxes apply, the invoice framework requires the applicable tax amounts to be identified. The exact presentation must follow the Rules and the nature of the supply.

How should GST be shown on an inter-State invoice?

Where IGST is applicable, the invoice should identify the IGST amount rather than treating the transaction as CGST plus SGST.

Is Section 33 the same as Rule 46?

No. Section 33 is the statutory requirement to indicate the tax amount. Rule 46 contains the detailed particulars required in a tax invoice.

Does Section 33 decide the GST rate?

No. The applicable rate must be determined under the charging provisions and relevant rate notifications. Section 33 concerns disclosure of the tax amount.

Does showing GST on an invoice automatically make the recipient eligible for ITC?

No. ITC is governed by Section 16 and other applicable provisions. Correct tax disclosure is only one part of invoice compliance.

Does Section 33 apply to a bill of supply?

The bill-of-supply framework is different from a tax invoice. A bill of supply is issued in circumstances prescribed by Section 31 and the Rules and should not be treated as a normal taxable tax invoice.

How is Section 33 connected with Section 32?

Section 32 controls whether GST can lawfully be collected. Section 33 controls the indication of the tax amount in the applicable documents. Both should be checked together.

How is Section 33 connected with Rule 46?

Rule 46 provides detailed tax-invoice particulars, including taxable value, tax rate and amount of tax charged. This operationalises the invoice disclosure framework surrounding Section 33.

26. Key takeaways

  • Section 33 requires the applicable tax amount to be prominently indicated in the specified documents.
  • It is a disclosure provision; it does not itself determine whether a supply is taxable or what rate applies.
  • Section 31, Section 32 and Section 33 should be read together when reviewing a GST invoice.
  • Rule 46 provides the detailed tax-invoice particulars, including taxable value, rate and amount of tax.
  • CGST/SGST/UTGST and IGST must be presented according to the applicable charging and place-of-supply framework.
  • Showing a GST amount on an invoice does not by itself make the tax legally payable or automatically create recipient ITC.
  • Construction and infrastructure RA bills need special attention to valuation, deductions, retention, advances and tax calculation.
  • Invoice data should reconcile across ERP, e-invoice, GST returns and tax payment records.
  • Use transaction-date-specific law because GST Rules, notifications and procedures can change.
