Section 122 of the CGST Act, 2017

At a glance

What is Section 122 of the CGST Act?

Section 122 is one of the central penalty provisions in the GST law. It identifies a wide range of specified GST offences and prescribes monetary penalties. These include incorrect or missing invoices, invoices without actual supply, non-payment of tax collected, TDS/TCS defaults, ITC taken without receipt of goods or services, fraudulent refunds, false records, failure to register, obstruction of officers, transport without prescribed documents, suppression of turnover, poor record keeping, non-compliance with information requests, dealing with confiscable goods and tampering with evidence or detained goods.

In simple words: Section 122 is not one single “GST penalty.” It is a collection of specific penalty triggers. The amount depends on which sub-section applies, what tax or ITC is involved, whether fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression is involved, and the precise facts of the case.

For a business, this section is especially important because several of the listed defaults can arise from weaknesses in invoicing, GST return preparation, purchase controls, e-invoice/e-way bill processes, ITC reconciliation, TDS/TCS compliance, document retention or responses to departmental proceedings. Serious cases can also have consequences under other provisions of the Act, including the provisions dealing with determination of tax, confiscation and prosecution.

Law

1Current statutory framework of Section 122

Section 122 is contained in Chapter XIX of the CGST Act, 2017, dealing with offences and penalties. The current text contains three principal penalty blocks: sub-section (1), sub-section (1A), sub-section (1B), sub-section (2) and sub-section (3). Sub-section (1) contains 21 specified offences.

Section 122(1) — core rule

Where a taxable person commits any of the specified acts in clauses (i) to (xxi), the person is liable to a penalty of ₹10,000 or an amount equivalent to the relevant tax/ITC/refund amount specified by the section, whichever is higher.

Section 122(1A)

A person who retains the benefit of a transaction covered by clauses (i), (ii), (vii) or (ix) of Section 122(1), and at whose instance the transaction is conducted, is liable to a penalty equal to the tax evaded or ITC availed of or passed on.

Section 122(1B)

An electronic commerce operator liable to collect tax at source under Section 52 can be penalised for specified platform-related contraventions. The penalty is ₹10,000 or the amount of tax involved, whichever is higher.

Section 122(2)

A registered person who has tax not paid/short-paid, tax erroneously refunded, or ITC wrongly availed or utilised is subject to different penalty treatment depending on whether the case is for a reason other than fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression or is for fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression to evade tax.

Section 122(3)

Certain persons other than the taxable person can face a penalty extending to ₹25,000, including persons who aid or abet specified offences, deal with confiscable goods or contravening services, fail to appear pursuant to a summons, or fail to issue/account for invoices as required.

Important: Section 122 is a penalty provision. It should not automatically be read as replacing the separate provisions for determination and recovery of tax, interest, confiscation or prosecution. Depending on the facts, more than one statutory consequence can be relevant.

Section 122(1)

3The 21 offences under Section 122(1)

Section 122(1) is often searched online as “21 GST offences under Section 122.” The safest way to understand it is to group the clauses by business activity rather than memorising them as a long list.

Group A — invoices and outward supplies

ClauseWhat the law targetsPlain-language meaningExample
(i) Supply without invoice, or incorrect/false invoice Goods/services are supplied but the required invoice is not issued, or the invoice is incorrect or false in the circumstances covered by the law. A taxable service is supplied for ₹10 lakh but no tax invoice is issued, or a fabricated invoice is issued with materially false details.
(ii) Invoice without actual supply An invoice or bill is issued even though the corresponding goods/services were not actually supplied. Company A raises a GST invoice to Company B only to enable ITC, although no goods are delivered and no genuine service is performed.
(xv) Suppression of turnover Sales/turnover are deliberately kept out of the declared figures and the suppression leads to tax evasion. A contractor receives taxable consideration of ₹50 lakh but deliberately records only ₹35 lakh in GST records to reduce tax.

Group B — tax collected but not paid

ClauseWhat it coversPlain-language meaning
(iii) Tax collected but not paid for more than three months after it became due If GST is collected from the customer and the amount is not paid to Government beyond the statutory three-month period, the penalty provision can apply.
(iv) Tax collected in contravention of the Act and not paid beyond three months This addresses collection of tax contrary to GST law followed by non-payment to Government beyond the specified period.

Group C — TDS and TCS compliance

ClauseDefaultPractical example
(v) Failure to deduct TDS, short deduction, or failure to pay deducted TDS to Government under Section 51 A notified deductor is required to deduct GST TDS but deducts less than required, or deducts it but does not remit it as required.
(vi) Failure to collect TCS, short collection, or failure to pay TCS collected under Section 52 An electronic commerce operator within the statutory TCS framework does not collect the required amount or does not remit the amount collected.

Group D — input tax credit and refund abuse

ClauseWhat it coversPlain-language explanation
(vii) ITC without actual receipt of goods/services Credit is taken or used even though the taxpayer has not actually received the goods or services, fully or partially, contrary to the Act/rules.
(viii) Fraudulent refund A refund is obtained fraudulently under the GST law.
(ix) ITC taken/distributed contrary to Section 20 or rules This primarily concerns improper distribution of input tax credit by an Input Service Distributor or other non-compliance with the statutory distribution mechanism.

Group E — records, registration and departmental proceedings

ClauseDefaultWhat a business should understand
(x) Falsifying/substituting financial records, fake accounts/documents, or false information/return with intention to evade tax This is a serious category because the statutory wording includes an intention to evade payment of tax.
(xi) Liable to register but fails to obtain registration A person who is required to register under the Act but remains unregistered can face the specified penalty consequences.
(xii) False information regarding registration particulars Incorrect information supplied at registration or subsequently can create penalty exposure.
(xiii) Obstructing/preventing an officer in discharge of duties Interfering with a lawful departmental function can trigger penalty exposure.
(xvi) Failure to keep, maintain or retain books/documents as required GST records must be maintained and retained in the manner required by the Act and Rules.
(xvii) Failure to furnish information/documents called for, or furnishing false information/documents during proceedings A response to a GST inquiry should be complete, accurate and supported by records. Silence or unsupported answers can create additional risk.

Group F — goods in transit, confiscation and evidence

ClauseDefaultPlain-language explanation
(xiv) Transporting taxable goods without prescribed documents Goods moving in transit must be accompanied by the documents prescribed under GST law, as applicable to the movement.
(xviii) Supplying, transporting or storing goods known/reasonably believed to be liable to confiscation The provision reaches persons dealing with goods when the statutory conditions concerning confiscability are met.
(xix) Using another registered person's GST registration number on an invoice/document A GSTIN is not something that can be borrowed or used to create a tax document for another person's transaction.
(xx) Tampering with or destroying material evidence/document Records relevant to a GST proceeding should not be altered, destroyed or manipulated.
(xxi) Disposing of or tampering with detained, seized or attached goods Once goods are under statutory detention, seizure or attachment, the business must not deal with them contrary to the applicable order/law.
Key point about 122(1): The minimum statutory figure is ₹10,000, but the section does not simply say “penalty is ₹10,000” for every offence. For the listed offences, the amount can be tied to tax evaded, TDS/TCS shortfall or non-payment, irregular ITC or fraudulent refund, with the statutory comparison determining the amount.
Section 122(1A)

4Penalty on the person who retains the benefit or causes the transaction

Section 122(1A) is especially important in fake-invoice and fraudulent-ITC cases. It was inserted by the Finance Act, 2020 and brought into force from 1 January 2021. The provision is designed to address situations where the person facing the commercial benefit or causing the specified transaction may not be the same person who physically issued the invoice or recorded the transaction.

Core wording: A person who retains the benefit of a transaction covered by Section 122(1)(i), (ii), (vii) or (ix), and at whose instance the transaction is conducted, is liable to a penalty equal to the tax evaded or ITC availed of or passed on.

Which four offences are linked to 122(1A)?

  1. 122(1)(i): supply without invoice or incorrect/false invoice;
  2. 122(1)(ii): invoice/bill without actual supply;
  3. 122(1)(vii): ITC without actual receipt of goods/services; and
  4. 122(1)(ix): taking/distributing ITC in contravention of Section 20/rules.

Why this matters in a company structure

Consider a company where an employee or intermediary arranges invoices from a supplier that does not actually provide the goods. The accounting team may technically receive and book the invoice, but Section 122(1A) asks a different question: who retained the benefit and at whose instance was the specified transaction conducted? The answer depends on evidence and facts; it should not be assumed merely because a person holds a particular designation.

Do not make a role-based assumption: Being a director, CFO, accountant, employee, consultant or broker does not by itself establish the factual requirements of Section 122(1A). The actual transaction, benefit, instructions, communications, documents and surrounding evidence matter.

Example — fake invoice arrangement

A business receives an invoice showing taxable value of ₹20 lakh and GST of ₹3.60 lakh. Investigation establishes that the goods were never received and the invoice was arranged to create ITC. If the statutory requirements of 122(1A) are established against a person who retained the benefit and at whose instance the transaction was conducted, the penalty under 122(1A) can be linked to the ₹3.60 lakh ITC involved. The exact legal result depends on the facts and adjudication.

Evidence that may become important

  • Purchase orders and work orders;
  • Goods receipt notes, delivery challans and lorry receipts;
  • E-way bill records;
  • Bank payment trail;
  • Stock register and consumption records;
  • Site inward registers;
  • Email/WhatsApp/business communication records where legally obtained and relevant;
  • Vendor master and due-diligence documents;
  • GST return and GSTR-2B/IMS reconciliation records;
  • Accounting entries and journal vouchers; and
  • Statements and other evidence collected during proceedings.
Section 122(1B)

5E-commerce operator penalty under Section 122(1B)

Section 122(1B) creates a separate penalty framework for specified electronic commerce operator (ECO) defaults. The provision is tied to an ECO that is liable to collect tax at source under Section 52.

DefaultWhat it means in simple languageControl for an ECO
Allows supply through the platform by an unregistered person who is not exempted from registration The platform permits a supplier to make the relevant supply despite the supplier not being registered and not falling under a notified exemption. Supplier onboarding and registration-status validation.
Allows an inter-State supply through the platform by a person not eligible to make such inter-State supply The platform facilitates an inter-State supply by a supplier who is not legally eligible for that supply. Inter-State eligibility and seller profile controls.
Fails to furnish correct details in the Section 52(4) statement for outward supplies by exempted persons The required statement data is incorrect or incomplete. GSTR-8 data validation and reconciliation before filing.
Scope clarification: The current wording specifically refers to an electronic commerce operator that is liable to collect tax at source under Section 52. The 2024 amendment to this provision was designed to align the penalty with ECOs within that TCS framework, with the amendment made effective from 1 October 2023.

Penalty amount

The provision specifies ₹10,000 or an amount equivalent to the tax involved had the supply been made by a registered person other than a person paying tax under Section 10, whichever is higher.

Section 122(2)

6Tax not paid, short-paid, erroneous refund or wrong ITC

Section 122(2) is different from the 21-clause list in Section 122(1). It specifically addresses a registered person where tax has not been paid or has been short-paid, a refund has been erroneously granted, or ITC has been wrongly availed or utilised.

122(2)(a) — non-fraud cases

Where the issue is for a reason other than fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax, the penalty is:

₹10,000 OR 10% of tax due
WHICHEVER IS HIGHER

This is not a rule that every tax mismatch automatically carries a 10% penalty. The statutory conditions and the applicable tax-determination provision must be considered.

122(2)(b) — fraud / wilful misstatement / suppression

Where the issue is for a reason involving fraud or wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax, the penalty is:

₹10,000 OR TAX DUE
WHICHEVER IS HIGHER

The distinction between ordinary error/non-fraud and fraud-related cases is therefore financially significant.

Example — short payment without fraud

Suppose the legally determined tax due is ₹80,000 and the case falls under 122(2)(a). Ten per cent of the tax due is ₹8,000. Comparing ₹10,000 and ₹8,000, the statutory penalty under this clause would be ₹10,000, subject to the full legal facts and applicable determination order.

Example — fraud-related case

Suppose tax due is ₹80,000 and the case falls under 122(2)(b). Comparing ₹10,000 and ₹80,000, the amount under this clause is ₹80,000, subject to adjudication and the applicable statutory provisions.

Important distinction: “Fraud” should not be inferred merely because an audit identifies a difference. A tax department proceeding must apply the relevant statutory requirements to the facts. The taxpayer should examine the exact allegation, evidence, period, tax computation and provision invoked in the notice/order.

Interaction with Sections 73, 74 and 74A

The tax-determination provisions have changed over time. Section 74 deals with specified fraud/wilful-misstatement/suppression cases for periods up to FY 2023-24, while Section 74A deals with determination for FY 2024-25 onward. Therefore, when reading a penalty provision for an actual dispute, always identify the financial year and the determination section used in the proceeding rather than applying a generic internet summary.

Section 122(3)

7Penalty on persons other than the main taxable person

Section 122(3) is important because GST penalties are not limited to the registered supplier. Certain other persons can also face a penalty of up to ₹25,000 if they fall within one of the specified categories.

ClauseWho/what is covered?Simple explanation
(a) Aids or abets an offence under Section 122(1)(i) to (xxi) A person who knowingly assists or encourages a specified offence may face a separate penalty consequence.
(b) Deals with goods known or reasonably believed to be liable to confiscation The provision can reach persons involved in acquiring, transporting, removing, depositing, keeping, concealing, supplying, purchasing or otherwise dealing with such goods.
(c) Deals with services known or reasonably believed to contravene GST law A person involved in a supply of services that he knows or has reason to believe is in contravention can fall within this clause.
(d) Fails to appear before a central tax officer when summoned A summons should not be ignored. The precise requirements and any lawful reason for non-appearance should be addressed through the proper channel.
(e) Fails to issue an invoice as required or fails to account for an invoice in books Invoice compliance includes both issuing the document correctly and properly accounting for the invoice where required.
Maximum vs fixed penalty: The wording says the person is liable to a penalty which may extend to ₹25,000. That is different from a provision saying that the penalty is automatically ₹25,000 in every case.
Practical application

8Detailed real-world examples

Example 1 — goods supplied but invoice not issued

A trader dispatches taxable goods worth ₹5 lakh and receives payment, but intentionally does not issue the GST tax invoice. The issue can fall under Section 122(1)(i). The penalty is not automatically limited to ₹10,000; the section compares ₹10,000 with the relevant amount specified in the provision.

Control: Ensure every dispatch/supply has an invoice sequence, invoice date, GSTIN, HSN/SAC, taxable value, tax rate and other required particulars as applicable.

Example 2 — invoice without supply

Company A issues an invoice to Company B for ₹10 lakh plus GST, but no goods are supplied and no genuine service is performed. The invoice is allegedly created only to generate ITC. This is a classic risk area under Section 122(1)(ii), and the beneficiary/instigator provisions may also become relevant where their statutory conditions are established.

Example 3 — ITC without receipt

A company books a purchase invoice for ₹12 lakh plus GST. The invoice appears in accounting records, but the goods never reached the warehouse, project site or customer location and there is no evidence of actual receipt. The company claims ITC. Section 122(1)(vii) specifically addresses ITC taken or utilised without actual receipt of goods or services.

Control: Do not rely only on the invoice. Maintain purchase order, GRN, e-way bill where applicable, transporter document, inward register, stock records, site acknowledgement and payment trail.

Example 4 — false books with tax-evasion intention

A business maintains one set of internal records and a manipulated set of financial records for GST reporting, with an intention to suppress taxable turnover. Section 122(1)(x) is potentially relevant because the clause expressly refers to falsification/substitution of financial records, fake accounts/documents or false information/return with an intention to evade tax.

Example 5 — registration not obtained

A person becomes liable for GST registration but continues taxable business without obtaining registration. Section 122(1)(xi) is one of the specified offences. Separate tax, interest and registration consequences may also arise under other provisions.

Example 6 — false registration information

A taxpayer deliberately provides incorrect information regarding registration particulars and later continues using those false particulars. Section 122(1)(xii) can become relevant. Businesses should therefore keep the GST registration profile aligned with the actual legal name, principal/additional places of business and other particulars as applicable.

Example 7 — transport without required documents

A transporter moves taxable goods without the documents required under GST law. If the statutory conditions are satisfied, Section 122(1)(xiv) may apply. Depending on the facts, the detention/confiscation provisions can also become relevant.

Example 8 — turnover suppression

A contractor receives ₹1 crore of taxable consideration but intentionally reports only ₹70 lakh, resulting in tax evasion. Section 122(1)(xv) addresses suppression of turnover leading to evasion of tax.

Example 9 — records not maintained

A registered business cannot produce the required books, supporting records and documents for the statutory period because records were discarded or were never properly maintained. Section 122(1)(xvi) can become relevant. Good accounting software does not by itself prove compliance; the underlying records must be maintained and retrievable.

Example 10 — ignoring a departmental information request

An officer calls for a purchase ledger, invoices, bank statements and reconciliation, but the taxpayer neither furnishes the information nor gives a proper response, or submits false documents during proceedings. Section 122(1)(xvii) can become relevant.

Example 11 — tampering with seized goods

Goods are detained or seized under GST law and a person moves, sells or tampers with them contrary to the statutory position. Section 122(1)(xxi) specifically addresses disposal or tampering with goods that have been detained, seized or attached.

Example 12 — TDS short deduction

A notified deductor is required to deduct GST TDS under Section 51 but deducts less than required. Section 122(1)(v) is one of the specified offences. The exact tax amount, period and other statutory consequences should be calculated separately.

Penalty is not the same as tax

If a business has short-paid GST, the department may determine the unpaid tax and applicable interest under the relevant provisions. Section 122 deals with penalty exposure. The taxpayer should therefore separate the working papers into at least three components: tax, interest and penalty, while also checking whether any other statutory consequence applies.

Penalty is not the same as prosecution

Some conduct covered by GST penalty provisions can also be relevant to the offences and prosecution framework under Section 132 if its conditions are met. A Section 122 analysis should therefore not be treated as a complete analysis of every legal consequence arising from serious fraud or fake-invoice activity.

Penalty is not automatically waived because tax is paid

Payment of tax can affect the consequences available under particular statutory provisions, but a taxpayer should not assume that payment alone automatically eliminates every penalty. The exact section, timing, nature of proceedings and applicable statutory relief must be checked.

For a company with multiple projects, branches, GST registrations, vendors and large purchase volumes, Section 122 compliance is best handled through internal controls rather than only by checking a notice after it arrives.

If a notice arrives

11What should a taxpayer do after receiving a Section 122 penalty notice?

A penalty notice should not be answered with a generic statement such as “we have paid the tax.” First identify exactly which clause or sub-section is invoked and what factual allegation is made.

  1. Read the allegation line by line. Identify the relevant clause: 122(1)(i), 122(1)(vii), 122(2)(a), 122(2)(b), 122(3), etc.
  2. Identify the period. Tax law changes can have different effective dates. Record the financial year and tax period.
  3. Reconcile the amount. Compare the department's tax/ITC/refund figure with the books, returns, invoices and payment records.
  4. Separate tax, interest and penalty. Do not mix the three calculations.
  5. Check the factual allegation. For example, “ITC without receipt” is different from “invoice not appearing in 2B,” because non-appearance alone does not establish every element of a 122(1)(vii) allegation.
  6. Collect evidence. Build an indexed bundle rather than sending hundreds of unorganised files.
  7. Respond within the applicable time. Track the statutory/procedural deadline and preserve proof of submission.
  8. Take professional advice where required. Large-value fake-invoice, fraud, confiscation or prosecution-linked matters should be assessed by an appropriately qualified professional.
Good response structure: allegation → legal provision → taxpayer's factual position → reconciliation → evidence reference → tax/interest computation → penalty position → prayer/request.

FAQ

16Frequently asked questions on Section 122

1. What is Section 122 of the CGST Act?

Section 122 prescribes penalties for specified GST offences. It covers invoice violations, certain tax-payment failures, TDS/TCS defaults, wrongful ITC, fraudulent refunds, false records, registration failures, obstruction, transport/document failures, turnover suppression, record-keeping failures and other specified conduct.

2. Are there 21 offences under Section 122(1)?

Yes. Section 122(1) lists clauses (i) to (xxi), giving 21 specified categories of offences.

3. Is the penalty under Section 122 always ₹10,000?

No. For Section 122(1), the statutory comparison can make the penalty the relevant tax/ITC/refund amount instead of ₹10,000. Other sub-sections have different formulas.

4. What is Section 122(1A)?

It targets a person who retains the benefit of specified transactions and at whose instance the transaction is conducted. The penalty is linked to tax evaded or ITC availed of or passed on.

5. Does Section 122(1A) apply to every wrong ITC claim?

No. Its wording is specifically tied to transactions covered by clauses (i), (ii), (vii) or (ix) of Section 122(1), and its own conditions must be established.

6. What is the penalty under Section 122(2)(a)?

For the statutory circumstances covered by 122(2)(a), where the reason is other than fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression to evade tax, the penalty is ₹10,000 or 10% of tax due, whichever is higher.

7. What is the penalty under Section 122(2)(b)?

For the statutory circumstances covered by 122(2)(b), involving fraud or wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax, the penalty is ₹10,000 or the tax due, whichever is higher.

8. Can a person other than the registered taxpayer be penalised?

Yes. Section 122(3) covers specified persons such as those who aid or abet certain offences, deal with goods/services in the circumstances specified, fail to appear pursuant to summons, or fail to issue/account for invoices. The penalty may extend to ₹25,000.

9. Can a director be penalised under Section 122?

A director is not automatically penalised merely because of the designation. Whether a particular provision applies depends on the wording of the provision and the facts/evidence establishing its statutory conditions. Section 122(1A), for example, focuses on retaining benefit and acting at the instance of the transaction.

10. Is non-reflection of an invoice in GSTR-2B automatically a Section 122(1)(vii) offence?

Not automatically. Section 122(1)(vii) specifically concerns taking or utilising ITC without actual receipt of goods or services. A 2B mismatch can be an important reconciliation exception, but the legal allegation and evidence must be examined against the statutory requirement.

11. What if the tax has already been paid?

Payment may affect the tax position and may have consequences under particular provisions, but it should not be assumed that every Section 122 penalty disappears automatically. Review the exact provision, timing, notice, order and any applicable statutory relief.

12. Is Section 122 only applicable to fake invoices?

No. Fake invoices are an important part of Section 122 risk, but the section is much broader. It also covers TDS/TCS defaults, registration, records, transport documents, obstruction, turnover suppression, summons-related conduct and other listed offences.

13. Can Section 122 penalty and prosecution both become relevant?

Depending on the conduct and statutory conditions, penalty provisions and prosecution provisions can both become relevant. Section 122 should therefore not be treated as the only possible legal consequence in a serious offence case.

14. What is the maximum penalty under Section 122(3)?

The wording of Section 122(3) says the person is liable to a penalty which may extend to ₹25,000.

15. Does Section 122 apply to an e-commerce operator?

Section 122(1B) specifically covers an electronic commerce operator that is liable to collect tax at source under Section 52 and commits one of the specified contraventions.

16. What should a company maintain to defend a wrong-ITC allegation?

Maintain the invoice plus evidence of actual receipt, purchase order, GRN, e-way bill where applicable, transporter documents, site/warehouse acknowledgement, stock records, payment trail, vendor master information and GST reconciliation workings. The exact evidence required depends on the nature of the transaction.
