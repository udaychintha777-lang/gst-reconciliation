1. What Section 12 does

Section 12 determines the time of supply of goods—the point at which GST liability is treated as arising for the goods supply under the CGST Act.

Core principle: Do not decide the GST period merely from the accounting posting date. Determine the statutory time of supply first, then map the result to invoice, books and return period.

The current statutory architecture separates ordinary taxable supplies, reverse-charge supplies, the former voucher rule, residual cases and additions to value such as interest or late-payment amounts. The current CBIC text records that the voucher provision was omitted with effect from 1 October 2025. citeturn0search0

3. Normal supply — Section 12(2)

For a normal supply of goods, Section 12(2) presently identifies the earlier of:

Invoice trigger
The date of issue of invoice or the last date on which the supplier is required under Section 31 to issue the invoice.
Payment trigger
The date on which the supplier receives payment for the supply.

The statutory proviso contains a limited concession where the supplier receives up to ₹1,000 in excess of the amount indicated in the tax invoice. To that extent, the supplier may opt to treat the date of issue of the invoice for the excess amount as the time of supply. Explanation 1 deems the supply to have been made to the extent covered by the invoice or payment. citeturn0search0

Professional approach: Always test the invoice date, legally prescribed invoice due date and payment date separately. Then document which event is earlier and why.

4. Payment and advances — an important historical GST issue

Section 12(2) contains a payment trigger, but the practical treatment of advances for goods has to be understood historically as well. GST authorities issued Notification No. 66/2017-Central Tax dated 15 November 2017, exempting registered persons from payment of tax on advances received for supply of goods, subject to the notification's scope. This changed the practical compliance burden for advances on goods without rewriting the core time-of-supply provision.

Do not confuse: “time of supply” and “when tax is actually required to be paid because of an exemption notification” are related but distinct legal questions. For historical transactions, identify the notification applicable on the advance date.
SituationReview
Advance received for goodsTest Section 12 and the exemption/relief notification applicable to the period.
Invoice issued before paymentCompare invoice and payment triggers under Section 12(2).
Payment exceeds invoice by up to ₹1,000Check the specific proviso and supplier option.
Historical periodDo not automatically apply today's operational treatment to an old transaction.

5. Reverse charge — Section 12(3)

For goods on which tax is paid or payable under reverse charge, Section 12(3) uses a different sequence. The current statutory rule identifies the earliest of:

  1. the date of receipt of the goods;
  2. the date of payment as entered in the recipient's books or the date the recipient's bank account is debited, whichever is earlier; or
  3. the date immediately following 30 days from the supplier's invoice or equivalent document.

If the time of supply cannot be determined under those triggers, the fallback is the date of entry in the recipient's books. citeturn0search6turn0search0

RCM control: The accounts payable team should track receipt date, invoice date and payment date separately. A single “invoice booking date” is not enough to establish the Section 12(3) position.

6. Vouchers — historical Section 12(4)

Historically, Section 12(4) provided that for a voucher, the time of supply was the date of issue where the supply was identifiable at that point, and otherwise the date of redemption.

Current-law change: The voucher-specific Section 12(4) provision was omitted with effect from 1 October 2025 by section 122 of the Finance (No. 7) Act, 2025. Therefore, a 2026 analysis should not reproduce the former Section 12(4) rule as current law. citeturn0search0

This is a good example of why GST research must be date-sensitive: a rule that was correct for an earlier period may no longer appear in the current statute.

7. Residual rule — Section 12(5)

Where the time of supply cannot be determined under Sections 12(2), 12(3) or the former 12(4) framework, Section 12(5) provides the fallback:

SituationFallback time
Periodic return is requiredDate on which the return is to be filed.
Other caseDate on which tax is paid.

This is a residual rule, not the starting point. A professional file should first demonstrate why the ordinary statutory triggers could not determine the time of supply.

8. Interest, late fee and penalty additions — Section 12(6)

Where the value of a goods supply increases because of interest, late fee or penalty for delayed payment of consideration, Section 12(6) places the time of supply for that additional value at the date on which the supplier receives the additional amount. citeturn0search0

Example: A customer pays the original invoice and later pays contractual interest for delayed payment. The GST time-of-supply analysis for the interest component is separate from the original supply and is linked to receipt of that additional amount under Section 12(6).

9. Amendment & development timeline

1 July 2017: Section 12 came into force as part of the GST provisions effective from the GST launch. India Code records 1 July 2017 as the commencement date for Section 12. citeturn0search5
15 November 2017: Notification 66/2017-CT provided relief from payment of tax on advances received for supply of goods by registered persons, an important operational development in the early GST regime.
1 February 2019: The CGST (Amendment) Act, 2018 omitted the words “sub-section (1) of” from Section 12(2)(a)'s reference to Section 31, correcting the invoice-reference drafting. CBIC records the amendment and effective date. citeturn0search0
2019 legislative development: The GST Council's 25th and 28th meeting agenda material discussed amendments to Sections 12 and 13, including removal of certain payment-related/drafting provisions. citeturn0search7turn0search8
1 October 2025: The voucher-specific Section 12(4) provision was omitted by the Finance (No. 7) Act, 2025. This is the most significant recent structural change visible in the current Section 12 text. citeturn0search0
Current 2026 position: The operative Section 12 framework covers normal goods, reverse charge, residual cases and additions for delayed-payment amounts, while the former voucher sub-section is no longer part of the current text.

10. GST Council & legislative development

Section 12 is closely connected with the GST Council's work on simplifying the time-of-supply mechanism. Early Council drafts show that the original legislative design contained additional triggers and a voucher provision. Subsequent Council discussions considered amendments intended to simplify or correct the framework. citeturn0search3turn0search7

Research rule: A GST Council agenda or recommendation explains legislative policy and proposed changes. The legally operative position must still be established from the enacted Act, effective-date notification and relevant subordinate legislation.

11. Practical examples

Example 1 — invoice before payment

Goods are supplied, the invoice is issued on 10 September and payment is received on 20 September. For a normal supply, the statutory Section 12(2) triggers are compared and the earlier event determines the time of supply.

Example 2 — payment before invoice

If payment is received before the invoice trigger, the payment event can become relevant under Section 12(2). For an advance involving goods, the historical notification framework must also be checked for the relevant period.

Example 3 — RCM purchase

For a reverse-charge goods transaction, do not apply the normal Section 12(2) test. Check receipt, payment and the 30-day invoice rule under Section 12(3).

Example 4 — delayed-payment interest

Original goods supply and later interest for delayed payment are analysed separately. Section 12(6) links the time of supply of the additional amount to its receipt.

Example 5 — old voucher transaction

If reviewing a voucher transaction from before 1 October 2025, the historical Section 12(4) rule may be relevant. For a current 2026 transaction, the omitted provision should not be presented as current law.

12. Professional Section 12 workflow

1. Identify the supply
Confirm that the transaction is a supply of goods.
2. Determine liability route
Normal charge or reverse charge?
3. Check invoice date
When was the invoice issued and when was it legally required?
4. Check payment
When was payment received/recorded?
5. Check receipt for RCM
When were goods actually received?
6. Check historical law
Especially advances and vouchers.
7. Apply fallback if necessary
Use Section 12(5) only after testing the preceding rules.
8. Map to returns
Ensure invoice, ledger and GST return period agree with the legal conclusion.

13. Common mistakes

  • Using accounting posting date as the time of supply without testing Section 12.
  • Applying the normal invoice/payment test to reverse-charge goods.
  • Ignoring the statutory invoice due date.
  • Applying current voucher treatment to a historical period without checking the law then in force.
  • Assuming every advance for goods automatically creates current GST payment liability without checking the applicable notification.
  • Ignoring the separate rule for interest/late-payment additions.
  • Using a GST Council recommendation as though it were itself the operative law.
  • Failing to retain evidence of receipt, payment and invoice dates.

15. Key takeaways

  • Section 12 determines when GST liability arises for goods.
  • Normal supplies require an invoice/payment comparison.
  • Reverse charge has a separate statutory trigger sequence.
  • Advance-payment treatment for goods must be understood historically as well as under current law.
  • The voucher-specific Section 12(4) rule was omitted from 1 October 2025.
  • Section 12(5) is a fallback, not the first test.
  • Interest, late fee and penalty additions have a separate time-of-supply rule.
  • For audit defence, retain invoice, payment, receipt and contractual evidence supporting the tax period.
