1. What Section 16 does

Section 16 is the principal eligibility provision for input tax credit under the CGST Act.

Section 16 does not simply say that every GST amount appearing on a purchase invoice can be claimed. It establishes the basic entitlement and then imposes a series of statutory conditions. In practice, an ITC claim should therefore be tested as a chain of conditions, not as a single invoice-checking exercise.

Core principle: An invoice may be genuine and still not be sufficient for ITC. The taxpayer must also test business use, document possession, supplier reporting, receipt, payment of tax to Government, return filing and the statutory time limit.

The official statutory framework begins with Section 16(1), under which a registered person is entitled, subject to conditions and restrictions, to credit of input tax charged on supplies used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of business. citeturn0search36

3. Section 16(1) — basic entitlement

A registered person is entitled to take credit of input tax charged on supplies of goods or services or both to him when those supplies are used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of business, subject to the conditions and restrictions prescribed.

Registered person
The entitlement is tied to GST registration and the relevant registered business.
Input tax
The tax must be tax charged on an inward supply falling within the statutory concept.
Business connection
The goods/services must be used or intended for business.
Conditions
Section 16(2), Section 17 and the Rules must also be examined.

Therefore, Section 16(1) is the gateway, not the final approval of every ITC claim.

4. Course or furtherance of business

The expression “course or furtherance of business” is deliberately broad. A purchase does not need to be directly resold to generate ITC. Business-use analysis can cover inputs, input services and capital goods used in operating the enterprise, subject to the Act's restrictions.

Examples

  • Raw material used in taxable production.
  • Professional services used for business operations.
  • Software subscriptions used by the finance department.
  • Machinery used for taxable project execution.
  • Security, audit, consultancy and other eligible business services.
But business use is not enough by itself. A business expense can still be blocked by Section 17(5), affected by exempt-supply apportionment, or fail another Section 16 condition.

5. Section 16(2)(a) — possession of tax document

The recipient must be in possession of a tax invoice or debit note issued by a registered supplier, or another prescribed tax-paying document.

This creates the first documentary control. The accounts team should verify not merely that a PDF exists, but that the document contains the information required under GST rules and corresponds to the actual supplier, recipient, supply and tax charged.

Supplier identity
GSTIN and legal/business identity.
Invoice identity
Invoice number and date.
Tax values
Taxable value and CGST/SGST/IGST/cess as applicable.
Supply details
Description, HSN/SAC, place of supply and other prescribed particulars.

Where an invoice is cancelled, amended, duplicated or otherwise disputed, the ITC trail should be reconciled before claiming or retaining credit.

6. Section 16(2)(aa) — supplier reporting condition

The supplier must furnish the details of the invoice or debit note referred to in Section 16(2)(a) in the statement of outward supplies, and those details must be communicated to the recipient in the prescribed manner.

This provision was introduced as GST moved toward invoice-level matching/communication architecture. In practical compliance today, this is why purchase-register reconciliation with the supplier-reported data and GSTR-2B has become a central ITC control.

Practical control: Do not treat GSTR-2B as a replacement for the invoice. Use it as an important system-level confirmation of supplier-reported data, while retaining the underlying tax document and receipt evidence.

The statutory condition and the electronic reporting architecture should be tested together. Section 16(2)(aa) is therefore particularly relevant to automated ITC reconciliation systems. citeturn0search36

7. Section 16(2)(b) — receipt of goods or services

ITC requires receipt of the goods or services or both. This is a substantive condition, not merely an accounting-entry condition.

Goods

Verify delivery records, GRN, e-way bill where applicable, transporter documents, warehouse/site records and stock or consumption evidence.

Services

Verify work completion, service reports, contracts, emails, professional deliverables, certification, site records or other evidence demonstrating actual receipt.

Invoice without receipt is not enough. If a supplier issues an invoice but the goods/services have not actually been received, Section 16(2)(b) must be considered before ITC is claimed.

8. Receipt of goods in lots or instalments

Where goods covered by an invoice are received in lots or instalments, the statutory condition is treated as satisfied upon receipt of the last lot or instalment.

This is particularly important in machinery procurement, large project supplies and phased material deliveries.

Example

A ₹1 crore machinery invoice covers five components. Four arrive in April and the final component arrives in June. The statutory receipt condition for the complete invoice should be analysed with reference to receipt of the last lot, rather than treating the first delivery as complete receipt of the entire invoice.

Control: Link invoice numbers to GRNs and delivery dates. A purchase-register reconciliation alone cannot establish the receipt condition.

9. Section 16(2)(c) — tax actually paid to Government

The tax charged in respect of the supply must have been actually paid to Government, either in cash or through utilisation of admissible ITC, subject to the statutory Section 41 framework.

This condition explains why supplier compliance can affect the recipient's ITC. It also explains the importance of reconciling supplier-reported invoices, amendments, cancellations and tax-payment compliance.

Professional approach: A recipient should not automatically assume that every invoice appearing in its books proves that the corresponding tax has reached Government. The recipient should maintain a reasonable supplier-compliance and reconciliation process appropriate to the risk.

10. Section 16(2)(d) — furnishing the return

The recipient must have furnished the return under Section 39. In practical terms, eligible ITC is claimed through the prescribed return mechanism, principally the GSTR-3B environment.

This creates an important distinction between eligibility and reporting. An invoice can be eligible in principle but still not be validly claimed if the return conditions and time limits have not been satisfied.

11. 180-day payment condition

The proviso to Section 16(2) requires the recipient, other than for specified reverse-charge supplies, to pay the supplier the amount towards the value of supply along with the tax payable within 180 days from the date of issue of the invoice.

If the recipient fails to make the payment within 180 days, an amount equal to the ITC availed is added to output tax liability, along with applicable interest, in the prescribed manner.

Start point
Date of issue of supplier's invoice.
Period
180 days.
Amount to be paid
Value of supply plus tax payable.
Consequence
ITC is reversed/added to output liability with interest as prescribed.

The GST Council's 25th meeting material records the 180-day payment framework and later discussions about re-availment after payment. citeturn0search5turn0search6

12. Re-availment after payment

The 180-day consequence is not intended to permanently destroy the credit in every case. When the recipient subsequently pays the supplier the value and tax, the prescribed mechanism permits re-availment of the ITC.

Rule 37 provides the operational mechanism. The GST Council's rules discussion specifically records that the time limit under Section 16(4) does not apply to re-availment of credit that had earlier been reversed under the 180-day mechanism. citeturn0search6

Accounting control: Maintain an “ITC reversed for 180 days” register with invoice date, 180-day due date, reversal date, interest, subsequent payment date and re-availment date.

13. Section 16(3) — depreciation restriction

Where depreciation has been claimed on the tax component of the cost of capital goods or plant and machinery under the Income-tax Act, ITC on that tax component is not allowed.

Why this matters

The law prevents a double benefit: the same GST tax component should not simultaneously be treated as part of depreciable cost for income-tax purposes and claimed as GST input tax credit.

Fixed-asset control: Finance teams should reconcile GST ITC with the fixed-asset register and the tax depreciation computation, especially for high-value machinery and project assets.

14. Section 16(4) — current normal ITC time limit

The current Section 16(4) provides that a registered person cannot take ITC in respect of an invoice or debit note after the 30th day of November following the end of the financial year to which the invoice or debit note pertains, or the date of furnishing the relevant annual return, whichever is earlier.

Financial year of invoice/debit noteNormal statutory cut-off under current 16(4)
2021-2230 November 2022, subject to the special provisions applicable to that period.
2022-2330 November 2023.
2023-2430 November 2024.
2024-2530 November 2025.
2025-2630 November 2026, unless the relevant annual return is furnished earlier.

The 53rd GST Council agenda records the 30 November substitution made through Notification 18/2022-Central Tax with effect from 1 October 2022, replacing the earlier September-return reference. citeturn0search0

Important: Section 16(4) is an outer time limit. It does not override the other eligibility conditions in Section 16(2), Section 17, Rules or other applicable provisions.

15. Debit notes after 1 January 2021

Finance Act 2020 amended Section 16(4) with effect from 1 January 2021 by removing the words that linked the debit note's ITC time limit to the financial year of the underlying invoice.

Consequently, for the amended provision, the relevant financial year is determined by the date of the debit note, rather than automatically by the date of the original invoice.

GST Council clarification: The 45th GST Council material confirms that from 1 January 2021 the date of issuance of the debit note, rather than the underlying invoice date, determines the relevant financial year for Section 16(4). citeturn0search2turn0search4

Example

An original invoice belongs to FY 2023-24, but a debit note is issued in FY 2025-26. For the amended Section 16(4) framework, the debit note's financial year is relevant to the ITC time-limit analysis.

16. Retrospective relief for specified earlier years

Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 introduced Section 16(5) retrospectively from 1 July 2017 for specified cases involving invoices/debit notes pertaining to FY 2017-18 through FY 2020-21.

The GST Council considered this relief because a significant number of taxpayers had filed delayed GSTR-3B returns for the initial GST years, and the normal Section 16(4) deadline had already expired. The Council material records the problem and the retrospective solution. citeturn0search0turn0search1

Do not generalise the relief. Section 16(5) is a specific statutory retrospective provision. It does not mean that every old ITC claim from any historical year is automatically revived.

Historical years covered

FY 2017-18, FY 2018-19, FY 2019-20 and FY 2020-21, subject to the precise statutory conditions and the relevant filing/payment history.

17. Cancellation and revocation relief

Section 16(6), inserted retrospectively through Finance (No. 2) Act 2024, addresses specified situations where registration was cancelled and subsequently revoked, and returns for the intervening period were furnished after revocation.

The 54th GST Council material explains that the retrospective provisions were designed to provide relief in specified cancellation/revocation situations while preserving the statutory framework. citeturn0search1

Professional review: For a cancelled-and-revoked GSTIN, do not apply the ordinary Section 16(4) deadline in isolation. Check Section 16(6), the cancellation/revocation dates, the relevant returns and the statutory conditions applicable to that case.

21. GSTR-2B and practical ITC reconciliation

Section 16(2)(aa) makes supplier reporting particularly important. For a modern finance department, ITC control should therefore combine the purchase register with GSTR-2B and underlying documentation.

Best practice: A reconciliation status such as “Matched” should not automatically mean “ITC eligible.” A robust GST reconciliation system should separately identify document match, receipt, payment ageing, blocked credit and Section 16(4) deadline.

22. Construction & infrastructure — Section 16 in practice

For construction and infrastructure businesses, Section 16 compliance must operate at project level as well as corporate level.

Material purchases
Match invoice, 2B, GRN, site receipt and consumption.
Sub-contractor invoices
Verify service receipt, work certification, invoice and supplier reporting.
Plant & machinery
Check business use, receipt, capitalisation and Section 16(3) depreciation restriction.
Retention/payment terms
Monitor 180-day payment condition separately from commercial retention.
Site-to-HO accounting
Maintain GSTIN-wise and project-wise documentation.
Year-end ITC
Track Section 16(4) cut-off by invoice/debit-note financial year.

For large EPC/HAM/works-contract businesses, a central ITC ageing dashboard should combine invoice date, receipt date, 2B appearance, payment due date and Section 16(4) deadline.

23. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — invoice present, 2B absent

Supplier invoice is booked in April, but the invoice is not reflected in the relevant supplier-reported data. Do not treat the accounting entry alone as satisfying the complete Section 16(2) chain. Investigate supplier reporting and applicable law before claiming credit.

Example 2 — 2B present, goods not received

Invoice appears in 2B, but goods are still in transit and have not been received. Section 16(2)(b) must be considered; GSTR-2B presence does not itself prove receipt.

Example 3 — goods received in lots

Complete machinery invoice is ₹1 crore and the final component arrives in June. The receipt condition for the complete invoice is linked to receipt of the last lot.

Example 4 — unpaid supplier after 180 days

Invoice is eligible and ITC was claimed, but the supplier has not been paid the required value plus tax within 180 days. The Section 16(2) proviso and Rule 37 consequence must be applied. After payment, re-availment can be considered under the prescribed mechanism.

Example 5 — old invoice

An FY 2024-25 invoice remains unclaimed after 30 November 2025. Ordinarily, Section 16(4) prevents later availment unless a specific statutory exception applies.

Example 6 — debit note

Original invoice is from FY 2023-24, but the debit note is issued in FY 2025-26. For the post-2021 Section 16(4) framework, the debit note's financial year is relevant.

Example 7 — depreciation

GST of ₹18 lakh is included in the cost of a machine and depreciation is claimed on that tax component under the Income-tax Act. Section 16(3) prevents ITC on that tax component.

Example 8 — cancellation and revocation

A GST registration is cancelled and later revoked. ITC for the affected period requires specific review under Section 16(6), rather than relying only on the ordinary Section 16(4) rule.

26. Common mistakes

  • Claiming ITC merely because an invoice is recorded in Tally/ERP.
  • Treating GSTR-2B as proof of physical receipt.
  • Ignoring Section 16(2)(aa) supplier reporting.
  • Failing to monitor the 180-day payment condition.
  • Reversing 180-day ITC but not maintaining a re-availment control after payment.
  • Missing the 30 November Section 16(4) deadline.
  • Using the old September cut-off for transactions governed by the post-1 October 2022 law.
  • Using the underlying invoice year for a debit note without checking the post-1 January 2021 rule.
  • Claiming ITC on tax capitalised into an asset where depreciation is claimed on that tax component.
  • Assuming retrospective Section 16(5)/(6) relief applies to every historical ITC dispute.
  • Ignoring Section 17(5) blocked-credit provisions while performing Section 16 reconciliation.

28. Key takeaways

  • Section 16 is a conditions-based ITC provision, not an invoice-only provision.
  • Section 16(1) establishes the basic business-use entitlement.
  • Section 16(2) creates the core compliance chain: document → supplier reporting → receipt → tax payment → return.
  • The 180-day payment condition must be monitored continuously.
  • Section 16(4) currently uses 30 November following the relevant financial year, or annual return filing, whichever is earlier.
  • Debit notes have a separate post-2021 financial-year rule based on their own issuance date.
  • Section 16(5) and 16(6) provide targeted retrospective relief for specified historical situations; they should not be applied broadly.
  • GSTR-2B reconciliation is important but does not replace receipt and other eligibility checks.
  • For construction businesses, invoice, GRN, site receipt, payment ageing and Section 16(4) ageing should be integrated into one ITC control system.
