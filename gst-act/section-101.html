Section 101 CGST Act — Orders of Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling

Section 101 explains what happens after an appeal against an advance ruling reaches the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling (AAAR). This practical guide explains the order-making process, confirmation or modification of the ruling, the 90-day statutory timeline, disagreement between members and—most importantly—how a business should implement and document the resulting GST position.

1. Why Section 101 matters in real business

Searches such as “Section 101 CGST Act”, “AAAR order”, “Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling order”, “what happens after GST advance ruling appeal”, and “AAAR ruling implementation” all point to the same practical question: once an advance ruling is challenged, what is the final outcome and what should the taxpayer or GST team do with it?

Section 101 is important because the original Authority for Advance Ruling (AAR) decision is not necessarily the end of the process. An eligible party may appeal under Section 100. Section 101 then governs how the AAAR disposes of that appeal or a reference arising from disagreement at the AAR stage.

Practical message: Do not treat an AAAR order as merely a legal document. For a business, it can affect tax classification, rate, exemption, place-of-supply analysis, ITC position, pricing, contracts, invoices, ERP tax codes, GST returns and future transactions.

2. Section 101 in simple language

Suppose a company asks the AAR a question about the GST treatment of a recurring transaction. The AAR gives a ruling. The applicant or an eligible officer is aggrieved and files an appeal under Section 100.

Step 1AAR gives advance ruling
Step 2Eligible party files appeal under Section 100
Step 3AAAR hears the parties
Step 4AAAR confirms or modifies the ruling

Section 101 therefore answers the question: what order can the Appellate Authority make after considering the appeal or reference?

3. What Section 101 actually provides

Sub-sectionCore rulePractical meaning
101(1)After giving the parties an opportunity of being heard, AAAR may pass an order confirming or modifying the ruling appealed against or referred to.The AAAR can uphold the AAR position or change it, after hearing the parties.
101(2)The order is to be passed within 90 days from filing of the Section 100 appeal or reference under Section 98(5).Track the filing date and monitor the statutory decision period.
101(3)If members differ on a point or points, it is deemed that no advance ruling can be issued for the question under the appeal or reference.A split decision does not become a majority ruling; the advance-ruling mechanism cannot produce a ruling on that question.
101(4)A signed and certified copy of the advance ruling is sent to the applicant, appellant, concerned officer, jurisdictional officer and Authority.Keep the certified order in the permanent GST legal/compliance file and circulate it to affected teams.

Section 101 should be read together with Sections 98, 99 and 100. A practical sequence is:

5. Right to be heard — why it matters

Section 101(1) expressly requires the Appellate Authority to give the parties to the appeal or reference an opportunity of being heard before passing its order. citeturn0search24

This is not a formality for the finance team. The quality of the factual record can determine how the tax issue is understood.

Practical control: Never allow the legal position to be presented without checking the commercial facts. If the ruling concerns a bundled construction contract, for example, the contract, BOQ, scope of work, payment terms, drawings, work orders and actual execution pattern should be consistent with the submissions.

What should be prepared for a hearing?

  • Clear statement of facts.
  • Exact question on which ruling is sought.
  • Relevant agreements and work orders.
  • Invoices and sample transaction records.
  • Tax classification/rate analysis.
  • ITC and return impact, where relevant.
  • Earlier correspondence or departmental views, if relevant.
  • A concise written submission linking every important fact to the legal issue.

6. When the AAAR confirms the AAR ruling

Confirmation means the appellate authority agrees with the ruling appealed against on the relevant question. The practical mistake is to stop reading at the word “confirmed.”

The finance team should read the question, facts, reasoning and final conclusion together. A ruling should not be operationalised by relying on a single sentence taken out of context.

Implementation rule: Prepare a one-page “AAAR impact note” stating the transaction, GST treatment, effective operational change, affected GSTINs, affected periods and responsible owner.

7. When the AAAR modifies the ruling

Modification is especially important for businesses because a changed conclusion may affect the tax treatment of an entire transaction stream.

8. The 90-day timeline under Section 101(2)

Section 101(2) provides that the order is to be passed within 90 days from the date of filing of the appeal under Section 100 or the reference under Section 98(5). citeturn0search24

9. What if AAAR members disagree?

Section 101(3) creates a specific consequence. Where the members differ on a point or points referred to in the appeal or reference, it is deemed that no advance ruling can be issued in respect of the question under the appeal or reference. citeturn0search24

This is important because a split between members should not be interpreted as if one member's view automatically becomes the applicable advance ruling.

Practical mistake to avoid: Do not implement the preferred member's view merely because it appears more favorable. The statutory consequence of the disagreement must be understood before deciding the tax treatment.

What should the business do?

  1. Obtain and preserve the certified order.
  2. Identify the exact question on which the members differed.
  3. Do not describe the dissenting view as the operative advance ruling.
  4. Obtain professional/legal advice on the transaction's ordinary GST treatment.
  5. Review whether another statutory remedy, clarification, departmental position or court proceeding is relevant.

10. Certified copy and communication of the AAAR ruling

11. What a business should do after receiving the AAAR order

A detailed implementation review is particularly useful when the business has multiple GST registrations, projects, branches, vendors or recurring contracts.

Recommended 10-point implementation review

  1. Read the order from facts through final conclusion.
  2. Identify the exact applicant, GSTIN and transaction covered.
  3. Identify whether the ruling concerns a specific contract or a broader recurring model.
  4. Prepare a tax treatment matrix before changing ERP masters.
  5. List affected invoices and transactions.
  6. Review tax already reported in GSTR-1/GSTR-3B.
  7. Review ITC consequences and GSTR-2B reconciliation where relevant.
  8. Update contracts, quotations or customer/vendor communication if pricing is affected.
  9. Document management approval for the new treatment.
  10. Set a review date to confirm that the new treatment is being followed consistently.

12. Accounting, invoicing and return impact

An AAAR order can have consequences beyond the GST legal file. Suppose a company had treated a recurring service as exempt but the appellate ruling changes the classification. The accounts team should not simply start charging GST from the next invoice without checking the entire transaction lifecycle.

AreaQuestions to ask
Sales invoicesWhich customers and GSTINs are affected? Is the tax type correct?
Purchase invoicesDoes the changed classification affect vendor invoices or ITC?
GSTR-1Are taxable values, rates and tax amounts reported correctly?
GSTR-3BDoes the output-tax or ITC position require a revised working?
GSTR-2B reconciliationWill the ruling change the way eligible/ineligible ITC is reviewed?
BooksAre tax ledgers and revenue/expense accounts aligned with the revised treatment?
Management reportingDoes the ruling affect project margin, customer pricing or tax provisions?

13. ITC issues after an advance-ruling order

One of the most common implementation failures is looking only at output GST. A ruling can indirectly affect the input-tax-credit analysis as well.

For example, if a business's transaction is reclassified, the finance team should review whether the inputs used for that transaction have a different ITC treatment, whether blocked-credit provisions become relevant, and whether the accounting team has consistently coded the purchases.

Reconciliation control: If the ruling changes the GST treatment of a recurring purchase/service, create a separate ITC review bucket in your purchase-register vs GSTR-2B reconciliation instead of silently changing the ledger code.

14. Contract and pricing impact

For project businesses, the tax treatment can materially affect commercial margins. Before implementing an AAAR order, compare the GST conclusion with the underlying contract.

15. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Classification of a recurring service

A company obtains an advance ruling on the GST classification of a recurring service. AAR gives a conclusion that increases the GST burden. The applicant appeals.

AAAR confirms: The finance team should formally close the appeal-impact review, update the tax matrix, check invoices from the relevant implementation point and train the billing team.

AAAR modifies: The revised classification must be mapped carefully to invoice tax code, rate, accounting ledger and return reporting.

Example 2 — Construction contract

A contractor receives an AAAR order concerning the GST treatment of a specific project activity. The company has similar activities across ten projects.

Solution: Do not automatically apply the ruling to all ten projects. First compare each contract's scope, customer type, location, work description and commercial structure with the facts considered by the AAAR. Create a project-wise applicability matrix.

Example 3 — Multiple GST registrations

A company has registrations in several States and receives an AAAR order relating to one registration.

Solution: Record the exact applicant and GSTIN. Then separately evaluate whether other registrations have identical facts and whether the ruling is legally applicable to them. Do not assume that one registration's advance ruling automatically becomes a universal ruling for every entity or GSTIN.

Example 4 — ITC treatment changes

An AAAR decision changes the tax characterization of a service used in a business operation.

Solution: Review historical purchase coding, current invoices, ITC eligibility, reversal workings and GSTR-2B reconciliation. Document the conclusion rather than making an unsupported retrospective adjustment.

Example 5 — Customer contract is tax-inclusive

A customer contract fixes a tax-inclusive price. An AAAR outcome changes the applicable GST treatment.

Solution: Finance should calculate the tax impact under the contract terms, review the tax-change clause and communicate with commercial/legal teams before changing the invoice value.

Example 6 — AAAR members disagree

The members disagree on the question appealed against.

Solution: Do not treat either individual view as the AAAR ruling. Record that the statutory consequence is that no advance ruling can be issued on that question through the appeal/reference. The business should then determine its ordinary GST position separately with appropriate professional review.

Construction, EPC, roads, irrigation, mining and infrastructure companies frequently face GST questions involving bundled contracts, works contract classification, subcontracting, project-site supplies, machinery, mobilisation advances and tax-inclusive commercial terms.

18. Common mistakes businesses should avoid

  1. Reading only the conclusion: The factual background can determine how far the ruling can be applied.
  2. Applying the order to unrelated transactions: Similar wording does not always mean identical facts.
  3. Changing ERP before legal review: A wrong tax master can create thousands of incorrect invoices.
  4. Ignoring historical transactions: Review whether already-reported transactions require attention.
  5. Ignoring ITC: Output tax and input tax consequences should be reviewed together.
  6. Ignoring contracts: Tax treatment can affect project margin and customer recovery.
  7. Treating a dissenting member's view as the ruling: Section 101(3) has a specific statutory consequence.
  8. No evidence trail: Keep the order, impact note, approvals, calculations and implementation evidence together.

19. Section 101 vs Section 102

Section 101Section 102
Deals with the AAAR order after appeal/reference.Deals with rectification of an advance ruling for an error apparent on the face of the record.
Can confirm or modify the ruling.Provides a separate rectification mechanism subject to its statutory conditions.
Order follows Section 100 appeal or Section 98(5) reference.Operates after an order under Section 98 or 101, subject to Section 102.

This distinction is important when a team sees an error after receiving an AAAR order: not every disagreement is automatically a rectification matter.

20. Section 101 vs Section 103 — order versus applicability

Section 101 is about the order of the Appellate Authority. Section 103 addresses the binding/applicability framework of an advance ruling. Therefore, after receiving an AAAR order, the finance team should not stop at Section 101; it should also review Section 103 before deciding how broadly the ruling should be applied.

Practical point: A legal conclusion and its scope of binding effect are two separate questions. Always document both.

22. Section 101 CGST Act — FAQs

What is Section 101 of the CGST Act?

Section 101 deals with orders of the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling after an appeal under Section 100 or a reference under Section 98(5).

Can AAAR confirm the AAR ruling?

Yes. Section 101(1) permits the Appellate Authority to confirm the ruling appealed against or referred to.

Can AAAR modify the AAR ruling?

Yes. Section 101(1) also permits modification after giving the parties an opportunity of being heard.

What is the 90-day period?

Section 101(2) provides that the order is to be passed within 90 days from the filing of the Section 100 appeal or the Section 98(5) reference.

What happens if AAAR members disagree?

Under Section 101(3), where members differ on a point or points referred to in appeal or reference, it is deemed that no advance ruling can be issued in respect of the question under the appeal or reference.

Who receives the AAAR order?

Section 101(4) provides for a signed and certified copy to be sent to the applicant, appellant, concerned officer, jurisdictional officer and the Authority.

Does an AAAR order automatically apply to every GST registration of a company?

Do not assume that. The business should separately examine the applicant, facts, GSTIN and the binding/applicability provisions before extending the treatment to another registration or materially different transaction.

What should an accountant do after an AAAR order?

Prepare an impact note, identify affected transactions, update the tax matrix and ERP controls, review GSTR-1/GSTR-3B and ITC consequences, and document approvals and implementation.

24. Key takeaways

  • Section 101 governs the order of the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling.
  • AAAR must give the parties an opportunity of being heard.
  • The AAAR may confirm or modify the ruling.
  • The order is to be passed within 90 days from the relevant appeal/reference filing date.
  • If members differ on a point, the statutory consequence is that no advance ruling can be issued on that question under the appeal/reference.
  • The signed and certified order should be preserved and circulated through a controlled internal process.
  • Businesses should translate the legal outcome into tax matrices, ERP controls, invoice logic, return workings, ITC review and contract/pricing decisions.
  • Do not apply a ruling mechanically to different GSTINs, projects or transactions without checking facts and applicability.
AAR ruling → Section 100 appeal/reference → AAAR hearing → Section 101 order → applicability review → business implementation
