GST ACT — SECTION 59

Section 59 CGST Act — Self Assessment Under GST

Section 59 is the legal foundation for GST self-assessment. In simple terms, the taxpayer is expected to calculate the tax payable, consider eligible input tax credit and report the resulting liability through the prescribed return process. This detailed guide explains what self-assessment really means, how it connects with GSTR-3B, invoices, GSTR-1, GSTR-2B, ITC, payment, interest and later departmental scrutiny.

1. Why Section 59 matters

Searches such as “Section 59 CGST Act”, “self assessment under GST”, “GST self assessment”, “how to calculate GST liability”, “GSTR-3B self assessment” and “who calculates GST payable” all lead back to the basic principle contained in Section 59.

GST is fundamentally a self-assessment based tax system. The taxpayer does not normally wait for a GST officer to calculate every month's tax and then issue a bill. The registered person is expected to calculate the liability, claim only eligible credits and discharge the resulting amount through the prescribed compliance mechanism.

Simple idea: You are responsible for getting your GST calculation right first. The department can later verify, scrutinise, audit or investigate that calculation.

2. Section 59 in very simple language

Imagine a business has the following for a month:

  • GST collected/collectible on taxable sales: ₹10 lakh
  • Eligible ITC: ₹6 lakh
  • Other legally payable amounts: ₹20,000

The business cannot simply wait for the department to tell it what to pay.

Broad calculation → Output tax − eligible ITC + other applicable liabilities = amount to be discharged

The actual GST return contains much more detail and separate treatment for different tax heads, reversals, reverse charge and other adjustments. But this is the basic idea behind self-assessment.

3. What does self-assessment mean?

Self-assessment does not mean “the taxpayer can decide how much tax it wants to pay.”

It means the taxpayer must apply the GST law to its own transactions and determine the amount legally payable.

Self-assessment meansIt does NOT mean
Classify supplies according to GST law.Choose an arbitrary tax rate.
Determine taxable value.Ignore taxable supplies because the officer has not contacted you.
Calculate output tax.Claim every invoice as ITC.
Apply eligible ITC and reversals.Use ITC without satisfying Section 16/17 conditions.
Pay the resulting liability.Assume filing a return automatically makes an incorrect calculation legal.

4. Statutory structure

Section 59 is deliberately short:

“Every registered person shall self-assess the taxes payable under this Act and furnish a return for each tax period as specified under section 39.”

In other words, Section 59 contains two linked responsibilities:

  1. Self-assess the tax payable; and
  2. furnish the return for the tax period under Section 39.

This is why Section 59 cannot be understood properly without reading Section 39 and the return Rules. Rule 61 provides the form and manner of furnishing the principal return for most regular taxpayers, including GSTR-3B. citeturn0search5

5. Responsibility of the taxpayer

The taxpayer's responsibility begins before the return is filed.

Identify supplies

Know what was sold, where it was supplied and whether it is taxable, exempt, zero-rated or otherwise treated under the Act.

Determine value

Apply the valuation provisions rather than simply using an internal sales figure.

Determine rate

Apply the correct rate, exemption or reverse-charge treatment.

Calculate ITC

Claim only credit satisfying the statutory conditions and make required reversals.

Calculate RCM

Identify supplies where tax is payable by the recipient under reverse charge.

File and pay

Report the liability and discharge it through the prescribed return/payment mechanism.

6. Calculate output tax

The first major part of self-assessment is determining output tax.

A finance team should normally start with the sales register and classify transactions into categories such as:

  • B2B taxable supplies;
  • B2C taxable supplies;
  • exports and other zero-rated supplies;
  • exempt/nil-rated/non-taxable supplies;
  • credit notes and debit notes;
  • advances where relevant;
  • reverse-charge transactions; and
  • other prescribed categories.

The calculation must follow the applicable time-of-supply, valuation, place-of-supply and rate provisions.

7. Consider eligible ITC

Self-assessment also requires the taxpayer to determine how much input tax credit can legally be claimed.

This is where Sections 16 and 17 become important.

Important: GSTR-2B is an important reconciliation statement, but it is not itself the legal source of ITC entitlement. The taxpayer must still satisfy the conditions and restrictions under the Act and Rules.

A proper self-assessment therefore considers invoice/document requirements, receipt, tax payment/reporting conditions, return filing, the 180-day payment rule where applicable, blocked credits and proportionate reversals.

8. Calculate net GST payable

After output tax and eligible ITC are determined, the taxpayer arrives at the amount to be discharged.

ComponentExample
Output CGST₹4,00,000
Output SGST₹4,00,000
Output IGST₹2,00,000
Eligible ITC₹6,00,000, subject to tax-head utilisation rules
RCM/other cash liabilities₹20,000

The actual utilisation of CGST, SGST/UTGST and IGST credits is governed by Section 49 and the prescribed rules. Therefore, “total ITC” should not be treated as one unrestricted bucket.

9. GSTR-3B connection

Section 59 requires the taxpayer to furnish a return for each tax period as specified under Section 39.

For regular taxpayers, FORM GSTR-3B is the principal summary return used to report tax liability and discharge it through the prescribed mechanism. Rule 61 specifies the form and manner of furnishing GSTR-3B and provides for payment through the electronic cash or credit ledger subject to the Act. citeturn0search5turn0search0

Therefore, a good self-assessment working paper should be prepared before the GSTR-3B is filed—not after the return has already been submitted.

10. GSTR-1 connection

GSTR-1 contains details of outward supplies and feeds important downstream GST systems.

Self-assessment should therefore reconcile:

Sales register → GSTR-1 → GSTR-3B → electronic liability/payment records

A mismatch between GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B does not automatically mean that Section 59 has been complied with. The taxpayer should identify and correct genuine errors through the permitted mechanisms.

11. GSTR-2B connection

For ITC, the finance team should compare the purchase register with GSTR-2B and other relevant records.

Typical checks include:

  • supplier GSTIN;
  • invoice number and date;
  • taxable value;
  • IGST/CGST/SGST/cess;
  • credit notes;
  • reverse-charge supplies;
  • blocked credit under Section 17(5);
  • 180-day payment condition; and
  • supplier reporting/payment risk.

Section 59 makes the taxpayer responsible for the final self-assessment; a reconciliation statement is therefore an internal control supporting that responsibility.

12. Reverse charge

Self-assessment is particularly important for reverse charge because the recipient may become responsible for paying GST even though the supplier did not charge GST in the ordinary manner.

The monthly self-assessment should therefore include a separate RCM checklist covering:

  • identified RCM categories;
  • supplier type;
  • place of supply;
  • taxable value;
  • applicable rate;
  • tax payment in cash where required;
  • invoice/self-invoice documentation where applicable; and
  • subsequent ITC eligibility.

13. Electronic cash and credit ledgers

Once self-assessed liability is determined, payment must be made through the prescribed electronic ledger mechanism.

The GST payment rules provide for the electronic tax liability register and specify how liabilities reported in returns are reflected and discharged through the electronic credit or cash ledger, subject to the statutory utilisation rules. citeturn0search0

Interest, late fee and several other amounts generally require cash payment rather than unrestricted ITC utilisation.

14. Interest and late payment

Self-assessment does not end when the return is prepared. The taxpayer must also ensure that the self-assessed liability is discharged within the applicable time.

If tax remains unpaid or is paid late, interest under Section 50 may arise, subject to the specific statutory framework applicable to the liability.

Therefore:

Correct self-assessment + timely payment = complete monthly compliance

17. Annual return connection

Self-assessment is performed for each tax period, but the taxpayer should also perform an annual reconciliation.

The annual review can identify:

  • missed outward supplies;
  • wrong tax rates;
  • unreported credit/debit notes;
  • excess or short ITC;
  • unreconciled GSTR-2B differences;
  • RCM omissions;
  • reversal differences; and
  • differences between books, returns and tax ledgers.

An annual reconciliation does not automatically permit every historical correction. The taxpayer must use the correction mechanism and statutory time limits applicable to the relevant error.

18. What happens after self-assessment?

Self-assessment does not prevent the GST department from checking the return later.

The Act contains separate mechanisms for scrutiny, audit, assessment and investigation.

For example, under Section 61, a proper officer can scrutinise a return and seek an explanation where discrepancies are noticed. The Rules provide for a scrutiny notice in FORM GST ASMT-10 and a response process. citeturn0search1

Think of it this way: taxpayer calculates first; department can verify later.

19. If the taxpayer discovers an error

A self-assessment error should not be ignored simply because the department has not noticed it.

The finance team should:

  1. identify the transaction and tax period;
  2. determine the correct legal treatment;
  3. calculate the tax difference;
  4. identify applicable interest;
  5. use the legally available return/correction mechanism; and
  6. preserve the correction working.

The exact correction route depends on the nature and period of the error. Section 59 creates the responsibility; other provisions determine how a particular correction is made.

20. Departmental verification

The existence of self-assessment means that a department officer does not normally need to issue a tax calculation before every return is filed.

Instead, departmental processes can later test whether the taxpayer's self-assessment was correct.

StageBasic concept
Self-assessmentTaxpayer calculates and reports liability.

Assessment/determinationTax may be determined where required under the applicable statutory provision.
AppealTaxpayer can use the prescribed appellate mechanism against an adverse order.

21. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Ordinary taxable sales

A company has taxable sales of ₹50 lakh at applicable GST rates. It calculates the output tax from invoices and reports the liability in its return. This is self-assessment.

Example 2 — Eligible ITC

Output tax is ₹8 lakh and eligible ITC after all restrictions is ₹5 lakh. Subject to tax-head utilisation rules and other liabilities, the net output liability is reduced by the eligible credit.

Example 3 — Blocked ITC

The purchase register contains ₹1 lakh GST on an item blocked under Section 17(5). The taxpayer must exclude it from eligible ITC even if the invoice appears in GSTR-2B.

Example 4 — RCM missed

A company receives a service covered by reverse charge but the accounting team does not identify it. The company has failed to properly self-assess the resulting liability.

Example 5 — Credit note

A supplier issues a GST credit note. The recipient's ITC working should assess the corresponding reduction/reversal required under the applicable provisions.

Example 6 — GSTR-1/GSTR-3B mismatch

Books show ₹1 crore taxable sales but GSTR-1 reports ₹90 lakh. The self-assessment working should investigate the ₹10 lakh difference rather than assuming the lower reported amount is correct.

Example 7 — GSTR-2B mismatch

Purchase register contains ₹10 lakh ITC but only ₹8 lakh appears in GSTR-2B. The missing ₹2 lakh requires investigation and cannot simply be treated as automatically eligible.

Example 8 — Late payment

Taxpayer correctly calculates ₹4 lakh liability but pays it late. Correct self-assessment does not remove the separate interest consequences of delayed payment.

Example 9 — Year-end error

An annual reconciliation identifies an omitted outward invoice. The company should determine the appropriate correction route and preserve the calculation rather than waiting for a departmental notice.

Example 10 — Department scrutiny

The department later issues an ASMT-10 regarding a mismatch. The taxpayer's monthly reconciliation files provide the evidence needed to explain the difference.

22. Construction and infrastructure examples

RA bills

A contractor should reconcile RA bills, tax invoices, advances, retention-related accounting, credit/debit notes and GST reporting before finalising the tax period's self-assessment.

Mobilisation advance

Where GST liability arises on an advance under the applicable time-of-supply rules, the self-assessment working must capture the advance rather than waiting for the final RA bill.

Subcontractors

Purchase/expense registers should identify subcontractor invoices, RCM cases where applicable, ITC eligibility and supplier reporting before GSTR-3B is finalised.

Multiple GST registrations

A construction group with registrations in several States should perform self-assessment separately for each GSTIN because each registration has its own return and tax records.

26. Common mistakes

  • Thinking self-assessment means the taxpayer can choose its tax liability.
  • Preparing GSTR-3B directly from the accounting ledger without reconciliation.
  • Claiming all GSTR-2B credit without checking Sections 16 and 17.
  • Ignoring reverse-charge liability.
  • Not reconciling GSTR-1 with GSTR-3B.
  • Ignoring tax-head utilisation rules.
  • Failing to calculate interest when liability is paid late.
  • Ignoring credit/debit notes and advances.
  • Not maintaining a monthly self-assessment working paper.
  • Assuming that because the department has not raised a query, the self-assessment must be correct.

28. Section 59 GST FAQs

What is Section 59 of the CGST Act?

Section 59 requires every registered person to self-assess the taxes payable under the CGST Act and furnish the return for each tax period as specified under Section 39.

What is self-assessment under GST?

It means the taxpayer calculates its own GST liability by applying the Act and Rules to its transactions, claims eligible ITC and reports/discharges the resulting liability through the prescribed return system.

Is GSTR-3B a self-assessment return?

GSTR-3B is the principal summary return through which regular taxpayers report and discharge liability under the prescribed return framework. Section 59 provides the underlying self-assessment responsibility.

Does GSTR-2B decide my ITC?

No. GSTR-2B is an important reconciliation source, but ITC entitlement must still satisfy the conditions and restrictions in Sections 16 and 17 and the applicable Rules.

Who is responsible for calculating GST payable?

The registered person is responsible for self-assessing the taxes payable under Section 59.

Does the GST officer calculate my monthly tax?

Normally, the taxpayer performs the initial self-assessment. The department can subsequently scrutinise, audit, investigate or determine liability under the applicable provisions.

What happens if I make a mistake in self-assessment?

The taxpayer should identify the error, calculate the tax/interest impact and use the legally available correction or payment mechanism applicable to that period and type of error.

Does self-assessment mean there is no GST department verification?

No. Sections such as Section 61 provide for departmental scrutiny of returns and discrepancies.

Does self-assessment include reverse charge?

Yes. The taxpayer must identify reverse-charge liabilities applicable to its transactions and comply with the prescribed payment and documentation requirements.

Can I claim every invoice appearing in GSTR-2B?

No. The taxpayer must independently test statutory ITC conditions, blocked credits, reversals and other restrictions.

Why should GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B be reconciled?

Because both contain important outward-tax information. Differences can indicate omissions, classification errors, credit-note issues or other compliance problems.

Why is Section 59 important for GST audits?

It establishes the taxpayer's legal responsibility for self-assessment. Audit and scrutiny therefore test whether the taxpayer's self-assessment was accurate and properly supported.

Is self-assessment the same as self-declaration?

They are related but not identical concepts. Self-assessment involves applying the law to determine the amount legally payable; the return is the prescribed reporting mechanism.

30. Key takeaways

  • Section 59 is the statutory foundation of GST self-assessment.
  • Every registered person must self-assess the taxes payable under the Act.
  • Self-assessment does not mean the taxpayer can choose its liability; the law determines the liability.
  • The taxpayer must correctly identify taxable supplies, value, rate, reverse charge and other liabilities.
  • Only legally eligible ITC should be claimed.
  • GSTR-3B is the principal summary return through which regular taxpayers report and discharge liability under the prescribed framework.
  • GSTR-1, GSTR-2B, purchase/sales registers and electronic ledgers are important inputs to a reliable self-assessment process.
  • Self-assessment does not prevent later departmental scrutiny or audit.
  • A monthly reconciliation working paper is one of the strongest practical controls supporting Section 59 compliance.
  • Section 59 should be read together with Section 39, Sections 16/17, Section 49, Section 50 and Section 61.
