GST ACT — SECTION 61

Section 61 CGST Act — Scrutiny of GST Returns

Section 61 is the provision under which the GST department can examine a registered person's return and related particulars to check whether the return appears correct. This guide explains GST scrutiny in simple language, the ASMT-10 notice, ASMT-11 reply, ASMT-12 closure, common GSTR-1/GSTR-3B/GSTR-2B mismatches, payment of accepted discrepancies and what can happen if the explanation is not satisfactory.

1. Why Section 61 matters

Searches such as “Section 61 CGST Act”, “GST scrutiny notice”, “ASMT-10 notice”, “ASMT-11 reply”, “ASMT-12”, “GST return scrutiny”, “GSTR-3B scrutiny” and “GST mismatch notice” all relate directly to this provision.

Section 59 places the initial responsibility on the taxpayer to self-assess GST. Section 61 gives the proper officer a separate mechanism to check that self-assessment by scrutinising the return and related particulars.

Simple idea: Section 59 says “taxpayer calculates and files”. Section 61 says “department can check that calculation and ask questions if it finds a discrepancy”.

2. Section 61 in very simple language

Suppose a company reports ₹1 crore of taxable sales in GSTR-1 but only ₹80 lakh in GSTR-3B.

The GST officer may notice the difference from information available to the department. Section 61 allows the officer to scrutinise the return and ask the taxpayer to explain the discrepancy.

Department notices discrepancy → ASMT-10 → Taxpayer explains/pays → ASMT-12 if acceptable
or → further departmental action if unresolved

This is the basic working of GST scrutiny.

3. What is scrutiny of returns?

Scrutiny is a return-level verification. The officer examines the return and related information available to the department to see whether the figures appear correct.

Scrutiny generally asksExample
Do different GST returns agree?GSTR-1 vs GSTR-3B mismatch.
Does ITC appear reasonable?Large ITC compared with GSTR-2B or previous periods.
Are tax payments consistent?Reported liability compared with payment/ledger data.
Are outward supplies consistent with other data?E-way bill or other available information.
Are unusual changes visible?Sudden fall in turnover with continued high ITC.

Scrutiny is not automatically a finding of tax evasion. A discrepancy may have a perfectly valid explanation.

5. Statutory structure

Sub-sectionEasy meaning
61(1)Proper officer may scrutinise the return and related particulars, identify discrepancies and seek explanation.
61(2)If the explanation is acceptable, the taxpayer is informed and no further action is taken under that scrutiny.
61(3)If the explanation is not satisfactory, or accepted discrepancy is not corrected, the officer may take further action under the specified provisions.

The current framework includes reference to Section 74A for the newer tax-period determination framework. citeturn0search0

6. Selection for scrutiny

Not every return is manually examined in the same way. Returns can be selected for scrutiny based on risk parameters and information available to the department.

CBIC Instruction No. 02/2022-GST describes scrutiny as a risk-based process and refers to information from GSTN, DGARM, ADVAIT, e-way bill systems and other available sources. citeturn0search23

Selection for scrutiny does not itself mean that tax is payable. It means the return has been selected for verification.

7. ASMT-10 notice

When a discrepancy is noticed during scrutiny, Rule 99 provides for a notice in FORM GST ASMT-10.

The notice should communicate the discrepancy and seek the registered person's explanation. Where possible, it also quantifies the tax, interest and other amount connected with the discrepancy. citeturn0search1turn0search19

When you receive ASMT-10What to do
Read each discrepancy separatelyDo not answer only with a general statement.
Check tax periodReconcile the exact period mentioned.
Check figuresMatch the officer's figures with books/returns.
Identify causeDetermine whether it is a timing, classification, reporting or genuine tax issue.
Prepare evidenceAttach reconciliations and supporting records.

8. Reply period

Section 61(3) provides that if no satisfactory explanation is furnished within 30 days of being informed by the proper officer, or within such further period as may be permitted, further action may be initiated.

Rule 99 requires the ASMT-10 notice to specify the response period. There are older CBIC web pages carrying a 15-day wording in Rule 99; however, the current statutory/rule text reflected in later compilations provides the 30-day framework. The article therefore follows the current statutory wording rather than relying on the stale webpage wording. citeturn0search1turn0search0

Practical point: Always follow the response date actually specified in the notice and do not wait until the last day.

9. ASMT-11 reply

The taxpayer responds to ASMT-10 in FORM GST ASMT-11.

There are two broad situations:

Accept discrepancy

Pay the applicable tax, interest and other amount and inform the officer.

Explain discrepancy

Provide the factual and legal explanation with reconciliation and supporting documents.

Rule 99 expressly provides for ASMT-11 for the taxpayer's payment/intimation or explanation. citeturn0search1

10. Accepting the discrepancy

If the taxpayer agrees that the discrepancy is genuine, it should calculate the correct liability carefully rather than simply paying the figure typed in the notice without verification.

The working should identify:

  • tax amount;
  • interest;
  • late fee/other amount, if applicable;
  • tax period;
  • reason for the original error; and
  • return/payment mechanism used to correct it.

11. Explaining a discrepancy

A good explanation should answer four questions:

  1. What difference did the officer identify?
  2. Why does the difference exist?
  3. What records prove the explanation?
  4. Does any tax actually remain payable?

For example, a GSTR-1/GSTR-3B mismatch may arise because a credit note was reported in one return in a different period, because an amendment was made, or because a genuine reporting error occurred.

A reconciliation table is usually much stronger than a long narrative without numbers.

12. ASMT-12 closure

Where the explanation or information furnished by the taxpayer is found acceptable, Rule 99 provides for communication in FORM GST ASMT-12.

In simple terms, ASMT-12 means the scrutiny issue has been accepted/closed at that stage.

It is important to understand the wording: closure of scrutiny under Section 61 does not create immunity from every other statutory proceeding based on separate information or a different legal provision.

13. What if explanation is not satisfactory?

Section 61(3) permits the proper officer to initiate appropriate action if:

  • no satisfactory explanation is furnished within the permitted period; or
  • the taxpayer accepts the discrepancy but fails to take the corrective measure in the relevant return.

The possible action includes proceedings under Sections 65, 66 or 67, or determination of tax and other dues under the applicable provisions, including Sections 73, 74 and the current Section 74A framework where applicable. citeturn0search3turn0search0

Important: ASMT-10 is not itself a final demand order. It is a scrutiny notice seeking explanation.

14. Section 65 audit

If scrutiny identifies issues requiring a broader examination, the department may consider audit under Section 65, subject to the statutory conditions.

An audit is more extensive than a simple return scrutiny because the officer examines books, records and other relevant documents for the selected period.

Rule 101 provides the audit procedure and FORM GST ADT-01 for the audit notice. citeturn0search20

15. Section 66 special audit

Section 66 deals with special audit where, at the relevant stage, an officer not below the rank of Assistant Commissioner considers the nature and complexity of the case and the interest of revenue and forms the statutory opinion that the value has not been correctly declared or credit is not within normal limits.

With prior approval of the Commissioner, the registered person can be directed to have specified records examined and audited by a nominated Chartered Accountant or Cost Accountant.

This is a separate and more specialised mechanism; an ASMT-10 scrutiny notice does not automatically mean a special audit will follow.

16. Section 67 inspection/search

Section 67 contains inspection, search and seizure powers in specified circumstances. These powers are materially different from ordinary return scrutiny.

Therefore, a taxpayer should not treat an ASMT-10 notice as equivalent to a search or investigation proceeding.

17. Tax determination under Sections 73/74/74A

If scrutiny does not resolve a discrepancy, the officer may move into a tax-determination proceeding under the applicable statutory provision.

ProvisionBroad role
Section 73Historically dealt with tax not paid/short paid or erroneous refund/ITC cases not involving fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression; its application is period-sensitive under the amended framework.
Section 74Fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression framework for the periods to which it applies.
Section 74ACurrent newer-period common tax-determination framework introduced by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024.

The precise determination provision depends on the tax period and applicable law. Section 61 is the scrutiny gateway; it should not be confused with the final determination provision.

18. GSTR-1 vs GSTR-3B scrutiny

This is one of the most common practical scrutiny areas.

GSTR-1GSTR-3BPossible reason for difference
₹1.00 crore₹80 lakhTiming difference, amendment, credit note, omission or genuine error.
₹80 lakh₹1.00 croreCould involve earlier-period reporting, classification or other adjustment.

Never assume that every difference is a tax shortfall. First prepare a transaction-level reconciliation and identify the reason.

19. GSTR-2B and ITC scrutiny

ITC can be scrutinised through comparisons between GSTR-3B, GSTR-2B and other available information.

Common questions include:

  • Why is ITC higher than the corresponding supplier-reported amount?
  • Were credit notes considered?
  • Was blocked ITC claimed?
  • Were Rule 42/43 reversals made?
  • Was Rule 37/37A treatment applied where relevant?
  • Was reverse-charge ITC separately handled?
  • Were imports/ISD credits correctly considered?

The taxpayer should respond with an invoice-level reconciliation rather than merely stating that the ITC is “correct”.

20. RCM scrutiny

Reverse-charge liabilities are another area where scrutiny can identify differences.

For example, an expense ledger may show legal, transportation, sponsorship or other transactions that require separate RCM analysis. The scrutiny response should show the relevant transaction, legal treatment, tax calculation, payment and ITC treatment.

21. E-way bill and other data

Departmental scrutiny can use information available from other GST systems. CBIC's scrutiny instruction specifically discusses use of data sources including GSTN, DGARM, ADVAIT and the e-way bill portal. citeturn0search23

Therefore, a business should maintain consistency between:

Books → Invoices → E-way bills → GSTR-1 → GSTR-3B → Tax payment records

A difference does not automatically prove tax evasion, but unexplained differences increase compliance risk.

22. How to read an ASMT-10

When an ASMT-10 arrives, do not start by writing a long legal reply. First convert the notice into a working sheet.

23. How to prepare a professional response

A strong ASMT-11 response should be:

  • Point-wise: answer every discrepancy separately.
  • Numerical: show figures and reconciliation.
  • Documented: identify supporting records.
  • Period-specific: explain the exact tax period.
  • Legally reasoned: cite the relevant GST provision where necessary.
  • Honest: do not defend an error as correct merely to avoid payment.
Best format: Officer's observation → taxpayer's reconciliation → reason for difference → legal position → supporting evidence → tax impact → conclusion.

24. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — GSTR-1 higher than GSTR-3B

GSTR-1 shows ₹1 crore while GSTR-3B shows ₹80 lakh. The company reconciles the ₹20 lakh difference and identifies ₹12 lakh as a credit-note/timing adjustment and ₹8 lakh as a genuine reporting error. The response should explain both separately.

Example 2 — ITC higher than GSTR-2B

GSTR-3B claims ₹50 lakh ITC while GSTR-2B supports ₹45 lakh. The taxpayer should reconcile the ₹5 lakh difference by invoice and explain legitimate timing/import/ISD/other differences rather than simply saying “ITC is correct”.

Example 3 — Accepted tax discrepancy

The officer identifies ₹2 lakh short-paid tax and the taxpayer confirms the calculation. The taxpayer should pay the applicable tax and interest and report the corrective action as required.

Example 4 — Timing difference

An invoice appears in GSTR-1 in one period and the corresponding tax treatment appears in GSTR-3B in another. The taxpayer should provide invoice-level evidence and explain the reporting sequence.

Example 5 — Credit note mismatch

The officer sees a difference caused by credit notes. The taxpayer provides original invoice, credit note, return reporting and accounting entries.

Example 6 — RCM omission

Scrutiny identifies an expense covered by RCM. The company verifies the legal position, calculates tax/interest, pays the amount and explains the corrective action.

Example 7 — E-way bill turnover difference

E-way bill data appears higher than reported outward supplies. The company identifies stock transfers, cancelled documents, exempt movement and actual taxable supplies before responding.

Example 8 — Previous-period amendment

A return comparison shows a difference because an earlier-period invoice was amended in a later return. The response should show the original and amended reporting trail.

Example 9 — No tax impact

A classification difference changes the presentation between tables but does not change total tax. The taxpayer should prove the no-tax-impact position with reconciliation.

Example 10 — Unsatisfactory explanation

If the taxpayer cannot substantiate the discrepancy and tax remains unpaid, the officer may proceed under the applicable statutory provisions after scrutiny.

25. Construction and infrastructure examples

RA bill reconciliation

For contractors, an ASMT-10 may involve differences between revenue recorded in books, RA bills, tax invoices, GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B. A project-wise reconciliation can explain timing differences.

Mobilisation advances

Advances should be separately tracked because their GST treatment can differ from final invoice reporting depending on the applicable time-of-supply provisions.

Retention money

The taxpayer should maintain contract clauses, RA bills, invoices and accounting treatment to explain timing differences involving retention amounts.

Subcontractor ITC

A construction company should reconcile subcontractor ITC with GSTR-2B, payment conditions, credit notes and blocked/restricted credit provisions before responding to an ITC discrepancy.

26. Documents to keep ready

  • GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B copies;
  • GSTR-2B reconciliation;
  • sales and purchase registers;
  • tax invoices and credit/debit notes;
  • e-way bills where relevant;
  • electronic liability, cash and credit ledger;
  • RCM workings;
  • ITC reversal workings;
  • bank/payment records where relevant;
  • contracts and agreements;
  • project-wise reconciliations;
  • prior-period amendment records; and
  • working papers supporting the ASMT-11 response.

30. Common mistakes

  • Ignoring an ASMT-10 because it is “only scrutiny”.
  • Sending a generic reply without a numerical reconciliation.
  • Answering only the tax amount and not the reason for the difference.
  • Failing to check the exact tax period.
  • Providing unsupported statements without documents.
  • Admitting liability without independently checking the calculation.
  • Assuming every GSTR-1/GSTR-3B difference means tax evasion.
  • Ignoring GSTR-2B, e-way bill or other relevant reconciliations.
  • Missing the response period.
  • Confusing ASMT-10 scrutiny with a final demand order.

32. Section 61 GST FAQs

What is Section 61 of the CGST Act?

Section 61 allows the proper officer to scrutinise a registered person's return and related particulars to verify correctness and seek an explanation for discrepancies.

What is GST scrutiny?

It is a return-level verification process in which the officer compares the return with related information available to the department and asks the taxpayer to explain discrepancies.

What is ASMT-10?

ASMT-10 is the scrutiny notice issued under Rule 99 when a discrepancy is noticed.

What is ASMT-11?

ASMT-11 is the taxpayer's response to the ASMT-10 discrepancy, including explanation or intimation of payment where the discrepancy is accepted.

What is ASMT-12?

ASMT-12 is the communication issued where the explanation or information submitted by the taxpayer is found acceptable.

Is ASMT-10 a GST demand notice?

No. ASMT-10 is a scrutiny notice seeking explanation. It is not itself a final determination of tax liability.

How many days are available to reply to ASMT-10?

Section 61(3) provides 30 days, or such further period as may be permitted. The taxpayer should always follow the specific response period stated in the notice.

Can the officer ask for GSTR-2B reconciliation?

Yes, where relevant to the discrepancy, the officer may use available ITC-related information and seek an explanation supported by records.

Can scrutiny lead to a GST audit?

Yes. Section 61(3) expressly allows appropriate action including Section 65 audit, Section 66 special audit or Section 67 action where the statutory conditions are met.

Can scrutiny lead to tax demand proceedings?

Yes. Where the discrepancy remains unresolved, the officer may proceed to determine tax and other dues under the applicable statutory provision, including the period-sensitive Section 73/74 framework or Section 74A where applicable.

Does receiving ASMT-10 mean the taxpayer has committed fraud?

No. A scrutiny notice only identifies a discrepancy requiring explanation. A discrepancy can arise from timing, reporting, classification or other legitimate reasons.

Should I accept every discrepancy mentioned by the officer?

No. The taxpayer should independently reconcile the issue. If the discrepancy is genuine, it should be accepted and corrected; if it has a valid explanation, supporting evidence should be provided.

What is the best way to reply to ASMT-10?

Use a point-wise numerical reconciliation supported by invoices, ledgers, returns and legal explanation wherever necessary.

Can ASMT-12 be issued if the explanation is accepted?

Yes. Rule 99 provides for ASMT-12 where the explanation or information submitted is found acceptable.

34. Key takeaways

  • Section 61 is the statutory basis for scrutiny of GST returns.
  • The officer checks the return and related particulars for correctness.
  • ASMT-10 communicates discrepancies and seeks explanation.
  • ASMT-11 is the taxpayer's response or payment/intimation.
  • ASMT-12 communicates acceptance/closure where the explanation or information is satisfactory.
  • An ASMT-10 is not itself a final tax demand.
  • A good response should be point-wise, numerical, documentary and period-specific.
  • GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, GSTR-2B, e-way bills and other available data can be relevant to scrutiny.
  • If the discrepancy remains unresolved, further audit, investigation or tax-determination action may follow under the applicable provisions.
  • Strong monthly GST reconciliation is the best practical preparation for Section 61 scrutiny.
