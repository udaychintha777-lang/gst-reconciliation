GST ACT — SECTION 99

Section 99 CGST Act — Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling

What happens when a taxpayer or GST officer disagrees with an advance ruling? Which authority hears the appeal? Is there a separate Central Appellate Authority for every State?

Section 99 identifies the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling (AAAR) for a State or Union territory. This guide explains its role, its connection with Sections 98, 100 and 101, the appeal structure and practical GST examples in simple language.

1. Why Section 99 matters

Section 98 deals with the original AAR process. Section 99 identifies the authority that handles the appellate stage.

An advance ruling can affect the GST treatment of a transaction, service, product, classification, exemption, valuation or tax liability. If an eligible party is aggrieved by the ruling, the law provides an appellate mechanism.

Section 99 is therefore the bridge between the original AAR ruling and the appellate stage governed by Sections 100 and 101.

Simple memory rule: Section 98 = AAR procedure and ruling. Section 99 = who is the Appellate Authority. Section 100 = appeal. Section 101 = AAAR order.

3. Section 99 in simple language

Suppose a company asks the Authority for Advance Ruling (AAR) whether a particular service is taxable. The AAR examines the application and gives an advance ruling under Section 98.

If the applicant or an eligible GST officer is aggrieved by that ruling, the GST law provides an appeal mechanism. Section 99 tells us which authority performs the appellate function for that State or Union territory.

AAR ruling → Aggrieved party → Appeal under Section 100 → AAAR identified under Section 99 → Order under Section 101

The key point is that Section 99 is primarily an authority-identification provision. It does not itself contain the complete appeal procedure.

4. Statutory structure

Section 99 provides, in substance, that subject to the provisions of the advance-ruling chapter, the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling constituted under the applicable State GST Act or Union Territory GST Act is deemed to be the Appellate Authority for the purposes of the CGST Act in that State or Union territory.

5. Who is the Appellate Authority?

The Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling is the authority constituted under the applicable State Goods and Services Tax Act or Union Territory Goods and Services Tax Act.

For CGST purposes, Section 99 gives that authority the deemed status of the Appellate Authority for the relevant State or Union territory.

This is important because the GST system has a dual structure. Central GST and State/UT GST operate together, and the advance-ruling appellate mechanism is designed around that corresponding framework.

State
The AAAR constituted under the relevant State GST law performs the appellate function for that State.
Union Territory
The corresponding AAAR constituted under the applicable UT GST law performs the appellate function for that Union territory.

6. Why State/UT GST law matters

Section 99 deliberately refers to an Appellate Authority constituted under a State GST Act or Union Territory GST Act.

Therefore, when studying an actual AAAR proceeding, finance teams should identify the relevant State or UT and examine the corresponding constitution of the authority under that law.

This is also why AAAR orders commonly identify the State/UT authority and may refer to corresponding CGST and SGST/UTGST provisions.

Practical point: Do not assume that an AAAR order from one State automatically becomes binding on every other State. The statutory binding framework for an advance ruling is separately governed by Section 103.

8. Section 99 and Section 100

These two provisions should be read together, but they do different jobs.

Section 99
Identifies the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling for the relevant State or Union territory.
Section 100
Provides the statutory route for an eligible person or officer to appeal against an advance ruling.

Section 100 also contains the statutory filing period. Therefore, a taxpayer should not read the 30-day appeal period as if it were contained in Section 99 itself.

9. Who can appeal?

Under Section 100, the concerned officer, jurisdictional officer or applicant aggrieved by an advance ruling pronounced under Section 98(4) may appeal to the Appellate Authority.

PersonWhy an appeal may arise
ApplicantThe AAR ruling may adversely affect the applicant's proposed or existing GST treatment.
Concerned officerThe officer may disagree with the AAR's conclusion.
Jurisdictional officerThe jurisdictional tax administration may consider the ruling incorrect and exercise the statutory appeal route.

This means the appellate mechanism is not exclusively a taxpayer remedy.

10. Appeal timeline

Section 100 generally requires an appeal to be filed within 30 days from the date on which the ruling is communicated to the concerned officer, jurisdictional officer and applicant.

The Appellate Authority may allow a further period of up to 30 days where it is satisfied that sufficient cause prevented the appellant from filing within the original period.

Ruling communicated: Record the communication date immediately.
First 30 days: Normal statutory appeal period.
Further period: Up to 30 additional days may be allowed where sufficient cause is established.
After filing: Track acknowledgement, hearing notices and submissions.
Do not wait until the last day. AAR/AAAR matters are legal proceedings. The complete ruling, application, facts and grounds should be reviewed before deciding the appeal strategy.

11. What happens at AAAR?

The appellate authority considers the appeal or reference according to the statutory framework. The parties are given an opportunity of being heard before the order is passed.

Section 101 provides that the Appellate Authority may pass an order it thinks fit, including confirming or modifying the ruling appealed against or referred to.

StagePractical activity
Appeal filedVerify form, grounds, fee and supporting documents.
Records reviewedAAAR considers the AAR ruling and relevant appeal material.
HearingAppellant and relevant parties present their submissions.
DecisionAAAR passes the statutory appellate order.

12. What if AAAR members disagree?

The appellate authority has a specific statutory mechanism for a disagreement between its members.

Where the members differ on the relevant question, the statutory framework deals with the consequence rather than automatically treating one member's view as the final ruling.

For finance teams, the practical lesson is simple: an AAAR proceeding should always be reviewed through the final statutory order and not through an isolated observation or one member's view.

13. Binding effect after AAAR

The fact that a matter reaches AAAR does not mean the resulting ruling becomes a universally binding GST rule for every taxpayer.

Section 103 governs the applicability of an advance ruling and generally connects its binding effect to the applicant who sought it and the concerned or jurisdictional officer in respect of that applicant, subject to the statutory conditions.

Important: An advance ruling should not be treated like a GST notification applicable to every business. AAR/AAAR rulings must be read in the context of the statutory binding provisions and the facts of the applicant.

14. Practical GST examples

Example 1 — Classification dispute

A manufacturer asks AAR to determine the classification of a specialised product. AAR gives a ruling that the manufacturer believes is incorrect. The applicant can evaluate the appeal route under Section 100, with the relevant AAAR identified under Section 99.

Example 2 — Taxability of a service

A company seeks a ruling on whether a service is taxable. The AAR holds that GST is payable. The company disagrees and considers an appeal within the statutory period.

Example 3 — ITC question

A business seeks an advance ruling on eligibility of ITC for a specific inward supply. If the AAR rejects the position, the applicant can examine the appellate remedy rather than treating the AAR ruling as the end of the process.

Example 4 — Notification benefit

A contractor seeks clarification on whether a particular exemption or concessional notification applies. An adverse AAR ruling can be taken through the prescribed appellate mechanism if the statutory requirements are met.

15. Construction and infrastructure examples

Government works contract

A contractor obtains an AAR ruling on the GST rate applicable to a government project and believes the conclusion is incorrect. The project team should immediately preserve the contract, tender, BOQ, work orders, invoices and the complete AAR order before evaluating an appeal.

HAM project

An infrastructure company asks an advance-ruling question involving a hybrid annuity structure. If the ruling affects valuation or taxability, the appeal team should analyse the exact contractual facts rather than relying on a general industry position.

Mining service

A mining contractor receives an adverse ruling on classification or taxability of a service. The appeal submission should map each activity to the exact question raised before AAR.

Subcontracting

A subcontractor receives a ruling concerning the GST treatment of its services. Before appealing, the finance team should reconcile the AAR facts with actual work orders, invoices and payment terms.

16. Detailed practical cases

Case 1 — Applicant disagrees

The applicant receives an adverse AAR ruling. The first action should be to record the communication date and calculate the Section 100 limitation period.

Case 2 — Officer disagrees

The concerned or jurisdictional officer may also exercise the statutory appeal route. The applicant should therefore monitor the complete appellate status.

Case 3 — Different State ruling

A taxpayer finds an AAAR ruling from another State supporting its position. That ruling may be useful for argument, but the taxpayer should not automatically assume that it is binding on its own State or facts.

Case 4 — Contract changed after ruling

If the commercial arrangement changes materially after an advance ruling, the original ruling should not automatically be assumed to cover the changed facts.

Case 5 — Appeal preparation

The appellant prepares grounds by identifying each finding in the AAR order, the factual assumption behind it and the statutory reason why that finding is disputed.

Case 6 — Hearing preparation

The finance/tax team prepares a concise written chronology, issue-wise arguments and supporting documents before the AAAR hearing.

Case 7 — Ruling implementation

After the final appellate order, the company updates tax configuration, invoice treatment, ITC controls and internal SOPs where the ruling is applicable.

Case 8 — Internal audit review

Internal audit checks whether the implemented GST treatment matches the actual facts and the final applicable ruling.

19. Common mistakes

  • Thinking Section 99 itself is the appeal form or limitation provision.
  • Confusing AAR with AAAR.
  • Missing the Section 100 appeal period.
  • Calculating limitation from an incorrect date.
  • Appealing without checking the exact facts recorded in the AAR order.
  • Using another State's ruling as if it were automatically binding.
  • Ignoring the corresponding State/UT GST framework.
  • Submitting generic grounds instead of challenging the specific findings.
  • Failing to preserve communication/service evidence.
  • Implementing an AAR/AAAR ruling beyond the applicant and circumstances to which the statutory binding provisions apply.

20. What Section 99 does not cover

Section 99 is not a general GST appellate provision for every dispute.

IssueSection 99?
Appeal against an advance rulingYes — Section 99 identifies the appellate authority.
General GST demand orderNo — separate appellate provisions apply.
Refund rejection orderNo — separate refund appeal framework applies.
Registration cancellation disputeNo — separate registration and appeal provisions apply.
Every order issued by a GST officerNo.
Always identify the type of order first. The correct appellate provision depends on the nature of the GST order or proceeding.

21. Current practical relevance

Section 99 remains practically relevant because GST Council records continue to publish AAR and appellate orders. Current appellate records include AAAR matters from multiple States, demonstrating that the advance-ruling appeal mechanism continues to operate in practice.

Recent appellate matters also show why Section 99 should be understood together with Section 100: an AAR ruling is not necessarily the final stage where an eligible party is aggrieved.

Practical takeaway: For every important AAR ruling, finance teams should maintain an AAR/AAAR register containing the applicant, GSTIN, State, question, AAR order date, communication date, appeal deadline, appeal status and final outcome.

22. Section 99 GST FAQs

What is Section 99 of the CGST Act?

Section 99 identifies the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling for a State or Union territory for CGST purposes.

What is AAAR in GST?

AAAR means Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling. It is the appellate authority dealing with appeals arising from advance rulings under the statutory framework.

Is AAAR a separate authority from AAR?

Yes. AAR is the original advance-ruling authority, while AAAR operates at the appellate stage.

Who can appeal against an AAR ruling?

The applicant, concerned officer or jurisdictional officer may appeal where the statutory requirements under Section 100 are satisfied.

What is the time limit for appeal?

Section 100 generally provides 30 days from communication of the ruling, with a possible further period not exceeding 30 days where sufficient cause is established.

Does Section 99 itself provide the 30-day time limit?

No. Section 99 identifies the Appellate Authority. The appeal period is provided in Section 100.

Can an AAAR confirm the AAR ruling?

Yes. Section 101 permits the Appellate Authority to pass an order confirming or modifying the ruling appealed against or referred to.

Can AAAR modify an AAR ruling?

Yes, subject to the statutory framework. Section 101 expressly contemplates confirmation or modification.

Is another State's AAAR ruling automatically binding?

No. An advance ruling's binding effect is governed by Section 103 and depends on the statutory conditions and the applicant/officer relationship.

Does Section 99 apply to ordinary GST disputes?

No. It is specifically part of the advance-ruling appellate framework.

What should a company do immediately after receiving an adverse AAR ruling?

Record the communication date, preserve the complete ruling and application, identify the disputed findings, assess Section 100 limitation and obtain appropriate professional advice before deciding the appeal.

24. Key takeaways

  • Section 99 deals with the Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling.
  • The AAAR is constituted under the applicable State GST or Union Territory GST law.
  • For CGST purposes, that authority is deemed to be the Appellate Authority for the relevant State or Union territory.
  • Section 99 should be read with Sections 98, 100 and 101.
  • Section 100 provides the appeal route and limitation period.
  • Section 101 deals with the appellate order, including confirmation or modification of the ruling.
  • An AAAR ruling should not automatically be treated as a universal GST rule for every taxpayer.
  • Finance teams should maintain an AAR/AAAR register and track communication dates and appeal deadlines.
