1. Why Section 34 matters

Searches such as “Section 34 CGST Act”, “GST credit note”, “GST credit note time limit”, “credit note GST 30 November”, “GST debit note”, “post sale discount credit note” and “ITC reversal on credit note” all point to the same practical compliance issue: how should GST be corrected when the original invoice no longer reflects the correct value or tax?

Section 34 provides the statutory mechanism for credit notes and debit notes. It is therefore one of the most important provisions for correcting invoice-level GST after the original tax invoice has been issued.

A credit note generally deals with a reduction in taxable value or tax, goods returned, deficiencies and other situations specifically recognised by the law. A debit note operates in the opposite direction where the original taxable value or tax was lower than the amount actually payable.

Simple rule: do not use a credit note or debit note merely because an accounting entry needs to be changed. First identify the statutory reason, then issue the prescribed document, report it correctly and reconcile the tax effect.

2. What Section 34 covers

Section 34 has four principal parts:

ProvisionCore subject
34(1)When a registered supplier may issue one or more credit notes for supplies made in a financial year.
34(2)Declaration/reporting of credit notes and adjustment of tax liability, including the current recipient ITC-reversal condition.
34(3)When the supplier is required to issue one or more debit notes because the original taxable value or tax was lower than payable.
34(4)Declaration/reporting of debit notes and adjustment of tax liability.

The current CBIC tax-information text reflects the 2018 changes allowing one or more credit/debit notes for supplies made in a financial year, the 2022 extension of the credit-note reporting deadline to 30 November, and the 2025 recipient-ITC-reversal condition. citeturn2search0

3. Complete statutory structure

Section 34(1) — Credit note

Where one or more tax invoices have been issued and the taxable value or tax charged exceeds the taxable value or tax payable, or goods are returned, or goods/services are deficient, the registered supplier may issue one or more credit notes containing the prescribed particulars. The current text also contains a 2026 amendment concerning specified post-sale discounts, but that amendment is stated by CBIC as not yet notified and therefore is not treated as operative in this article. citeturn2search0

Section 34(2) — Reporting and tax adjustment

A credit note must be declared in the return for the month in which it is issued, subject to the statutory outer deadline: 30 November following the end of the financial year of the original supply, or the date of furnishing the relevant annual return, whichever is earlier. citeturn2search0

Section 34(3) — Debit note

Where the taxable value or tax charged in the original invoice is less than the taxable value or tax payable, the registered supplier shall issue one or more debit notes containing prescribed particulars.

Section 34(4) — Debit note reporting

The debit note is declared in the return for the month in which it is issued, and the tax liability is adjusted as prescribed.

4. What is a GST credit note?

A GST credit note is a document issued by a registered supplier under Section 34 when the original invoice has resulted in an excess taxable value or tax, goods are returned, goods/services are deficient, or another legally recognised credit-note situation applies.

It is important to distinguish three concepts:

Commercial credit note
A commercial/accounting document may adjust a receivable, but it does not automatically reduce GST liability.
GST credit note
A document issued under Section 34 with the prescribed particulars and GST reporting treatment.
Accounting reversal
An internal journal entry does not by itself create the statutory tax adjustment available under Section 34.
Refund
A refund is a separate statutory mechanism and is not automatically the same thing as issuing a credit note.

5. When can a credit note be issued?

Section 34(1) recognises the following core situations:

SituationTypical example
Taxable value charged is too highInvoice taxable value ₹10 lakh but the correct value is ₹9 lakh.
Tax charged is too highGST was calculated at a higher amount than legally payable.
Goods returnedRecipient returns part of the goods after the original invoice.
Goods deficientDefective/short-supplied goods result in a price/tax adjustment.
Services deficientService quality/scope deficiency results in an eligible reduction.
Specified post-sale discountCurrent Section 15/34 treatment must be checked for the transaction date and the applicable statutory conditions.

The exact legal effect depends on whether the credit note is being used merely for a commercial adjustment or to reduce the supplier's GST output tax liability.

6. Excess taxable value or tax

Suppose a supplier invoices:

ParticularOriginalCorrect
Taxable value₹10,00,000₹9,00,000
GST @ 18%₹1,80,000₹1,62,000
Excess GST₹18,000

A Section 34 credit note can be used where the statutory conditions are satisfied. The supplier should then report the credit note and make the corresponding tax adjustment through the prescribed return mechanism.

Do not simply reduce the next invoice. The original invoice, credit note, return reporting and recipient ITC treatment should all reconcile.

7. Goods returned by the recipient

Return of goods is one of the expressly recognised Section 34(1) situations.

For example, a supplier sells 1,000 units and invoices GST. The recipient returns 100 units because they are not required or do not meet the agreed specifications. Where the legal requirements are satisfied, the supplier may issue a credit note for the returned portion.

Controls should include:

  • original invoice reference;
  • quantity returned;
  • reason for return;
  • return delivery documentation;
  • credit-note taxable value;
  • GST amount adjusted; and
  • recipient's corresponding accounting/ITC treatment.

8. Deficient goods or services

Section 34 also covers situations where goods or services supplied are found to be deficient.

The word “deficient” should not be treated as a blanket authorisation for every commercial price negotiation. The credit note should have a genuine connection with the original supply and the relevant statutory conditions.

Example

A contractor completes work subject to agreed quality standards. ₹50 lakh is originally billed, but an identified deficiency results in a contractually agreed reduction of ₹2 lakh. The GST treatment of that reduction must be examined under Section 34 and the valuation provisions before issuing a GST credit note.

9. Post-sale discount and Section 34

Post-sale discounts require special care because Section 15 and Section 34 interact.

The GST Council in September 2025 recommended changes to Section 15(3)(b) and Section 34 so that post-sale discounts could be given through a credit note with corresponding recipient ITC reversal, while removing certain earlier requirements concerning pre-existing agreements and invoice linkage. citeturn0search6

Current-law caution: a recommendation or enacted-but-not-commenced amendment is not automatically the law applicable today. The transaction date and commencement notification must be checked before applying a post-sale discount mechanism.

As of the current statutory material reviewed for this article, the 2026 Section 34(1) insertion referring to a discount under Section 15(3)(b) is specifically marked by CBIC as “w.e.f. yet to be notified.” Therefore this article does not present that insertion as operative law. citeturn2search0

10. GST credit note time limit — is there a 30 November deadline?

Yes, for the Section 34(2) tax adjustment/reporting mechanism, the current statutory outer date is 30 November following the end of the financial year in which the original supply was made, or the date of furnishing the relevant annual return, whichever is earlier. citeturn2search0

Original supplyCurrent outer date for Section 34(2) credit-note reporting
FY 2025-2630 November 2026, subject to the annual-return-earlier condition.
FY 2026-2730 November 2027, subject to the annual-return-earlier condition.

The important distinction is between:

  • issuance of a credit note as a commercial/GST document; and
  • the statutory time limit for declaring the credit note and obtaining the corresponding reduction in output tax liability under Section 34(2).

Do not automatically state that a GST credit note “cannot be issued after 30 November” without explaining this distinction. The statutory deadline specifically governs the return declaration and tax adjustment under Section 34(2).

11. Credit note reporting and tax adjustment

Section 34(2) requires the supplier to declare the credit note in the return for the month in which the credit note is issued, subject to the statutory outer deadline.

Therefore, a credit-note control should connect:

Original invoice
Invoice number, date, value and tax.
Credit note
Reason, value, rate and tax adjustment.
Return reporting
Credit note reported in the appropriate return period.
Recipient ITC
Recipient's corresponding ITC treatment where applicable.

The GST Council's 2022 clarification confirmed that the extended 30 November compliance deadline applies to the specified compliances from FY 2021-22 onwards. citeturn1search7turn1search4

12. Recipient ITC reversal — important change from 1 October 2025

Section 34(2) was changed with effect from 1 October 2025. The current provision states that reduction in the supplier's output tax liability is not permitted if:

  • the input tax credit attributable to the credit note, if availed, has not been reversed by the registered recipient; or
  • in other cases, the incidence of tax on the supply has been passed on to another person.

This is a major practical change because the supplier's output-tax reduction is now directly connected to the recipient-side ITC reversal where the recipient is a registered person. The current CBIC statutory text identifies this amendment as section 126 of the Finance (No. 7) Act, 2025, effective 1 October 2025. citeturn2search0

Practical control: from 1 October 2025 onward, credit-note workflows should capture evidence of recipient ITC reversal where applicable before treating the corresponding supplier output-tax reduction as fully compliant.

13. What is a GST debit note?

A debit note is the statutory document used where the taxable value or tax charged in the original tax invoice is less than the taxable value or tax payable for the supply.

In simple terms:

Original invoiceCorrect positionDocument
Value/tax too highReduceCredit note
Value/tax too lowIncreaseDebit note

Section 34(3) says the registered supplier shall issue one or more debit notes where the original taxable value or tax charged is less than the taxable value or tax payable. citeturn2search0

14. When is a debit note required?

Typical situations include:

  • additional quantity supplied but omitted from the original invoice;
  • taxable value originally understated;
  • additional contractual consideration becoming payable;
  • GST rate was applied lower than legally applicable;
  • certain post-invoice valuation adjustments increase taxable value; and
  • any other situation where the taxable value or tax originally charged is less than the amount legally payable.

Example

Original invoice taxable value is ₹10 lakh at 18%, but the correct taxable value is ₹11 lakh. Additional taxable value is ₹1 lakh and additional GST is ₹18,000. Subject to the facts, a Section 34(3) debit note is required for the additional amount.

15. Debit note as supplementary invoice

The Explanation to Section 34 provides that the expression “debit note” includes a supplementary invoice.

This is important where an additional amount becomes payable after the original invoice. The debit note is not merely a commercial memo; it forms part of the statutory GST documentation and tax-adjustment process.

The document should contain the prescribed particulars and should be linked clearly to the underlying supply.

16. Financial-year-wise credit and debit notes

Since the 2018 amendment, Section 34 permits one or more credit notes and debit notes for supplies made in a financial year.

This facilitates practical consolidation rather than forcing the supplier to create a separate credit/debit note for every individual invoice in every situation, subject to the prescribed documentation and reporting requirements.

However, consolidation should not destroy the audit trail. A professional system should maintain invoice-level mapping even where the statutory document covers multiple supplies.

For ERP purposes, use a credit/debit note register with original invoice references, recipient GSTIN, financial year, taxable value, tax heads, reason and reporting period.

17. Rule 53 — particulars of credit and debit notes

Rule 53 prescribes the particulars for revised invoices and credit/debit notes. The CBIC Rules identify, among other requirements:

  • nature of the document;
  • supplier name, address and GSTIN;
  • unique consecutive serial number;
  • date of issue;
  • recipient GSTIN/UIN where applicable;
  • recipient details for unregistered recipients as prescribed;
  • serial number and date of the corresponding tax invoice or bill of supply;
  • taxable value;
  • tax rate;
  • amount of tax credited or debited; and
  • signature/digital signature of the supplier or authorised representative.

The current CBIC invoice-rules page lists these credit/debit-note particulars. citeturn0search0turn0search5

18. Credit note and recipient ITC

A credit note that reduces taxable value or tax can affect the recipient's input tax credit. This is why supplier and recipient records should be reconciled.

For example:

OriginalAmount
Taxable value₹10,00,000
GST @ 18%₹1,80,000
Credit note taxable value₹1,00,000
Credit-note GST₹18,000

If the recipient had claimed ITC of ₹1,80,000 on the original invoice, the credit-note adjustment may require corresponding reduction of ITC, subject to the applicable statutory requirements.

The 2025 Section 34(2) amendment makes this relationship especially important for the supplier's output-tax reduction from 1 October 2025. citeturn2search0

19. Credit notes to unregistered recipients

Credit-note situations involving unregistered recipients require careful treatment because the recipient may not have an electronic ITC reversal mechanism.

The GST framework has also addressed situations where an unregistered buyer seeks refund of tax paid in cases such as cancellation/termination of certain construction contracts or long-term insurance arrangements after the Section 34 credit-note period has expired.

The GST Council's 48th-meeting agenda material records the practical problem and the subsequent mechanism for refund claims by unregistered persons in specified circumstances. citeturn1search4

Do not generalise this into a universal refund right. The unregistered-person refund mechanism is subject to its own statutory conditions and prescribed procedure.

20. Construction and RA-bill examples

Section 34 is especially important in construction and infrastructure businesses because contract values are frequently adjusted after RA bills, certification, measurement, quality assessment, retention, recoveries and final account settlement.

Example — overbilling in RA bill

ParticularOriginalCorrect
Certified taxable value₹1,00,00,000₹98,00,000
GST @ 18%₹18,00,000₹17,64,000
Excess GST₹36,000

Where the statutory conditions are met, the supplier may issue a credit note for the reduction. The accounts team should maintain the measurement/certification document, original RA bill, credit note, recipient acknowledgement and tax-return evidence.

Example — additional work

Additional work of ₹5 lakh is certified after the original invoice. If the amount represents additional taxable consideration for the original supply, the supplier should analyse whether a debit note under Section 34(3) is required and report the additional tax accordingly.

Retention

Retention money should not automatically be treated as a credit-note item. The time of supply, valuation, contractual terms and actual taxability must be analysed separately.

21. Cancellation and long-term contracts

Contract cancellation can create a timing problem when the Section 34 credit-note reporting period has expired.

The GST Council's 48th-meeting material records examples involving:

  • construction contracts cancelled because of non-completion or delay; and
  • long-term insurance policies terminated prematurely.

Where the Section 34 period had expired, a separate refund route for eligible unregistered recipients was developed subject to prescribed conditions. Where the credit-note period had not expired, the supplier could issue a credit note and refund the relevant tax subject to the applicable rules. citeturn1search4

22. Wrong tax / wrong tax-head situations

A credit note is not a universal cure for every GST mistake.

ProblemFirst question
Excess taxable valueDoes Section 34(1) permit a credit note?
Excess tax rateWas excess tax actually charged/collected and how should it be corrected?
CGST + SGST charged instead of IGSTCheck the special wrong-tax-head provisions, particularly Section 77 and the IGST Act.
IGST charged instead of CGST + SGSTAnalyse the place-of-supply correction mechanism.
GST charged on exempt supplyDetermine whether Section 34, refund or another statutory mechanism applies.
GST collected but not paidSection 76 and penalty/recovery provisions may apply.
Key point: first identify the legal error. Only then choose a credit note, debit note, refund or wrong-tax-head correction mechanism.

23. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — excess invoice value

A supplier invoices ₹12 lakh instead of ₹10 lakh. The excess ₹2 lakh is established as an error. A credit note may be appropriate subject to Section 34 conditions.

Example 2 — sales return

Goods worth ₹3 lakh are returned by the customer. The supplier issues the appropriate credit note and adjusts the corresponding tax, subject to the statutory conditions.

Example 3 — defective goods

Goods supplied are deficient and the customer receives a ₹50,000 price reduction. The supplier examines Section 34 and issues the prescribed credit note if eligible.

Example 4 — additional quantity

Goods worth ₹2 lakh were omitted from the original invoice. A debit note may be required for the additional taxable value and tax.

Example 5 — wrong GST rate

GST was charged at 18% instead of 12%. The supplier must determine the correct correction route rather than automatically assuming that a credit note alone solves the entire issue.

Example 6 — post-sale discount

A customer receives a year-end discount. The supplier must examine Section 15 and the Section 34 treatment applicable on the transaction date. The 2025 GST Council recommendation and 2026 enacted-but-not-notified amendment should not be applied prematurely.

Example 7 — credit note after 30 November

A supplier identifies an old invoice after the statutory Section 34(2) reporting deadline. The tax-adjustment consequences must be distinguished from the commercial/documentation question. The supplier should not assume an output-tax reduction is available after the statutory deadline.

Example 8 — recipient has claimed ITC

A supplier issues a credit note reducing GST by ₹18,000. From 1 October 2025, the supplier's output-tax reduction is subject to the current Section 34(2) condition concerning reversal of attributable recipient ITC where applicable.

Example 9 — debit note issued later

A final measurement establishes additional taxable consideration. A debit note can be issued and reported in the return for the month in which it is issued; Section 34 does not prescribe the same annual outer reporting deadline for debit notes that it prescribes for credit-note tax reduction.

Example 10 — consolidated credit note

A supplier has multiple invoices relating to supplies made in a financial year and eligible adjustments. The 2018 amendment permits one or more credit notes for supplies made in a financial year, subject to prescribed particulars and adequate invoice-level audit trail.

25. GST Council background

25th GST Council — delinking credit/debit notes from individual invoices

The 25th GST Council agenda considered allowing credit notes and debit notes to be issued on a consolidated basis for supplies made in a financial year rather than forcing a one-to-one document for every invoice. This proposal became part of the 2018 amendment architecture. citeturn0search2

48th GST Council — unregistered recipient refund problem

The Council considered cases where a construction agreement or long-term insurance policy was cancelled after the Section 34 credit-note period had expired, resulting in a refund mechanism for eligible unregistered persons. citeturn1search4

53rd GST Council — credit/debit note system changes

The 53rd Council agenda included changes relating to credit/debit notes and the GST system's invoice functionality. citeturn0search3

55th GST Council — recipient ITC reversal

The GST Council recommended a specific amendment to Section 34(2) to provide for reversal of input tax credit attributable to a credit note by the recipient. This recommendation was subsequently reflected in the statutory change effective 1 October 2025. citeturn0search7turn2search0

56th GST Council — post-sale discounts

The September 2025 Council recommended changes to Section 15 and Section 34 concerning post-sale discounts. Because the 2026 Section 34 insertion is currently marked “yet to be notified”, this article does not treat that insertion as operative. citeturn0search6turn2search0

26. Current 2025–2026 changes — what businesses need to know

The most important currently operative recent change is the amendment to Section 34(2) effective 1 October 2025.

IssueCurrent position
Credit-note outer reporting date30 November following the relevant financial year, or annual return date, whichever is earlier.
Registered recipient ITCSupplier's output-tax reduction is not permitted if attributable ITC has not been reversed by the registered recipient.
Other casesReduction is restricted where the incidence of tax has been passed on to another person.
Debit notesReported in the return for the month in which issued; Section 34(4) does not impose the same 30 November outer date.

This makes supplier-recipient reconciliation more important than before. A credit-note register should now include an ITC-reversal status for registered customers where relevant.

27. 2026 enacted-but-not-yet-notified change

Finance Act 2026 inserted language into Section 34(1) referring to a discount under Section 15(3)(b). However, the current CBIC statutory database expressly states that this insertion is “w.e.f. yet to be notified.” citeturn2search0

Do not apply this provision as current law until its commencement is notified. This distinction is important for a legal reference article intended to remain accurate over time.

The GST Council's September 2025 recommendation proposed a simplified post-sale discount framework involving credit notes and recipient ITC reversal. The final operative law must be determined from the notified commencement and the statutory text applicable on the transaction date. citeturn0search6

31. Common mistakes

  • Calling every commercial adjustment a GST credit note.
  • Issuing a credit note without identifying the statutory reason.
  • Missing the Section 34(2) 30 November/annual-return earlier deadline.
  • Assuming a credit note automatically reduces output tax even when the statutory conditions are not satisfied.
  • Ignoring recipient ITC reversal for credit notes covered by the current Section 34(2) condition.
  • Using a debit note for a situation that actually requires correction of the original invoice or a different statutory mechanism.
  • Failing to link the credit/debit note to the original invoice or supply.
  • Not reconciling credit notes with GSTR-1 and output-tax liability.
  • Ignoring the difference between commercial credit note and GST credit note.
  • Applying the 2026 post-sale discount amendment before its commencement is notified.
  • Assuming a credit note can correct any wrong CGST/SGST/IGST tax-head issue.
  • Failing to retain supporting contract, return, quality, delivery and approval records.

32. Section 34 GST FAQs

What is Section 34 of the CGST Act?

Section 34 provides the GST framework for credit notes and debit notes where the original taxable value or tax requires a statutory adjustment, or where goods are returned or goods/services are deficient.

What is a GST credit note?

A GST credit note is a document issued by a registered supplier under Section 34 where the statutory conditions for reducing taxable value or tax are satisfied.

What is the GST credit note time limit?

For reducing output tax liability under Section 34(2), the credit note must be declared no later than 30 November following the end of the financial year of the original supply, or the date of furnishing the relevant annual return, whichever is earlier.

Can a credit note be issued after 30 November?

The statutory 30 November rule specifically governs declaration and adjustment of tax liability under Section 34(2). The commercial/documentation question should be distinguished from the availability of output-tax reduction.

What is the difference between a credit note and debit note in GST?

A credit note is generally used where the original taxable value or tax is higher than legally payable or in other specified reduction situations. A debit note is required where the original taxable value or tax is lower than legally payable.

Is a debit note subject to the 30 November deadline?

Section 34(4) requires declaration of a debit note in the return for the month in which it is issued. Section 34 does not impose the same 30 November outer deadline on debit notes that applies to credit-note tax adjustment under Section 34(2).

What changed in Section 34 from 1 October 2025?

The supplier's reduction in output tax liability on a credit note is subject to reversal of attributable ITC by a registered recipient where that ITC was availed, along with the separate protection against reduction where tax incidence has been passed on in other cases.

Can a GST credit note be issued for post-sale discount?

The answer depends on the transaction date and the statutory conditions applicable at that time. The 2025 GST Council recommended changes, and Finance Act 2026 inserted further wording that CBIC currently marks as not yet notified.

What are the reasons for issuing a GST credit note?

Core Section 34(1) situations include excess taxable value or tax, return of goods and deficiency in goods or services. Other specific statutory treatment must be checked separately.

What is Rule 53 of GST?

Rule 53 prescribes particulars for revised invoices and credit/debit notes, including document number/date, supplier and recipient details, original invoice reference, taxable value, tax rate and tax amount.

Does a credit note reduce the recipient's ITC?

It can. Where a credit note reduces the tax component of an original supply and the recipient had availed corresponding ITC, the recipient's ITC treatment must be adjusted according to the applicable law.

Can a credit note correct wrong IGST instead of CGST/SGST?

Do not assume so. Wrong-tax-head situations should be analysed under the specific provisions dealing with wrongly paid Central/State tax and inter-State tax.

Can a credit note be issued to an unregistered person?

The document and tax-adjustment treatment must be analysed under Section 34 and the applicable Rules. Separate refund mechanisms may apply in specified cases involving unregistered recipients.

34. Key takeaways

  • Section 34 is the principal GST provision for credit notes and debit notes.
  • Credit notes address specified reductions such as excess taxable value/tax, returns and deficiencies.
  • Debit notes are required where the original taxable value or tax is lower than legally payable.
  • The current Section 34(2) credit-note reporting deadline is 30 November following the relevant financial year, or annual-return date, whichever is earlier.
  • From 1 October 2025, supplier output-tax reduction is linked to recipient ITC reversal where applicable.
  • Debit notes do not have the same 30 November outer reporting deadline under Section 34(4).
  • Rule 53 contains the detailed credit/debit-note particulars.
  • A commercial credit note and a GST credit note are not automatically the same thing.
  • Wrong-tax-head cases should be analysed under the specific correction provisions rather than automatically using Section 34.
  • The 2026 Section 34 post-sale-discount insertion should not be treated as operative until its commencement is notified.
  • For construction and infrastructure businesses, credit/debit-note controls should be integrated with RA bills, measurement sheets, contract variations, retention and final-account reconciliation.
