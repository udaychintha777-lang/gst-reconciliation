CGST Act Section 169 – Service of Notice in Certain Circumstances

Section 169 explains how GST decisions, orders, summonses, notices and other communications may be served, including direct service, post, email, the common portal, newspaper publication and affixation.

3. Statutory Text of Section 169

169. Service of notice in certain circumstances.— (1) Any decision, order, summons, notice or other communication under this Act or the rules made thereunder shall be served by any one of the following methods, namely:— (a) by giving or tendering it directly or by a messenger including a courier to the addressee or the taxable person or to his manager or authorised representative or an advocate or a tax practitioner holding authority to appear in the proceedings on behalf of the taxable person or to a person regularly employed by him in connection with the business, or to any adult member of family residing with the taxable person; or (b) by registered post or speed post or courier with acknowledgement due, to the person for whom it is intended or his authorised representative, if any, at his last known place of business or residence; or (c) by sending a communication to his e-mail address provided at the time of registration or as amended from time to time; or (d) by making it available on the common portal; or (e) by publication in a newspaper circulating in the locality in which the taxable person or the person to whom it is issued is last known to have resided, carried on business or personally worked for gain; or (f) if none of the modes aforesaid is practicable, by affixing it in some conspicuous place at his last known place of business or residence and if such mode is not practicable for any reason, then by affixing a copy thereof on the notice board of the office of the concerned officer or authority who or which passed such decision or order or issued such summons or notice. (2) Every decision, order, summons, notice or any communication shall be deemed to have been served on the date on which it is tendered or published or a copy thereof is affixed in the manner provided in sub-section (1). (3) When such decision, order, summons, notice or any communication is sent by registered post or speed post, it shall be deemed to have been received by the addressee at the expiry of the period normally taken by such post in transit unless the contrary is proved.
Core point: Section 169 provides statutory service methods. It does not by itself determine whether the underlying notice, order or decision is substantively valid.

4. Meaning in Simple Language

A GST proceeding often begins with a communication: a notice, summons, order, decision or another official document. Section 169 provides the legal routes by which that communication may be served.

The section is deliberately broad. It recognises traditional delivery methods as well as electronic communication. It also provides fallback methods where earlier methods are not practicable.

Why this matters to taxpayers: Do not assume that a GST communication is legally relevant only when a physical envelope is received. Section 169 expressly includes email and making the communication available on the common portal.

5. Six Modes of Service Under Section 169(1)

ClauseModePractical description
(a)Direct/tendered serviceDirectly or through a messenger/courier to specified persons including the addressee, taxable person, authorised representative, advocate, tax practitioner, employee or adult family member in the circumstances stated.
(b)Registered/speed post/courier with acknowledgement dueTo the person or authorised representative at the last known place of business or residence.
(c)EmailTo the email address provided at registration or amended from time to time.
(d)Common portalBy making the communication available on the common portal.
(e)NewspaperPublication in a newspaper circulating in the locality specified by the section.
(f)AffixationIf none of the earlier modes is practicable, affixation at the last known business/residence and, if that is also not practicable, on the concerned office notice board.

6. Direct Service – Section 169(1)(a)

The first mode permits giving or tendering the communication directly or through a messenger, including a courier.

The provision identifies several persons to whom service may be made in the circumstances stated:

  • the addressee or taxable person;
  • manager;
  • authorised representative;
  • advocate;
  • tax practitioner holding authority to appear in the proceedings;
  • person regularly employed in connection with the business;
  • adult member of the family residing with the taxable person.
Practical point: The exact facts matter. A company should document who received the communication, their role, date, delivery method and the document delivered.

7. Registered Post, Speed Post and Courier – Section 169(1)(b)

Section 169(1)(b) permits service by registered post, speed post or courier with acknowledgement due.

The communication is addressed to the person for whom it is intended or the authorised representative, if any, at the last known place of business or residence.

Keep the envelopeRetain the envelope, acknowledgement, tracking details and complete document where available.
Record the addressCompare the address used with the last known business/residence information relevant to the proceeding.
Record datesCapture dispatch, delivery/receipt evidence and the statutory service date used for the proceeding.

8. Email Service – Section 169(1)(c)

Email is expressly recognised as a statutory mode of service. The communication may be sent to the taxpayer's email address provided at the time of registration or amended from time to time.

Compliance lesson: GST registration email addresses should be monitored continuously. Changes in responsible personnel do not by themselves change the statutory email address maintained on the GST registration.

Recommended controls

  • Monitor the registered GST email account daily.
  • Use an internal forwarding/alert system where permitted.
  • Maintain authorised access and continuity during employee changes.
  • Archive official GST emails and attachments.
  • Record the date and time the communication was identified internally.

9. Common Portal Service – Section 169(1)(d)

The section expressly permits service by making the communication available on the common portal.

This is particularly important for modern GST compliance because businesses may receive statutory communications through electronic GST workflows rather than traditional paper delivery.

ControlRecommended practice
Login monitoringAssign responsibility for checking GST portal communications.
Download evidenceSave the complete notice/order and relevant attachments.
Date recordRecord when the communication became available and when it was downloaded internally.
Deadline trackerImmediately enter response/payment/appeal dates into the compliance calendar after legal review.
Important: Internal “download date” and the statutory date of service are not necessarily the same legal concept. The applicable service rule and facts should be examined.

10. Newspaper Publication – Section 169(1)(e)

Section 169 includes publication in a newspaper circulating in the locality in which the taxable person or other person concerned is last known to have resided, carried on business or personally worked for gain.

This is one of the statutory service mechanisms available under the Act. For a specific case, the facts surrounding publication and the statutory requirements should be verified.

11. Affixation – Section 169(1)(f)

Affixation is a further fallback method. The provision comes into play if none of the earlier modes is practicable.

The section provides for affixing the communication in a conspicuous place at the person's last known place of business or residence. If that mode is also not practicable, a copy may be affixed on the notice board of the office of the concerned officer or authority that passed the decision/order or issued the summons/notice.

Important wording: Clause (f) expressly refers to the earlier modes being impracticable. The statutory sequence should therefore be read carefully in any dispute concerning affixation.

12. Deemed Service and Postal Receipt

Section 169(2)

Every decision, order, summons, notice or communication is deemed to have been served on the date on which it is tendered, published or a copy is affixed in the manner provided in sub-section (1).

Section 169(3)

Where the communication is sent by registered post or speed post, it is deemed to have been received by the addressee at the expiry of the period normally taken by that post in transit, unless the contrary is proved.

ProvisionKey concept
169(1)Permitted modes of service.
169(2)Deemed service date for tendering, publication or affixation in the prescribed manner.
169(3)Postal deeming rule for registered/speed post, subject to contrary proof.
Why the date matters: A service date can affect the time available for a reply, payment, appeal or other procedural action. Therefore, notice management should capture service evidence immediately.

13. Practical Examples

Example 1 – Direct delivery

A GST officer or messenger tenders a notice directly to the taxable person. The company should record the date, recipient and document received.

Example 2 – Authorised representative

A communication is tendered to an authorised representative who is authorised to appear in the proceeding. The service facts should be documented.

Example 3 – Registered post

A notice is sent by registered post to the last known place of business. Section 169(3) contains a statutory deemed-receipt rule for registered/speed post, unless the contrary is proved.

Example 4 – GST email

A communication is sent to the email address provided at GST registration. The finance team should monitor that account and preserve the original email and attachment.

Example 5 – Common portal

An order is made available on the common portal. The company should download the complete document and enter the matter into its notice tracker.

Example 6 – Newspaper

Where the statutory circumstances permit newspaper publication, the publication can constitute a mode of service under Section 169(1)(e).

Example 7 – Affixation

If earlier service modes are not practicable, the provision permits the specified affixation procedure.

Example 8 – Employee receives notice

A person regularly employed in connection with the business receives a communication under the direct-service method. The company should record the employee, role and date of receipt.

Example 9 – Multiple GST registrations

A group company with several GSTINs should route each portal notice to the correct GSTIN-specific compliance owner.

Example 10 – Employee leaves the company

If a former employee controlled the GST email account, the company should immediately update internal access and ensure the registered GST email is actively monitored.

Example 11 – Appeal deadline

If an order is served, the legal team should determine the statutory appeal period from the legally applicable service date rather than simply using the internal download date.

Example 12 – Notice register

A tax department can maintain document number, type, GSTIN, service mode, service date, response deadline, responsible person and closure status.

Example 13 – Service dispute

If a taxpayer disputes service, preserve portal logs, emails, postal records, tracking information, envelopes and internal receipt records for legal review.

Example 14 – Notice versus validity

Even if service is established, separate questions may remain about jurisdiction, limitation, statutory authority or merits. Section 169 primarily addresses service.

14. GST Notice Management Workflow

  1. Monitor all channels: GST portal, registered email, physical mail and relevant internal communication channels.
  2. Identify the document: notice, order, summons, decision or other communication.
  3. Capture service evidence: save portal availability, email, postal tracking, acknowledgement or other relevant evidence.
  4. Record service date: do not confuse internal download date with statutory service date.
  5. Read the proceeding deadline: identify reply, payment or appeal timelines.
  6. Assign owner: nominate finance/tax/legal responsibility.
  7. Check Section 169 mode: identify the statutory service route used.
  8. Review merits separately: service validity is only one aspect of the proceeding.
  9. Preserve the file: retain the complete communication and evidence for audit and litigation.

16. Common Mistakes

  • Assuming a GST notice is validly served only through physical post.
  • Ignoring the registered GST email address.
  • Not monitoring the GST common portal.
  • Confusing internal download date with statutory service date.
  • Failing to preserve email, portal, postal or delivery evidence.
  • Ignoring the statutory deemed-service rules.
  • Assuming a service dispute automatically decides the merits of the underlying notice.
  • Failing to check whether an authorised representative had authority to receive/appear in the proceeding.
  • Using only one employee's inbox to monitor all GST communications.
  • Not maintaining a central notice tracker for multiple GST registrations.
  • Failing to check historical service evidence when an old order is under appeal or litigation.

18. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 169 of the CGST Act?

Section 169 prescribes methods for serving decisions, orders, summonses, notices and other communications under the CGST Act or rules made under it.

2. How many service methods are listed in Section 169(1)?

Six broad methods are listed: direct/tendered service, registered/speed post or courier with acknowledgement due, email, common portal, newspaper publication, and affixation when earlier modes are not practicable.

3. Can a GST notice be served by email?

Yes. Section 169(1)(c) expressly includes sending a communication to the email address provided at registration or amended from time to time.

4. Can a GST communication be made available on the common portal?

Yes. Section 169(1)(d) expressly includes making it available on the common portal.

5. What happens if direct service is not practicable?

The section provides additional methods, including newspaper publication and, if none of the earlier modes is practicable, affixation at the last known place of business or residence and, if that is also not practicable, on the concerned officer's office notice board.

6. When is a communication deemed served under Section 169(2)?

It is deemed served on the date on which it is tendered, published or a copy is affixed in the manner provided in sub-section (1).

7. What is the postal deeming rule in Section 169(3)?

For registered post or speed post, the communication is deemed to have been received at the expiry of the period normally taken by the post in transit, unless the contrary is proved.

8. Does Section 169 apply only to notices?

No. It expressly covers decisions, orders, summonses, notices and other communications under the Act or rules.

9. Can service on an authorised representative be valid?

Section 169(1)(a) and (b) expressly refer to an authorised representative in the circumstances specified there.

10. Can service be made on an advocate or tax practitioner?

Under the direct-service method in clause (a), the section expressly includes an advocate or tax practitioner holding authority to appear in the proceedings on behalf of the taxable person.

11. What should a company do when a GST notice appears on the portal?

Record the date it became available, download the complete document, identify the proceeding and response deadline, and preserve the portal/electronic evidence. The exact legal effect of service should be assessed from the applicable facts.

12. Can a company ignore an email because it did not open it?

The statutory service framework should not be reduced to whether an email was personally opened. Section 169(1)(c) provides for sending to the registered/amended email address, and the precise service issue depends on the facts and applicable law.

13. What is the difference between service and actual reading?

Service is a statutory legal concept. Whether the recipient actually read or understood a communication can be a different factual question.

14. Why is the date of service important?

Many GST proceedings have response, payment or appeal timelines. Establishing the legally relevant service date can therefore be critical.

15. Does Section 169 decide the validity of every notice?

No. It deals with service methods and deemed service. The validity of the underlying notice may involve separate questions such as jurisdiction, statutory authority, limitation, reasons and natural justice.

16. Should businesses maintain a GST notice register?

Yes. A notice register can record document type, reference number, date visible on the document, date of service, mode, response deadline, responsible person and closure status.

17. Does Section 169 apply to State GST notices?

The CGST provision governs communications under the CGST Act or rules made thereunder. State GST Acts generally contain corresponding provisions; the exact State provision should be checked for State GST proceedings.

18. Is this article legal advice?

No. It is an educational explanation. For a particular service dispute, notice, appeal limitation or litigation matter, verify the exact document, service evidence, applicable provision and judicial interpretation.

19. Quick Revision – Section 169

  • Subject: Service of notice in certain circumstances.
  • Coverage: Decision, order, summons, notice and other communication under the Act/rules.
  • Modes: Direct service; registered/speed post/courier with acknowledgement due; email; common portal; newspaper; affixation.
  • 169(2): Deemed service on tendering, publication or affixation in the manner specified.
  • 169(3): Registered/speed post deemed received after normal transit period unless contrary is proved.
  • Compliance lesson: Monitor portal and registered email continuously and preserve service evidence.
  • Legal lesson: Service validity and merits of the underlying notice are separate questions.
