Section 69 CGST Act — Power to Arrest under GST

Section 69 deals with the power to arrest a person in specified GST offence cases. This guide explains the connection between Section 69 and Section 132, the Commissioner's “reasons to believe”, written arrest authorisation, grounds of arrest, Magistrate production, bail, cognizable and non-cognizable offences, CBIC arrest guidelines and important safeguards for taxpayers and business personnel.

1. Why Section 69 matters

Searches such as “Section 69 CGST Act”, “GST arrest”, “power to arrest under GST”, “GST arrest procedure”, “GST arrest order”, “GST arrest rights”, “Section 132 arrest”, “GST bail” and “Can GST officers arrest?” relate directly to this provision.

Section 69 is one of the most serious enforcement provisions in the CGST Act because it concerns personal liberty, not merely tax recovery. The section must therefore be read together with Section 132, the criminal-procedure framework and the safeguards laid down through judicial decisions and CBIC instructions.

Important: A GST demand, scrutiny notice or summons does not automatically mean that a person can be arrested. Section 69 contains a specific statutory framework connecting arrest to specified Section 132 offences and the Commissioner's reasons to believe.

2. Section 69 in very simple language

Think of Section 69 as the GST law's arrest gateway.

First, the law identifies certain serious GST offences in Section 132. Section 69 then gives the Commissioner power to authorise an officer of central tax to arrest a person where the Commissioner has the required reasons to believe that the person committed the specified offence.

Section 132 offence → statutory punishment category → Commissioner forms reasons to believe → written arrest authorisation → authorised officer may arrest

The power is therefore not simply “GST officer can arrest whenever tax is unpaid”. The statutory offence, punishment category and procedural safeguards matter.

3. What Section 69 actually provides

Section 69(1): Where the Commissioner has reasons to believe that a person has committed an offence specified in Section 132(1)(a), (b), (c) or (d), punishable under Section 132(1)(i) or (ii), or under Section 132(2), the Commissioner may, by order, authorise an officer of central tax to arrest that person.

Section 69(2): Where a person is arrested for an offence specified in Section 132(5), the authorised officer must inform the person of the grounds of arrest and produce the person before a Magistrate within twenty-four hours.

Section 69(3): It contains the bail/custody framework for the offences covered by the provision, subject to the applicable criminal-procedure law.

4. Role of the Commissioner

The Commissioner is central to Section 69.

The authorised arrest is not simply based on an investigating officer deciding personally that an arrest should happen. Section 69(1) specifically states that the Commissioner, where the statutory “reasons to believe” requirement is met, may by order authorise an officer of central tax to arrest.

5. Section 69 and Section 132 offences

Section 69 cannot be understood in isolation. It expressly refers to four categories of Section 132(1) offences:

132(1)(a)
Supplying goods/services without issue of invoice, with the relevant intention to evade tax.
132(1)(b)
Issuing invoice/bill without supply, leading to wrongful ITC or refund consequences.
132(1)(c)
Availing ITC using invoices/bills covered by clause (b), or fraudulently without any invoice.
132(1)(d)
Collecting tax but failing to pay it to Government beyond the statutory period.

These are criminal-offence provisions. The exact facts, evidence, statutory wording applicable to the relevant period and punishment classification must be examined before drawing a conclusion.

6. Monetary thresholds and offence classification

Section 132(1) classifies punishment by the amount of tax evaded, ITC wrongly availed/utilised or refund wrongly taken in the cases specified by the Act.

Important: Do not reduce Section 69 to a “₹2 crore or ₹5 crore rule”. Section 69's text specifically links arrest to specified Section 132 offences and punishment clauses. The exact classification must be determined from the applicable law and facts.

7. Meaning of “reasons to believe”

“Reasons to believe” is a crucial legal safeguard. It means the Commissioner must have a rational and legally relevant basis for believing that the specified offence has been committed.

It is not the same as simply having a suspicion that a taxpayer has made a mistake.

Evidence-based
The belief should be founded on relevant investigation material.
Offence-specific
The material must connect to an offence covered by Section 69/132.
Application of mind
The statutory authority must consider the facts before authorising arrest.
Recorded in order
The arrest authorisation must reflect the statutory basis.

8. Written arrest authorisation

Section 69(1) expressly provides for authorisation by order.

This is an important distinction between an investigation generally and the specific statutory act of authorising arrest.

A taxpayer/business should preserve the arrest order and all documents associated with the arrest because these may become relevant in subsequent bail, judicial-review or criminal proceedings.

9. Who can actually make the arrest?

The Commissioner authorises an officer of central tax to arrest the person. The officer executing the arrest should act within the authority granted by the arrest order and the applicable procedural law.

This does not mean that every GST officer automatically possesses an unrestricted arrest power.

10. Grounds of arrest

Section 69(2) expressly requires the authorised officer to inform the arrested person of the grounds of arrest in the situation covered by that subsection.

There has also been important judicial development around communication of the arrest order and reasons. In Union of India v. Sunil Biyani, the Supreme Court in 2026 held that communication of the Commissioner's Section 69 arrest order containing the reasons to believe is mandatory before arrest, enabling the person to understand the basis and exercise available legal remedies. citeturn0search10

Practical point: For current proceedings, the arrest order, recorded reasons and their communication should be treated as important procedural documents.

11. Magistrate within 24 hours

Section 69(2) provides that where a person is arrested for an offence specified under Section 132(5), the authorised officer must inform the person of the grounds of arrest and produce the person before a Magistrate within 24 hours.

The 24-hour rule is a critical protection associated with the arrest procedure. The calculation and circumstances should be considered together with the applicable constitutional and criminal-procedure framework.

12. Bail and cognizable/non-cognizable offences

Section 69(3) connects the arrest consequences with the classification of the Section 132 offence.

CategoryBroad effect
Section 132(4)The Act provides the cognizable/non-bailable classification for the specified offences.
Section 132(5)The Act provides the classification of other offences and the applicable bail/production framework.
Non-cognizable and bailable offenceDeputy Commissioner or Assistant Commissioner has specified powers for release on bail or otherwise, similar to the officer-in-charge of a police station, subject to the Act.

The exact bail strategy is a legal matter and should be handled with qualified criminal/GST counsel.

13. CBIC arrest guidelines

CBIC issued Instruction No. 02/2022-23 [GST-Investigation] on guidelines for arrest and bail under the CGST Act.

The instruction emphasises that the existence of a statutory power to arrest does not mean arrest must be routine. It refers to the importance of personal liberty and requires officers to consider whether arrest is necessary in the circumstances.

  • Arrest should not be treated as automatic merely because the law permits it.
  • The investigation should establish the statutory offence and supporting material.
  • The officer should consider the necessity of arrest.
  • Cooperation with investigation is relevant to the exercise of arrest power.
  • The prescribed safeguards and procedural requirements should be followed.

CBIC's instruction expressly distinguishes the existence of power to arrest from the justification for exercising that power. citeturn0search15

14. Important Supreme Court development

The law around GST arrest has developed significantly through constitutional and procedural decisions.

Sunil Biyani — 2026 Supreme Court development

The Supreme Court's 2026 decision addressed the Section 69 arrest order and held that communication of the order containing the “reasons to believe” before arrest is mandatory. The Court linked this requirement to fairness, natural justice and the ability of the person to seek appropriate pre-arrest judicial remedies. citeturn0search10

Why this matters: A current Section 69 article should not explain arrest only through the bare statutory wording. The procedural safeguards developed by the courts are highly relevant to actual GST investigations.

17. Arrest vs investigation

GST investigation and GST arrest are separate concepts.

An investigation can involve searches, summons, statements, document collection and transaction analysis without necessarily resulting in arrest.

Conversely, where the statutory conditions for arrest are satisfied, the Commissioner can authorise arrest under Section 69.

Investigation ≠ automatic arrest

Businesses should therefore not respond to an ordinary investigation as though arrest is inevitable, but should also take serious legal advice where the investigation concerns potential Section 132 offences.

18. Company and responsible-person issues

Section 69 concerns arrest of a person. In a company investigation, this can raise questions about which individuals may be proceeded against for offences committed by the company.

Section 137 contains the separate framework concerning offences by companies and persons who were in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the business, subject to its statutory conditions and exceptions.

Therefore, a company's tax liability and an individual's criminal liability should not be treated as automatically identical.

19. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Fake invoice network

An investigation identifies invoices issued without actual supply and corresponding wrongful ITC. If the facts satisfy the specified Section 132 offence and punishment category, Section 69 may become relevant.

Example 2 — ITC without genuine supply

A person allegedly avails fraudulent ITC using invoices without actual underlying transactions. The department examines evidence before considering arrest.

Example 3 — Tax collected but not paid

A person collects GST from customers but does not pay the collected amount to Government beyond the statutory period. Section 132(1)(d) may become relevant, subject to the facts.

Example 4 — Genuine accounting mistake

A reconciliation error causes excess ITC but there is no evidence of the specified criminal offence. A tax correction/demand proceeding is not automatically the same thing as a Section 69 arrest case.

Example 5 — Director receives summons

A director receives a Section 70 summons. Receiving the summons does not itself mean that the director has been arrested or that arrest is automatic.

Example 6 — Search followed by investigation

Documents are seized under Section 67. The department later analyses them. If evidence indicates a specified Section 132 offence, Section 69 may be considered subject to the statutory safeguards.

Example 7 — Cooperation

A person consistently cooperates with summons and provides records. CBIC arrest guidelines require the necessity of arrest to be considered rather than treating arrest as routine.

Example 8 — Arrest order communication

Before arrest, the person receives the Commissioner's Section 69 order containing the reasons to believe, enabling the person to understand the basis and seek legal remedies where appropriate.

Example 9 — Construction company

A large contractor is investigated for allegedly using fabricated invoices and wrongful ITC. The investigation must establish the statutory offence; the company's large turnover alone does not create an arrest power.

Example 10 — Multiple individuals

Where a company investigation involves several persons, the legal position of each person must be examined individually under Section 137 and the evidence concerning the alleged offence.

20. Construction and infrastructure examples

Large subcontractor network

Construction businesses may have hundreds of subcontractors. A mismatch or disputed invoice should not automatically be equated with a criminal offence. The department must establish the statutory facts.

Fake purchase invoices

If investigation establishes that invoices were generated without actual supply to create wrongful ITC, the matter can become substantially more serious.

Material suppliers

Actual receipt of steel, cement, machinery and other project materials should be supported by GRNs, weighbridge records, e-way bills, delivery challans and payment records.

Cash transactions

Large unexplained cash transactions can become relevant evidence, but the legal conclusion must still be based on the specific GST offence and statutory conditions.

Project advances

Advance receipts, invoices and tax payments should be reconciled carefully to avoid disputes being caused by poor documentation rather than deliberate evasion.

22. Practical rights and safeguards

  • Understand whether the matter concerns a Section 132 offence before assuming that a tax dispute automatically permits arrest.
  • Preserve the arrest order and grounds/reasons communicated to the person.
  • Seek qualified legal representation immediately where arrest is threatened or effected.
  • Do not obstruct a lawful investigation, search or summons.
  • Do not destroy, alter or fabricate records.
  • Maintain a complete chronology of summons, statements, searches and communications.
  • Where applicable, use available bail/pre-arrest legal remedies through competent counsel.
  • Ensure company and individual positions are analysed separately.
Important: Arrest matters concern personal liberty and criminal procedure. A business should not rely solely on a general GST consultant for an arrest/bail decision; specialist legal counsel should be involved.

25. Common mistakes

  • Assuming every GST demand permits arrest.
  • Assuming every Section 70 summons means arrest is imminent.
  • Ignoring the connection between Sections 69 and 132.
  • Failing to identify the exact Section 132 offence alleged.
  • Ignoring the Commissioner's statutory role.
  • Not preserving the arrest order and grounds.
  • Confusing search under Section 67 with arrest under Section 69.
  • Ignoring Section 137 in company cases.
  • Providing inconsistent statements or destroying records.
  • Waiting until arrest occurs before taking specialist legal advice.

27. Section 69 GST FAQs

What is Section 69 of the CGST Act?

Section 69 provides the power to arrest a person where the Commissioner has the statutory reasons to believe that the person committed specified offences under Section 132 and the punishment conditions in Section 69 are met.

Can GST officers arrest a taxpayer?

Arrest is legally possible in the specified Section 69/132 circumstances, but it is not an automatic consequence of a GST demand, scrutiny notice or ordinary tax dispute.

Who authorises GST arrest?

The Commissioner authorises an officer of central tax by order under Section 69(1).

What is “reasons to believe” in GST arrest?

It is the statutory basis on which the Commissioner believes that a specified Section 132 offence has been committed. It should be founded on relevant investigation material and application of mind.

What offences can lead to arrest under Section 69?

Section 69 specifically refers to Section 132(1)(a), (b), (c) and (d) offences that fall within the punishment categories specified in Section 69, as well as Section 132(2).

Is arrest possible for every GST offence?

No. Section 69 does not create a general arrest power for every GST contravention.

What is the importance of Section 132?

Section 132 defines the criminal GST offences and their punishment framework to which Section 69 connects.

Must the person be told why they are arrested?

Section 69(2) expressly requires grounds of arrest to be communicated in the specified situation. The Supreme Court's 2026 Sunil Biyani decision further addressed mandatory prior communication of the Section 69 order containing reasons to believe.

When must an arrested person be produced before a Magistrate?

Section 69(2) requires production before a Magistrate within 24 hours in the statutory situation covered by that subsection, subject to the applicable legal framework.

Is GST arrest bailable?

It depends on the classification of the alleged offence under Section 132 and the applicable statutory/criminal-procedure provisions. Specialist legal advice is appropriate.

Can a director be arrested for company GST offences?

Individual liability requires analysis under the company's-offence provisions, including Section 137, and the evidence against the particular person. Company liability does not automatically mean every director is personally liable.

Does a search automatically lead to arrest?

No. Search under Section 67 and arrest under Section 69 are separate statutory powers.

Does receiving a summons mean arrest?

No. Section 70 summons are investigative tools. A summons does not itself constitute an arrest.

What should a business do if arrest is threatened?

Preserve records, cooperate lawfully, obtain the relevant documents and immediately involve qualified GST/criminal legal counsel to assess available remedies.

29. Key takeaways

  • Section 69 concerns arrest, not ordinary GST recovery.
  • It must be read together with Section 132.
  • The Commissioner must have the statutory reasons to believe.
  • The Commissioner authorises the central-tax officer by order.
  • The arrest power is connected to specified Section 132 offences and punishment categories.
  • Grounds of arrest and procedural safeguards are important.
  • Section 69(2) contains the Magistrate-production requirement in the specified statutory situation.
  • CBIC's arrest guidelines emphasise that the existence of arrest power does not make arrest routine.
  • The 2026 Supreme Court development makes prior communication of the Section 69 arrest order/reasons particularly important.
  • Search under Section 67, summons under Section 70 and arrest under Section 69 are separate powers.
  • In company cases, individual liability must be analysed separately under Section 137 and the evidence against the individual.
