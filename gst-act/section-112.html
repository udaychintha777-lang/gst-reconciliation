1. Why Section 112 matters

Section 112 is the principal provision governing appeals to the GST Appellate Tribunal. If a taxpayer or other aggrieved person receives an appealable order under Section 107 or Section 108, this is the provision that becomes central to the next stage of litigation.

For finance and tax teams, Section 112 is especially important because a Tribunal appeal is not only a legal exercise. It affects cash flow, pre-deposit, recovery exposure, accounting provisions, evidence, limitation and management decisions.

Section 107First appeal before Appellate Authority.
Section 108Departmental revision mechanism.
Section 112Appeal/application before GSTAT.
Section 117Further appeal to High Court on statutory grounds.
Practical message: When an adverse appellate or revisional order arrives, do not wait for the legal team to “start the appeal”. Finance should immediately capture the communication date, disputed amount, pre-deposit requirement and limitation deadline.

2. Section 112 in simple language

Section 112 can be understood through ten operational questions:

QuestionSection 112 answer
Who may appeal?A person aggrieved by specified orders under Section 107 or 108.
Normal time?Three months, subject to the statutory notified-date provision and condonation rules.
Can a departmental authority appeal?Yes, through the mechanism in sub-section (3).
Can the other party challenge another part of the order?Yes, through cross-objections within the statutory period.
Can delay be condoned?Yes, within the additional period specified in sub-section (6), where sufficient cause is shown.
Is there a pre-deposit?Yes. The current Section 112(8) requires admitted amounts plus the prescribed percentage of remaining disputed tax, subject to the statutory ceiling.
Does payment stop further recovery?Section 112(9) provides deemed stay of recovery of the balance after the required payment.
Which forms?Taxpayer appeal: GST APL-05; cross-objections: GST APL-06; departmental application: GST APL-07.
Can additional evidence be filed freely?No. The appeal rules restrict additional evidence except in specified circumstances.
Can errors be corrected?Section 112(10) provides for applications concerning rectification and restoration, subject to prescribed procedure and fees.

3. Who can appeal to the Appellate Tribunal?

Section 112(1) allows any person aggrieved by an order passed against that person under Section 107 or Section 108 of the CGST Act, SGST Act or UTGST Act to appeal to the Appellate Tribunal.

The phrase “person aggrieved” is important. The Tribunal appeal is connected to an actual order that adversely affects the appellant and falls within the statutory appellate route.

Examples of potentially affected persons

  • A registered business against whom additional tax, interest or penalty is confirmed.
  • A taxpayer whose ITC is denied or reduced.
  • A person affected by a classification or valuation finding.
  • A person affected by a revisional order under Section 108.
  • A department pursuing an application through the mechanism in Section 112(3).
Do not confuse an unfavourable internal departmental view with an appealable order. First identify the actual order, the section under which it was passed and whether Section 112 applies.

5. Three-month limitation

The normal rule under Section 112(1) is that an appeal should be filed within three months from the date on which the order sought to be appealed against is communicated to the person preferring the appeal.

This means the finance/tax team should record the actual communication date rather than relying only on the date printed on the order.

Practical limitation control

ControlAction

Limitation dateCalculate the statutory deadline.

Pre-depositArrange payment early enough to avoid filing delays.
Never use the last day as the company's internal target. A filing may involve document certification, payment, portal issues, authorised signatures and Registrar requirements.

6. Special notified-date provision

Section 112 was amended in 2024 to address the delayed operational commencement of the GST Appellate Tribunal. The amended text provides that the three-month period is linked to the later of the communication date and the date that may be notified by the Government, on the recommendation of the GST Council, for filing appeals before the Tribunal.

A corresponding provision was inserted into Section 112(3) for departmental applications.

Why this matters: For older appellate/revisional orders, do not mechanically calculate the Tribunal limitation period without checking the applicable Government notification governing the commencement date for the relevant category of appeals.

The 2024 amendment specifically inserted these later-date provisions with effect from 1 August 2024.

7. Small-value appeal discretion

Section 112(2) gives the Appellate Tribunal discretion to refuse to admit an appeal where the tax or ITC involved, the difference in tax or ITC involved, or the amount of fine, fee or penalty determined by the order does not exceed ₹50,000.

This provision is different from saying that every appeal below ₹50,000 is automatically prohibited. It gives the Tribunal discretion to refuse admission in the circumstances specified.

Practical point: Do not confuse the ₹50,000 threshold in Section 112(2) with the filing fee calculation or the pre-deposit requirement under Section 112(8).

8. Departmental appeal/application

Section 112(3) provides a departmental route. The Commissioner may, on the Commissioner's own motion or on a request from the relevant Central/State/UT Commissioner, call for and examine the record of an order passed by the Appellate Authority or Revisional Authority.

If the Commissioner considers the order not legal or proper, the Commissioner may direct a subordinate authorised officer to apply to the Appellate Tribunal for determination of the specified points arising from that order.

The statutory time framework is six months from the date of the order, subject to the amended notified-date provision in Section 112(3).

9. Cross-objections

Section 112(5) provides an important protection for the party against whom an appeal has been filed.

When that party receives notice of an appeal, it may file a memorandum of cross-objections even if it did not itself file an appeal against the order or a particular part of it.

The statutory period is 45 days from receipt of the notice, subject to the condonation mechanism in Section 112(6).

Example

The department appeals against a first appellate order that reduced a demand from ₹1 crore to ₹40 lakh. The taxpayer did not appeal because the taxpayer was satisfied with the ₹40 lakh outcome. If the department challenges the relief, the taxpayer may need to consider cross-objections to protect its position on other aspects of the order.

Action point: Whenever a GSTAT notice is received, immediately check whether the case creates a need for cross-objections. Do not assume “we did not file an appeal, so there is nothing to do.”

10. Condonation of delay

Section 112(6) allows the Tribunal to admit a taxpayer appeal filed after the normal three-month period if it is presented within the additional statutory period and the Tribunal is satisfied that there was sufficient cause for the delay.

The same sub-section also provides a mechanism for delayed cross-objections and, following the 2024 amendment, delayed departmental applications within the additional period specified for applications under Section 112(3).

What should “sufficient cause” documentation look like?

  • Exact date the order was received.
  • Date the responsible officer became aware of the order.
  • Reason for the delay.
  • Evidence supporting the reason.
  • Internal approval chronology.
  • Actions taken to file immediately after the obstacle was removed.
Best practice: Never build a condonation explanation only after the deadline has expired. Maintain a contemporaneous litigation diary.

The current CBIC appeal-rules page states that a Section 112(1) appeal is filed electronically in FORM GST APL-05, while cross-objections use APL-06 and departmental applications use APL-07. It also prescribes submission of the certified order and supporting documents within the specified filing process.

Practical filing control: Save the provisional acknowledgement, certified order, final acknowledgement and appeal number in the same case folder. The filing is not complete for internal control purposes merely because someone says “the form was uploaded.”

12. Pre-deposit — current position

Section 112(8) contains a mandatory payment condition for filing an appeal under Section 112(1).

The appellant must pay:

  1. in full, the portion of tax, interest, fine, fee and penalty arising from the impugned order that is admitted by the appellant; and
  2. a prescribed percentage of the remaining amount of tax in dispute, in addition to the amount already paid under Section 107(6), subject to the statutory maximum.
Current CGST position: Following the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 amendment effective from 1 November 2024, Section 112(8)(b) uses 10% instead of 20%, with a maximum of ₹20 crore under the CGST provision.

This change is extremely important for large taxpayers because older GST articles may still show the previous 20% / ₹50 crore wording.

Simple illustration

ParticularAmount
Tax demanded₹10 crore

Plus amount already paid under Section 107(6)As applicable to the case

The actual computation must be made from the impugned order and the applicable statutory provisions, including the correct treatment of admitted amounts and earlier pre-deposit.

13. Stay of recovery after the required payment

Section 112(9) provides that once the appellant has paid the amounts required under Section 112(8), recovery proceedings for the balance amount are deemed to be stayed until disposal of the appeal.

This is one of the most important cash-flow protections available at the Tribunal stage.

Finance example

Suppose the disputed tax is ₹5 crore and the taxpayer completes the required pre-deposit under Section 107 and Section 112. The remaining disputed balance should be tracked as subject to the statutory stay mechanism rather than treated by the finance team as an ordinary immediately payable liability.

Control point: Keep proof of every statutory payment. The recovery-stay position should be supported by the actual appeal and payment records, not merely an internal spreadsheet.

14. Tribunal filing fee

The appeal rules prescribe the Tribunal filing/restoration fee. The current CBIC appeal-rules page states a fee of ₹1,000 for every ₹1 lakh of tax or ITC involved/difference involved, or the amount of fine, fee or penalty determined, subject to a maximum of ₹25,000.

The rules also state that there is no fee for an application before the Tribunal for rectification of errors referred to in Section 112(10).

Always verify the current prescribed rule and filing instructions at the time of filing.

15. Additional evidence — do not wait until GSTAT

The appeal rules generally restrict an appellant from introducing oral or documentary evidence before the Appellate Authority or Tribunal if it was not produced during the earlier proceedings, except in specified circumstances.

Exceptions include situations such as:

  • the earlier authority refused to admit evidence that should have been admitted;
  • the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause from producing evidence it was called upon to produce;
  • the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause from producing relevant evidence; or
  • the order was made without giving sufficient opportunity to produce relevant evidence.

Where additional evidence is admitted, reasons must be recorded and the other side must be given a reasonable opportunity to examine or rebut it. The Tribunal can also direct production of documents or examination of witnesses needed to dispose of the appeal.

Business lesson: “We will give all the documents to the Tribunal later” is a poor litigation strategy. Build the evidence record at the adjudication and first-appeal stages.

16. Rectification and restoration

Section 112(10) recognises applications before the Tribunal relating to rectification of errors and restoration of an appeal or application, with fees as prescribed.

These remedies should not be confused with a fresh appeal on the merits. If a party believes the Tribunal order contains an error apparent within the applicable statutory framework, the correct remedy should be evaluated rather than automatically filing another substantive appeal.

Internal decision tree

Merits problem?Check the statutory appellate route.
Apparent error?Consider rectification under the applicable provision.
Default/restoration?Check the applicable restoration mechanism.
Higher legal question?Evaluate the High Court route under Section 117.

17. Practical examples

Example 1 — Large ITC demand

A company receives a Section 107 order confirming ₹6 crore of disputed ITC.

Immediate actions: record communication date, calculate the Section 112 deadline, quantify admitted versus disputed tax, calculate the additional Tribunal-stage pre-deposit, preserve the ITC reconciliation and obtain management approval.

Example 2 — Department appeals against relief

The taxpayer succeeds substantially before the Appellate Authority, but the department challenges the relief before GSTAT.

Taxpayer action: do not simply wait for the hearing. Examine the department's grounds and determine whether cross-objections under Section 112(5) are appropriate.

Example 3 — Delay in filing

The order was received by the tax department but the internal litigation team learned about it much later.

Solution: document the complete communication chain, establish the cause of delay, determine whether the matter remains within the statutory condonable period, and file the appropriate delay application with supporting evidence.

Example 4 — Very large demand

A group company has a disputed tax demand running into hundreds of crores.

Finance action: the team should not calculate pre-deposit as a simple percentage without checking the statutory ceiling. The current CGST Section 112(8)(b) ceiling is ₹20 crore after the 2024 amendment effective from 1 November 2024.

21. Management decision framework

A Tribunal appeal should be evaluated like a significant financial/legal project.

QuestionManagement should know
What is the disputed amount?Tax, ITC, interest and penalty separately.
What is the cash requirement?Admitted amount + applicable pre-deposit + fee/professional cost.
What is the legal strength?Key statutory provisions, facts and precedents.
What evidence exists?Contracts, invoices, reconciliations, payments and operational records.
What is the deadline?Statutory limitation plus internal buffer.
What is the downside?Potential tax/interest/penalty and future-period impact.
What is the upside?Relief, refund/re-credit and reduction of exposure.
Management recommendation: Maintain a one-page “GSTAT decision memo” for major disputes before filing. It should show the amount, legal position, evidence, cash requirement, deadline, proposed counsel and requested relief.

22. Common mistakes

  1. Using old 20% pre-deposit information. Section 112(8)(b) was amended to 10%, with a ₹20 crore CGST ceiling effective from 1 November 2024.
  2. Counting limitation only from the order date. Section 112 refers to communication and now contains the special notified-date wording.
  3. Missing cross-objections. A department appeal can create a need for action even where the taxpayer did not originally appeal.
  4. Waiting until GSTAT to collect evidence. Additional evidence is restricted by the appeal rules.
  5. Not separating admitted and disputed tax. This can lead to incorrect pre-deposit calculations.
  6. Ignoring earlier Section 107 payment. Section 112(8) works in addition to the amount paid under Section 107(6).
  7. Not preserving payment proof. Keep challans and electronic payment references.
  8. Assuming recovery automatically stops without checking payment compliance. The statutory deemed stay is linked to payment under Section 112(8).
  9. Using the wrong form. APL-05, APL-06 and APL-07 have different purposes.
  10. Failing to create an internal deadline. Portal and documentation issues can arise near the statutory deadline.

26. Frequently Asked Questions

What is Section 112 of the CGST Act?

Section 112 deals with appeals to the GST Appellate Tribunal.

Who can file a Section 112 appeal?

Any person aggrieved by an order passed against that person under Section 107 or Section 108 of the CGST Act, SGST Act or UTGST Act may appeal to the Appellate Tribunal, subject to the statutory conditions.

What is the normal limitation period?

The normal period under Section 112(1) is three months from communication of the order, subject to the special notified-date wording introduced by amendment and the condonation mechanism in Section 112(6).

What is the current GSTAT pre-deposit percentage?

For Section 112(8)(b), the percentage was reduced from 20% to 10% with effect from 1 November 2024, and the CGST statutory ceiling was reduced from ₹50 crore to ₹20 crore. The admitted portion must also be paid in full as specified by Section 112(8)(a).

Is the 10% calculated on the total demand?

No. The provision refers to the remaining amount of tax in dispute after taking into account the admitted amount, and it operates in addition to the amount paid under Section 107(6). The exact calculation should be made from the impugned order and the statutory provisions.

What is GST APL-05?

It is the prescribed form for an appeal to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 112(1).

What is GST APL-06?

It is the prescribed form for a memorandum of cross-objections under Section 112(5).

What is GST APL-07?

It is the prescribed form for a departmental application to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 112(3).

Can delay in filing be condoned?

Yes, Section 112(6) provides a limited additional period where the Tribunal is satisfied that sufficient cause prevented timely filing.

What are cross-objections?

They allow the respondent to challenge a part of the order even when that respondent did not independently appeal, after receiving notice of the other party's appeal.

Does filing the Tribunal appeal automatically stop recovery?

The statutory deemed stay under Section 112(9) follows payment of the amounts required by Section 112(8). It is important to complete and preserve the required payments and filing records.

Can new documents be submitted directly before GSTAT?

Not freely. The appeal rules restrict additional evidence and allow it in specified circumstances, subject to procedural safeguards.

What happens after GSTAT?

Section 117 provides the statutory route for appeal to the High Court on substantial questions of law, subject to its conditions.

28. Key takeaways

  • Section 112 is the principal provision for appeals to the GST Appellate Tribunal.
  • A person aggrieved by a specified Section 107 or Section 108 order can appeal, subject to the statutory conditions.
  • The normal limitation period is three months, with the amended notified-date mechanism needing attention for the relevant orders.
  • The department has a separate application route under Section 112(3).
  • Cross-objections can protect the respondent even where that party did not file its own appeal.
  • Section 112(6) provides limited condonation for delayed appeals, cross-objections and specified applications where sufficient cause is established.
  • The current CGST Section 112(8)(b) requirement is 10% of the remaining disputed tax, in addition to the Section 107(6) payment, subject to a ₹20 crore CGST ceiling.
  • After the required Section 112(8) payment, recovery of the balance is deemed stayed under Section 112(9).
  • APL-05 is used for taxpayer appeals, APL-06 for cross-objections and APL-07 for departmental applications.
  • Additional evidence is restricted by the appeal rules, so evidence should be collected early.
  • Finance teams should treat a GSTAT appeal as a combined legal, cash-flow, accounting and evidence-management project.
