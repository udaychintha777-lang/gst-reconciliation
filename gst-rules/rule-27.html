Rule 27 – Value of Supply Where Consideration Is Not Wholly in Money

A practical guide to GST valuation when a supply is settled partly in money and partly through goods, services, exchange, barter or another non-monetary consideration.

1. Rule 27 at a glance

Rule 27 of the CGST Rules, 2017 prescribes the valuation mechanism where the consideration for a supply is not wholly in money. The rule is relevant where the recipient pays some amount in cash and gives something else in return, or where the transaction is structured as an exchange or barter.

The key point is that GST valuation does not stop at the amount appearing as the cash portion of the invoice. The non-monetary component must also be considered through the sequential valuation mechanism in Rule 27.

Simple principle: If consideration is partly money and partly something other than money, determine the value using the Rule 27 sequence rather than automatically taxing only the cash received.

Rule 27 is linked to the valuation framework in Section 15 of the CGST Act. It should therefore be read as a specific valuation rule for this type of transaction, while the wider requirements of Section 15 continue to apply.

2. Rule 27 valuation hierarchy

The rule operates sequentially. Move to the next step only when the earlier step does not determine the value.

OrderMethodWhat it means
(a)Open market valueUse the open market value of the supply.
(b)Money consideration + known monetary equivalentIf open market value is unavailable, add the money received/payable and the monetary equivalent of the non-money consideration, where that amount is known at the time of supply.
(c)Like kind and qualityIf the first two methods do not determine value, use the value of goods or services of like kind and quality.
(d)Rule 30 or Rule 31If value is still not determinable, determine the monetary equivalent of the non-money consideration using Rule 30 or Rule 31, in that order, and add it to the monetary consideration.
Remember the sequence: OMV → cash + known non-cash equivalent → like kind and quality → Rule 30 → Rule 31.

3. Step 1 – Open market value

The first and preferred test is the open market value (OMV) of the supply. If the same or substantially comparable supply is ordinarily available in the market for a determinable value, that value becomes the starting point.

Example – exchange transaction

A new phone is supplied for ₹20,000 along with an old phone under an exchange arrangement. If the price of the new phone without exchange is ₹24,000, the open market value of the new phone is ₹24,000. The valuation does not become ₹20,000 merely because ₹20,000 is the cash portion.

Practical test: Ask, “What would this exact supply ordinarily sell for in the open market without the barter/exchange component?” If a reliable value exists, Rule 27(a) is the natural first step.

4. Step 2 – Money consideration plus known non-money equivalent

If the open market value is not available, Rule 27(b) looks at the sum total of the monetary consideration and the amount equivalent to the non-monetary consideration, if that amount is known at the time of supply.

Example

A laptop is supplied for ₹40,000 and the recipient gives a printer manufactured by the recipient as part of the barter. If the value of the printer is known at the time of supply as ₹4,000, and the open market value of the laptop is not available, the value under this step is ₹44,000.

ComponentValue
Cash consideration₹40,000
Known value of non-money consideration₹4,000
Rule 27 value₹44,000
Documentation matters: Where a non-cash item is assigned a monetary value at the time of supply, retain the agreement, valuation working, purchase documents, market evidence or other records supporting how that amount was established.

5. Step 3 – Value of like kind and quality

Where neither the open market value nor the method under Rule 27(b) determines the value, the rule moves to the value of the supply of goods or services, or both, of like kind and quality.

“Like kind and quality” should be supported by commercially comparable supplies. Useful evidence can include recent invoices for similar products or services, comparable contracts, customer quotations, standard price lists and other reliable internal business records.

Illustration

If an air-conditioning unit with similar characteristics, quality, quantity, functional components, materials and reputation has been supplied to another customer for ₹60,000, that comparable value may support valuation under this stage where the earlier methods cannot determine value.

Do not pick a convenient comparable: The closer the comparison in characteristics, quality, quantity and commercial circumstances, the stronger the valuation file.

6. Step 4 – Rule 30 and Rule 31

If value cannot be determined under clauses (a), (b) or (c), Rule 27(d) requires the non-money consideration to be valued using Rule 30 or Rule 31 in that order, with that amount then added to the monetary consideration.

Rule 30 – cost-based method

Rule 30 generally determines value at 110% of the cost of production or manufacture, cost of acquisition of goods, or cost of provision of services, as applicable.

Rule 31 – residual method

Where the value cannot be determined under the preceding valuation rules, Rule 31 uses reasonable means consistent with the principles and general provisions of Section 15 and the valuation chapter. For services, the rule contains a flexibility to use Rule 31 by ignoring Rule 30.

Important: Rule 30/31 is not the first choice merely because a non-cash component exists. Rule 27 requires the earlier valuation steps to be considered sequentially.

8. Construction and business examples

Rule 27 can become particularly important in commercial arrangements where consideration is not entirely settled through a bank payment. Examples can include asset-for-service arrangements, exchange of equipment, barter arrangements and contracts where an identifiable non-monetary component forms part of the consideration.

Example – contractor receives an asset

Suppose a contractor provides taxable construction-related services for a customer. The customer agrees to pay ₹50 lakh in money and transfer equipment to the contractor as part of the consideration. The GST valuation should not automatically be restricted to ₹50 lakh. The equipment component must be examined under the Rule 27 hierarchy.

Example – land or property-related consideration

Where an arrangement involves immovable property, development rights or another non-cash component, the GST analysis can become more complex because the transaction may involve additional provisions governing the nature, time and value of the supply. Rule 27 should be applied only after identifying exactly what supply is being made and what constitutes the consideration.

Management point: Do not treat every non-cash commercial adjustment as “barter” without analysing the underlying contract. The legal character of each supply and the consideration should be documented before valuation is finalised.

Correctly identifying the applicable rule is essential. A transaction involving non-monetary consideration is not automatically a Rule 28 transaction merely because the parties have a particular relationship; the facts and the relevant valuation provision must be examined.

10. Finance and ERP controls

For businesses with frequent exchanges, asset settlements or non-cash commercial arrangements, Rule 27 should be built into the transaction review process rather than handled only during GST return preparation.

  • Create a transaction flag for non-monetary consideration.
  • Capture cash consideration and non-cash consideration separately.
  • Store the description, quantity and valuation of the non-cash component.
  • Record the valuation method selected: OMV, known equivalent, like kind/quality, Rule 30 or Rule 31.
  • Attach supporting agreements, quotations, comparable invoices and valuation workings.
  • Prevent the system from calculating GST only on the cash component where a non-cash component is present.
  • Route unusual barter/exchange transactions for finance review before invoicing.
  • Reconcile Rule 27 transactions from sales register to GST return workings.

11. Common mistakes

  1. Taxing only the cash received: ignoring the non-monetary component can understate taxable value.
  2. Skipping the valuation hierarchy: jumping directly to cost-plus valuation without checking OMV and comparable values.
  3. No evidence for non-cash value: recording an arbitrary amount without supporting documents.
  4. Using an unrelated comparable: treating any similar-looking transaction as “like kind and quality”.
  5. Confusing Rule 27 with Rule 28: the reason for the valuation rule matters.
  6. Ignoring the contract: the actual commercial terms may reveal additional supplies or consideration.
  7. Late identification: discovering barter consideration only while preparing GSTR-1/GSTR-3B can create invoice and tax-period issues.

12. FAQs

Does Rule 27 apply only to barter?

No. It applies where consideration for the supply is not wholly in money. Barter and exchange are common examples, but the principle is broader.

If the cash price is known, can I ignore the non-cash item?

Not automatically. If the non-cash component forms part of the consideration, the Rule 27 valuation mechanism must be considered.

Which method comes first?

Open market value comes first. If unavailable, Rule 27 moves to the monetary consideration plus the known equivalent of the non-monetary consideration, then like kind and quality, and finally Rule 30/31 as prescribed.

Is Rule 30 applied before Rule 31?

Yes. Rule 27(d) specifically refers to Rule 30 or Rule 31 in that order.

Can I use a valuation report?

A valuation report can be useful evidence, especially for significant or unusual non-cash consideration. It should support the valuation method actually applicable to the transaction and be consistent with the contractual and commercial facts.

Does every exchange transaction have the same GST treatment?

No. The nature of each supply, the parties, the consideration and any other applicable GST provisions must be analysed. Rule 27 is a valuation provision, not a substitute for identifying the underlying supplies.

13. Compliance checklist

  • ☐ Identify whether consideration is wholly monetary or partly non-monetary.
  • ☐ Identify every supply arising from the arrangement.
  • ☐ Check open market value first.
  • ☐ If OMV is unavailable, establish the monetary equivalent of non-cash consideration if known at supply.
  • ☐ If necessary, document like kind and quality comparables.
  • ☐ If required, apply Rule 30 and then Rule 31.
  • ☐ Preserve valuation evidence and the underlying agreement.
  • ☐ Ensure invoice taxable value reflects the applicable Rule 27 method.
  • ☐ Reconcile the transaction to GST return workings and accounting records.
