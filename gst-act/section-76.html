GST ACT — SECTION 76

Section 76 CGST Act — Tax Collected but Not Paid to Government

Section 76 deals with a very specific but important GST situation: a person collects an amount from another person as representing GST but does not pay that amount to the Government. The rule can apply even where the underlying supply itself is ultimately found to be non-taxable. This guide explains the provision in simple language, including notice, penalty, interest, hearing, payment and refund consequences.

1. Why Section 76 matters

Searches such as “Section 76 CGST Act”, “Section 76 GST”, “tax collected but not paid to Government”, “GST collected from customer but not paid”, “Section 76 GST notice”, “GST penalty for collecting tax”, and “GST tax collected not deposited” all relate directly to this provision.

Section 76 addresses a different problem from an ordinary GST tax short-payment case. Here, the important fact is that the person has actually collected an amount from another person while representing it as tax and has not paid that amount to the Government.

Simple idea: If you collect GST from your customer, you cannot keep that GST as your own money. The amount represented as tax must reach the Government in accordance with the law.

2. Section 76 in very simple language

Suppose a supplier issues an invoice showing ₹1,00,000 as GST and collects the amount from the customer. If the supplier does not pay that ₹1,00,000 to the Government, Section 76 can become relevant.

GST amount collected from customer → Government must receive it → if unpaid → notice + interest + statutory penalty process

The provision is designed to protect the Government and the person from whom the amount was collected. It also prevents a person from retaining an amount merely because it was described as tax on an invoice.

3. What Section 76 covers

Section 76(1) applies where a person:

  • has collected an amount from another person;
  • represented that amount as tax under the CGST Act; and
  • has not paid the amount to the Government.

The provision contains a strong overriding clause. It states that the amount must be paid to Government notwithstanding anything contrary in an order/direction of an appellate authority, tribunal or court, other provisions of the Act/rules, or another law, subject to the statutory scheme.

The key factual question is not merely “was GST legally payable?” It is also “was an amount collected from another person as representing GST?”

4. Why even a non-taxable supply can matter

One of the most important features of Section 76(1) is that it expressly says the amount must be paid to Government irrespective of whether the supplies in respect of which the amount was collected are taxable or not.

Example: a supplier incorrectly charges an amount as GST on a supply that is actually exempt. The supplier cannot simply retain the collected “GST” on the argument that no GST was legally due on the supply.

This is why businesses should carefully review tax collected on exempt, nil-rated, non-GST and incorrectly classified supplies.

5. Notice under Section 76(2)

If an amount required to be paid under Section 76(1) has not been paid, the proper officer may serve a notice requiring the person to show cause:

  • why the amount specified in the notice should not be paid to Government; and
  • why a penalty equivalent to that amount should not be imposed under the Act.

The notice therefore has two separate components: payment of the collected amount and penalty proposal.

6. Penalty under Section 76

The Section 76 notice can propose a penalty equivalent to the amount that was collected as tax and not paid to Government.

ComponentMeaning
Collected amountThe amount represented as tax and collected from another person.
PenaltyCan be equivalent to the amount specified in the notice, subject to the adjudication process and applicable law.
InterestSeparate liability under Section 76(4), calculated from collection until payment to Government.
Important: Do not confuse the Section 76 penalty with the general penalty provisions under Section 122. The same conduct and the applicable statutory provisions must be analysed before concluding that a second penalty is permissible.

7. Right to hearing

Section 76(5) provides an opportunity of hearing where the person to whom the notice was issued requests it in writing.

This means that a taxpayer receiving a Section 76 notice should make a clear written request for personal hearing where the facts, amount, classification or penalty are disputed.

Prepare for the hearing

  • identify the amount actually collected;
  • identify the invoice(s) and customer(s);
  • show whether the amount was already paid to Government;
  • explain any correction/refund made;
  • address the penalty proposal; and
  • provide bank and accounting evidence.

8. Determination by proper officer

After considering the representation made in response to the notice, the proper officer determines the amount due from the person and the person becomes liable to pay the amount determined.

The adjudication process should therefore distinguish:

  • the amount actually collected;
  • the amount already deposited with Government;
  • the amount still outstanding;
  • interest; and
  • penalty.

A good response should reconcile each invoice rather than merely dispute the total amount without evidence.

9. Interest from date of collection

Section 76(4) contains a particularly important interest rule. The person who collected the amount must pay interest at the rate specified under Section 50 from the date the amount was collected until the date it is paid to Government.

This is different from an ordinary late-payment calculation where the starting point is generally connected to the tax due date. Section 76 specifically links interest to the date of collection.

Interest period = Date amount was collected → Date amount is paid to Government

10. Payment and recovery

Once the amount is determined, it becomes payable to Government. If the person does not pay the amount, the normal GST recovery framework can become relevant.

The electronic tax liability and recovery mechanism should be checked along with the applicable rules and forms.

Where a demand has been determined and remains unpaid, Section 79 provides the principal recovery framework under the CGST Act.

11. Refund to the person who bore the tax

Section 76 contains a special mechanism where the amount collected as tax has already been paid to Government.

Where the person who ultimately bore the incidence of the tax can be identified and the statutory conditions are satisfied, the amount may be refunded to that person through the prescribed mechanism rather than automatically becoming a windfall for the person who collected it.

This provision is closely connected with the principle that the economic burden of tax should not be refunded to someone who did not actually bear it.

12. Unjust enrichment and incidence of tax

GST refund law generally examines whether the incidence of tax has been passed on to another person. Section 76's refund mechanism similarly focuses on the person who actually bore the amount.

For practical purposes, businesses should preserve:

  • invoice and tax-collection records;
  • customer ledgers;
  • receipts/bank statements;
  • credit notes or refunds issued;
  • proof of tax payment; and
  • evidence showing who ultimately bore the amount.

13. Adjustment where tax is already paid

Before responding to a Section 76 notice, reconcile the amount collected with the GST actually deposited.

SituationPractical treatment
Collected and fully paidProduce return/challan/ledger evidence and reconcile the payment.
Collected but partly paidIdentify unpaid balance and calculate applicable interest.
Collected but incorrectly reported under another tax headAnalyse the tax-head correction mechanism separately; do not simply assume that the amount is unpaid.
Collected and refunded to customerMaintain proof of refund/credit and examine whether Government payment/refund consequences remain.

14. Section 76 vs Section 32

Section 32Section 76
Restricts collection of GST by persons who are not authorised to collect it and by registered persons except in accordance with the Act.Deals with an amount that has actually been collected as representing tax but has not been paid to Government.

The two provisions can therefore be relevant to the same transaction but answer different legal questions.

15. Section 76 vs Section 73/74/74A

ProvisionMain focus

Section 74Historical fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression demand framework for periods up to FY 2023-24.
Section 74ANewer determination framework for specified periods from FY 2024-25 onward.
Section 76Tax amount collected from another person as representing GST but not paid to Government.
Section 76 is not simply another version of Section 73/74. The fact of collection from another person as tax is central to Section 76.

16. Section 76 vs Section 122 penalty

Section 122 contains various GST offences and penalties, including situations involving collection of tax and failure to pay it to Government. Section 76, however, contains its own specific mechanism requiring payment of the collected amount, interest and a penalty proposal.

For a live proceeding, the exact acts alleged and the statutory provisions invoked must be examined to ensure that the same act is not being penalised twice contrary to applicable statutory protections.

18. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — GST shown on an exempt supply

A supplier incorrectly charges ₹18,000 as GST on an exempt supply and collects it from the customer. Even though the underlying supply is exempt, Section 76 can require the collected amount to be paid to Government.

Example 2 — Tax collected but cash retained

A supplier collects GST from customers but uses the money for working capital and does not deposit it. The collected amount can become a Section 76 liability, with interest.

Example 3 — Tax collected but return omission

The invoice shows GST and the customer pays it, but the supplier fails to include the corresponding liability in the return. The collection evidence becomes important.

Example 4 — Partial payment

₹10 lakh is collected as GST but only ₹6 lakh is paid to Government. The outstanding ₹4 lakh must be reconciled and the applicable interest considered.

Example 5 — Incorrect tax head

GST is collected but reported under an incorrect head. Before accepting a Section 76 demand, reconcile the payment and examine whether it is actually an unpaid amount or a tax-head issue.

Example 6 — Customer refund

A supplier collected an amount as GST and later refunded the amount to the customer. The accounting and Government-payment consequences should be examined rather than assuming the refund alone closes the statutory issue.

Example 7 — Construction invoice

A contractor charges GST on a RA bill, collects it from the customer, but the corresponding GST is omitted from the tax payment. Section 76 exposure can arise because the amount was collected as tax.

Example 8 — Advance

A GST amount is collected with an advance and later the supply treatment changes. The business should trace the advance, invoice, tax payment and adjustment.

Example 9 — Incorrect GST rate

A supplier collects GST at 18% but the correct tax treatment is exempt. The excess amount represented as tax still requires specific analysis under Section 76.

Example 10 — Multiple GST registrations

One GSTIN collects tax while the payment is mistakenly made under another registration. Reconcile GSTIN-wise collection, liability and payment before accepting that the tax was never paid.

19. Construction and infrastructure examples

RA bills

Maintain RA bill-wise GST charged, customer receipt, GSTR-1 reporting, GSTR-3B payment and ledger reconciliation.

Mobilisation advance

Trace the GST collected on the advance through receipt, tax invoice/adjustment, return and payment.

Retention

Separate amounts actually collected from amounts contractually retained by the customer. Do not assume every retention entry represents tax collected.

Subcontractor billing

Maintain supplier invoices and payment records to establish whether GST shown by the subcontractor was actually collected and deposited.

Project-wise controls

For large infrastructure businesses, reconcile tax charged to customers with GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, bank receipts and project ledgers GSTIN-wise.

21. Practical safeguards

  • Never treat GST collected from customers as unrestricted business cash.
  • Reconcile tax charged with GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B every month.
  • Check exempt/non-taxable invoices for accidental GST charging.
  • Reconcile customer receipts with tax invoices.
  • Investigate tax-head mismatches promptly.
  • Track GST collected on advances.
  • Maintain proof of GST payment.
  • Where tax is incorrectly charged, document the correction/refund process.
  • If a Section 76 notice arrives, request hearing in writing where required.
  • Separate collected amount, interest and penalty in the working.

23. Legislative position

Section 76 came into force from 1 July 2017 and remains part of the current CGST Act. The current consolidated text continues to provide the tax-collected-but-not-paid framework without a substantive replacement by Sections 73, 74 or 74A. citeturn0search0turn0search1

The provision therefore remains important even though the general tax-determination architecture changed with the introduction of Section 74A.

Section 74A changes the framework for determining tax for newer periods; it does not make Section 76 disappear. Section 76 addresses the distinct fact of tax being collected from another person as tax and not being paid to Government.

24. Common mistakes

  • Thinking Section 76 applies only when the underlying supply is taxable.
  • Assuming an exempt supply means GST collected on it can be retained.
  • Failing to reconcile customer collections with tax payments.
  • Confusing Section 76 with ordinary tax short-payment proceedings.
  • Ignoring interest from the actual collection date.
  • Not checking whether tax was paid under another GSTIN or tax head.
  • Failing to maintain proof of refund to customers.
  • Ignoring the penalty proposal in the notice.
  • Not requesting a personal hearing in writing.
  • Assuming a DRC form itself explains the complete legal basis of the demand.

26. Section 76 GST FAQs

What is Section 76 of the CGST Act?

Section 76 deals with an amount collected from another person as representing GST but not paid to the Government.

Does Section 76 apply if the supply was exempt?

Yes. Section 76(1) expressly says the amount must be paid to Government irrespective of whether the underlying supply was taxable or not.

What happens if GST is collected but not deposited?

The proper officer can issue a notice requiring payment of the collected amount and proposing a penalty equivalent to the amount, subject to adjudication.

Is interest payable under Section 76?

Yes. Section 76(4) provides interest at the Section 50 rate from the date the amount was collected until it is paid to Government.

Can I request a hearing under Section 76?

Yes. Section 76(5) provides an opportunity of hearing where the person served with the notice requests it in writing.

What is the penalty under Section 76?

The notice may propose a penalty equivalent to the amount specified in the notice. The final liability follows the statutory adjudication process.

Is Section 76 the same as Section 73?

No. Section 73 historically deals with determination of tax not paid/short paid, erroneous refund or wrong ITC in non-fraud cases. Section 76 focuses on an amount actually collected from another person as tax but not paid to Government.

Does Section 76 apply to wrongly collected GST?

It can. The fact that the underlying supply was non-taxable does not by itself permit retention of an amount collected as representing GST.

What if GST was paid under another GSTIN?

The taxpayer should reconcile the GSTIN-wise liability and payment records before accepting that the amount was not paid. Tax-head and GSTIN errors may require a separate correction analysis.

What records are needed for a Section 76 notice?

Invoice records, customer ledgers, bank receipts, GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, electronic ledgers, challans, credit notes and refund evidence are particularly useful.

Does Section 76 still apply after Section 74A?

Yes. Section 74A is a tax-determination framework for newer periods; Section 76 remains the specific provision dealing with tax collected but not paid to Government.

Can Section 76 and Section 122 both be relevant?

Potentially, depending on the acts alleged and statutory provisions invoked. The same conduct should not be subjected to impermissible double penalty, and the exact statutory basis must be examined.

28. Key takeaways

  • Section 76 deals with tax collected from another person but not paid to Government.
  • It can apply even where the underlying supply is not taxable.
  • The proper officer can issue a notice for payment and propose an equivalent penalty.
  • Interest runs from the date of collection until payment to Government.
  • A written request can trigger the statutory hearing opportunity.
  • The collected amount and the penalty are separate components of the proceeding.
  • Section 76 is different from ordinary Section 73/74/74A tax determination.
  • GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, customer collections and GST payment records should be reconciled regularly.
  • Tax-head/GSTIN errors should be investigated before accepting a “not paid” allegation.
  • Section 76 remains relevant under the current GST law even after the introduction of Section 74A.
