GST ACT — SECTION 24

Compulsory Registration in Certain Cases

Section 24 identifies situations in which GST registration is compulsory even when ordinary turnover-based registration under Section 22 would not otherwise be triggered. It is therefore one of the most important provisions to test after, and together with, Sections 22 and 23.

1. What Section 24 does

Section 24 is the compulsory-registration provision. Its opening words make the basic point: the specified categories are required to register notwithstanding Section 22(1).

This means that turnover is not the only registration test. A person can be below the ordinary threshold in Section 22 and still have a registration obligation because the person's activity falls within a Section 24 category.

Practical rule: Do not conclude “turnover is below the threshold, so GST registration is not required” until Section 24 has been checked.

The original GST Council drafting already used Section 24 as a separate compulsory-registration layer over the normal threshold mechanism. citeturn0search5

3. Relationship with Sections 22 and 23

Section 22 is the normal threshold-based registration provision. Section 23 identifies persons not liable for registration and permits notification-based exemptions. Section 24 then creates compulsory-registration categories.

QuestionProvision
Has ordinary turnover threshold been crossed?Section 22
Is the person exclusively exempt/non-taxable or an agriculturist to the specified extent?Section 23(1)
Is a category specifically exempted from registration by notification?Section 23(2)
Does the person fall into a compulsory category?Section 24
Important historical/current-law point: Section 23 was retrospectively amended by the Finance Act, 2023 to give it overriding effect over Section 22(1) and Section 24. The amendment was introduced to resolve the anomaly between exempt-only suppliers and compulsory-registration categories. The GST Council's 49th meeting agenda records both the problem and the legislative solution. citeturn0search3

Therefore, for the current law, Section 23 cannot be treated as automatically subordinate to Section 24. The exact wording of Section 23, applicable notification and category-specific conditions must be checked together.

4. Section 24(i) — inter-State taxable supply

Section 24(i) covers persons making an inter-State taxable supply. Historically, this was one of the most significant departures from the ordinary threshold system: an inter-State taxable supplier could face compulsory registration even when turnover was below the ordinary threshold.

However, this clause must be read with exemption notifications issued under Section 23(2) and the nature of the supply.

Do not overgeneralise: “Inter-State supply = mandatory registration in every case” is too broad. Notification-based exemptions have been issued for specified categories, particularly certain inter-State service suppliers.

The GST Council's 32nd meeting material records the exemption for qualifying inter-State taxable services under Notification No. 10/2017-Integrated Tax. citeturn0search11

5. Section 24(ii) — casual taxable person

A casual taxable person is a person who occasionally undertakes transactions involving supply of goods or services or both in the course or furtherance of business in a State or Union territory where the person has no fixed place of business.

Registration is compulsory subject to the statutory framework. This category is particularly relevant to exhibitions, temporary project activities, events, fairs and short-duration commercial operations.

Construction/event example

A contractor based in one State takes a taxable temporary project-related commercial activity in another State without establishing a fixed place of business there. The registration analysis must consider whether the person qualifies as a casual taxable person and whether the activity creates registration liability.

Operational point: Casual taxable persons have separate registration/payment mechanics under Section 27 and the registration rules. Do not apply the ordinary “30 days from threshold crossing” approach without checking the special provisions.

6. Section 24(iii) — persons required to pay tax under reverse charge

Section 24(iii) covers persons required to pay tax under reverse charge. The provision is important because the registration trigger can arise from the recipient-side tax liability, not from the person's outward taxable turnover.

Illustration: An otherwise small business exclusively dealing in exempt outward supplies may receive a supply on which it is independently liable to pay GST under a reverse-charge notification. The interaction with Section 23 and the current statutory wording must be checked before concluding that registration is unnecessary.

The GST Council's 49th meeting agenda specifically records the concern that the 2023 Section 23 amendment could unintentionally remove registration for exempt-only persons liable to reverse charge, and records the subsequent clarification process. citeturn0search0turn0search36

Therefore, RCM registration questions should be analysed using the exact current Sections 23 and 24 wording together with the RCM notification applicable to the transaction.

7. Section 24(iv) — Section 9(5)

Section 24(iv) covers persons required to pay tax under Section 9(5). Section 9(5) shifts the tax-payment responsibility for specified categories of services supplied through an electronic commerce operator to the operator.

This is distinct from Section 24(ix), which concerns suppliers making specified supplies through an ECO that is required to collect tax at source under Section 52.

IssueSection
ECO becomes liable for tax on specified notified servicesSection 9(5), linked to Section 24(iv)
Supplier sells through ECO subject to TCS frameworkSection 52 / Section 24(ix), subject to current exemptions

8. Section 24(v) — non-resident taxable person

A non-resident taxable person undertaking taxable supplies in India falls within compulsory registration. The special registration framework is designed for persons who do not have a fixed place of business or residence in India but make taxable supplies here.

Section 27 contains the special registration period and advance-tax mechanics. Therefore, the compliance process differs materially from an ordinary domestic taxpayer.

Practical check: Determine whether the supplier is genuinely a non-resident taxable person, identify the Indian taxable activity, and then apply Sections 24(v), 25 and 27 together.

9. Section 24(vi) — TDS deductors

Persons required to deduct tax under Section 51 are included in the compulsory-registration framework. The registration is linked to the person's role as a specified GST-TDS deductor, not simply to its turnover.

This is particularly relevant for government departments, local authorities, governmental agencies and other notified categories that fall within Section 51.

Key distinction: GST-TDS registration is different from the supplier's ordinary GST registration. An entity can have a TDS registration obligation because of its deductor role even where its own outward business turnover does not cross the ordinary threshold.

10. Section 24(vii) — agents and persons supplying on behalf of others

Section 24(vii) covers persons who supply goods or services or both on behalf of other taxable persons, whether acting as an agent or otherwise.

This clause has historically generated questions about the meaning of “agent”, especially where a person merely facilitates transactions versus where the person actually supplies on behalf of a principal.

The analysis should be based on the actual contractual arrangement, ownership/title, invoicing, authority to conclude supply, stock handling and consideration rather than the label used in the agreement.

Documentation: Review principal-agent agreements, purchase/sales invoices, stock records, commission arrangements, accounting entries and authority clauses.

GST Council discussions have specifically examined the registration consequences for agents and consignment agents under Section 24(vii). citeturn0search10

11. Section 24(viii) — Input Service Distributor

An Input Service Distributor is a specialised GST mechanism for receiving eligible input-service invoices and distributing the corresponding input tax credit to specified distinct persons.

Section 24(viii) makes ISD registration part of the compulsory-registration framework. The post-1 April 2025 ISD system should be read with Section 20, Rule 39 and the current return/reporting framework.

Control point: Keep ISD registration separate from the GST registrations of operating units. The ISD registration serves the credit-distribution function; it does not replace the recipient GSTIN's normal registration.

12. Section 24(ix) and (x) — e-commerce framework

The e-commerce provisions have evolved substantially. Section 24(ix) addresses suppliers making specified supplies through an electronic commerce operator required to collect tax at source under Section 52, while Section 24(x) addresses the ECO itself where it is required to collect tax under Section 52.

The 28th GST Council meeting records the amendment to Section 24(x) so that compulsory registration for an ECO is linked to the Section 52 TCS obligation rather than applying to every ECO regardless of turnover. citeturn0search9

Later GST reforms also enabled specified small suppliers to make intra-State supplies of goods through ECOs without automatically being forced into registration, subject to conditions and the relevant notifications/rules. The 47th and 49th GST Council records discuss this interaction with Section 23(2) and Section 24(ix). citeturn0search2turn0search3

Current-law method: For an ECO transaction, identify the supplier, ECO, nature of supply, whether TCS under Section 52 applies, whether Section 9(5) applies, and the specific registration exemption available on the transaction date.

13. Section 24(xi) — cross-border online information/database access or retrieval services

Section 24(xi) addresses persons supplying online information and database access or retrieval services from a place outside India to a person in India, other than a registered person.

This provision works with the IGST framework for OIDAR services and is particularly relevant to foreign digital businesses selling directly to consumers or other unregistered persons in India.

The original GST Council draft already contemplated compulsory registration for this category, reflecting the policy objective of capturing cross-border digital consumption in India. citeturn0search5

Example: A foreign digital-content platform provides qualifying OIDAR services directly to an unregistered Indian consumer. The foreign supplier should examine the special registration and tax-payment mechanism rather than relying on the ordinary domestic turnover threshold.

14. Section 24(xia) — online money gaming from outside India

The online money gaming provisions were introduced as part of the 2023 amendments dealing with online money gaming and related digital supplies.

Section 24(xia) covers every person supplying online money gaming from a place outside India to a person in India. The GST Council's 51st meeting agenda expressly records this proposed compulsory-registration clause. citeturn0search1

This category should be read with the definitions of online gaming and online money gaming, the IGST place-of-supply provisions, Section 14A of the IGST Act where applicable, and the special payment/compliance rules for overseas suppliers.

15. Section 24(xii) — other notified categories

Section 24(xii) provides flexibility to bring additional persons or classes of persons into compulsory registration through notification on the recommendation of the GST Council.

This is a delegated legislative mechanism. The statutory clause itself does not tell you which additional category is currently covered; the operative notification must be identified.

Professional method: Never cite Section 24(xii) alone as the reason for registration. Identify the notification number, date, category, conditions and effective date.

16. Section 23 exemptions and Section 24 compulsory registration

The relationship between Sections 23 and 24 has been a recurring legislative issue. The original architecture could produce an apparent conflict: Section 23 said an exempt-only person was not liable to registration, while Section 24 independently contained compulsory categories.

The Finance Act, 2023 substituted Section 23 retrospectively from 1 July 2017 to give Section 23 overriding effect over Section 22(1) and Section 24. The GST Council's 49th meeting agenda explains that the change was intended to remove the anomaly and clarify the treatment of small exempt-only suppliers, while also recording concerns about reverse-charge taxpayers. citeturn0search3turn0search36

Practical conclusion: When Sections 23 and 24 appear to point in different directions, do not rely on the old “Section 24 always overrides Section 23” explanation. Apply the current Section 23 wording, the relevant notification and the specific category involved.

17. Legislative development — why Section 24 looks different today

2017Original compulsory-registration architecture covered inter-State taxable suppliers, casual taxable persons, RCM, Section 9(5), non-residents, TDS deductors, agents, ISD, ECOs, OIDAR and notified categories.
2017–2018Exemptions were introduced for specified categories, including qualifying inter-State service suppliers, reducing the practical reach of some compulsory-registration clauses.
2018Section 24(x) was refined so compulsory ECO registration was connected with the Section 52 TCS framework.
2019Job-worker registration guidance clarified how Section 24(i) interacts with the service threshold and Notification 10/2017-IGST.
2023Section 23 was retrospectively substituted to address its relationship with Section 24; online money gaming provisions also introduced new digital registration requirements.
2025 onwardISD and digital/e-commerce compliance continued evolving, requiring category-specific reading of the Act, Rules and notifications.

The GST Council's records show these developments were not merely administrative: the Council repeatedly considered the interaction between threshold registration, compulsory registration and notification-based exemptions. citeturn0search3turn0search9turn0search11

19. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — ₹10 lakh inter-State consultant

A small consultant supplies taxable services inter-State. Do not stop at Section 24(i); check the applicable Section 23(2) exemption notification for inter-State services and its conditions.

Example 2 — ₹5 lakh exempt trader + RCM

An exempt-only trader has a separate reverse-charge liability. The current Section 23/24 wording and the relevant RCM notification must be reviewed before concluding that no registration is required.

Example 3 — temporary exhibition

A business without a fixed place of business undertakes taxable sales at an exhibition in another State. Casual-taxable-person rules may apply.

Example 4 — government TDS deductor

A government body deducts GST TDS under Section 51. Registration arises from the TDS-deductor category rather than ordinary turnover.

Example 5 — agent

An agent sells goods on behalf of a principal under an arrangement falling within Section 24(vii). The agent's own turnover threshold is not the only registration test.

Example 6 — ISD

A head office receives common input-service invoices for group registrations and distributes eligible ITC. The ISD mechanism requires the appropriate registration.

Example 7 — foreign OIDAR

A foreign platform supplies qualifying OIDAR services directly to an unregistered Indian consumer. The special Section 24(xi) framework must be examined.

Example 8 — overseas online money gaming

A foreign online money gaming supplier provides services to Indian users. Section 24(xia) and the special digital-supply framework apply.

Example 9 — ECO supplier

A small goods supplier sells through an ECO. Check whether the ECO is required to collect TCS, whether the supplier falls within Section 24(ix), and whether the Section 23(2) exemption for specified small suppliers applies.

Example 10 — ordinary local trader

A local trader sells taxable goods only within its State and does not fall into a special Section 24 category. Registration is primarily tested under Section 22, subject to Section 23 and any applicable notification.

Apply Section 22 separately.

Do not use a generic checklist only.
6. Identify exemption notifications
Match the exact category and conditions.
7. Determine effective date
Registration liability can arise from a specific event.

Record clause, notification and effective date.

New State, new ECO, RCM, agent role or digital supply can change the answer.

22. Documentation

  • GST registration applications and certificates.
  • Inter-State supply invoices and place-of-supply analysis.
  • RCM register and relevant tax-payment notifications.
  • CTP/NRTP documentation where applicable.
  • Section 51 TDS-deductor authority/notification evidence.
  • Principal-agent agreements and transaction records.
  • ISD invoices and distribution workings.
  • ECO agreements, TCS data and supply reports.
  • OIDAR/digital-service customer-location evidence.
  • Online-money-gaming supply and recipient records where applicable.
  • Section 23 exemption notifications relied upon.
  • Historical notifications for past-period assessments.
For tax-audit or notice purposes, document the exact Section 24 clause + applicable Section 23 exemption/notification + effective date. This is much stronger than a generic statement that registration was “not required” or “required because of inter-State supply”.

23. Common mistakes

  • Checking only the turnover threshold and ignoring Section 24.
  • Assuming every inter-State service automatically requires registration without checking the applicable exemption.
  • Assuming every inter-State goods supplier gets the same exemption available to specified services.
  • Treating Section 9(5) and Section 52 as the same ECO concept.
  • Ignoring reverse-charge exposure.
  • Assuming every agent is outside registration because commission income is below the threshold.
  • Using old Section 24 wording without considering the 2018/2023 legislative changes.
  • Ignoring Section 23's retrospective overriding effect introduced in 2023.
  • Applying an exemption notification without checking conditions and effective date.
  • Failing to distinguish domestic suppliers from foreign OIDAR/online-money-gaming suppliers.

25. Key takeaways

  • Section 24 is the compulsory-registration layer of GST.
  • Turnover below Section 22 does not by itself mean registration is unnecessary.
  • Always test Section 23 before concluding that a Section 24 category creates liability.
  • Inter-State taxable supplies, CTPs, RCM, Section 9(5), NRTPs, TDS deductors, agents, ISD, ECOs and specified digital suppliers require category-specific analysis.
  • Section 23 was retrospectively substituted in 2023 with overriding effect over Section 22(1) and Section 24.
  • Notification 10/2017-IGST is an important practical exemption for qualifying inter-State taxable service suppliers.
  • E-commerce rules must be separated into Section 9(5), Section 24(ix)/(x), Section 52 and applicable Section 23(2) exemptions.
  • OIDAR and overseas online-money-gaming suppliers have special registration rules.
  • For historical matters, apply the law, notification and effective date applicable to that period.
