1. Quick decision framework: never choose a GST rate by product name alone

A classification decision should start with the actual goods or service supplied, its objective characteristics, the contractual promise, the relevant tariff heading, the exact rate-notification entry and every condition attached to that entry. A familiar trade name or an online search result is not a substitute for this chain of reasoning.

Identify the supply

What is delivered, installed, licensed, processed, repaired, rented or otherwise promised? Is there one supply or several independent supplies?

Find the legal entry

Check the applicable HSN/SAC, chapter notes, section notes, explanatory material where relevant, and the exact notification entry effective on the supply date.

Test every condition

Read exclusions, provisos, supplier/customer/premises conditions, end-use restrictions and any referenced definition.

Record the conclusion

Prepare a short classification memo with facts, competing entries considered, reasons for rejection, rate, effective date and reviewer approval.

Practical warning: The correct rate cannot safely be determined from a short description such as “equipment”, “consultancy”, “food product”, “maintenance” or “construction work”. Small factual differences can change the classification or rate entry.

2. Why GST classification disputes are common

Classification disputes often arise because commercial teams, procurement, accounts and tax teams describe the same transaction differently. A purchase order may call a transaction “AMC”; the contract may include parts, labour, software updates and emergency support. The invoice may say “service charges”. The legal analysis must consider what is actually supplied and how the applicable notification treats it.

3. Legal hierarchy: Act, Rules, tariff, notification, circular and case law

When sources appear inconsistent, identify what each source legally does and whether it applies to the period and transaction under review. Do not treat every document published by a department as interchangeable.

SourceRole in analysisPractical question
CGST / IGST ActsProvide statutory definitions, charging provisions, place of supply, valuation, composite/mixed supply and other legal rules.Which section governs the issue?
RulesPrescribe detailed procedures or methods under the enabling Act.Is there a specific rule, test or prescribed process?
Customs Tariff / HSN structureProvides headings, subheadings, notes and classification architecture for goods where incorporated or relevant under the GST framework.Which heading fits the objective characteristics?
Rate notificationSpecifies taxable rate entries, descriptions, conditions and exclusions.Does the precise entry cover this supply on the relevant date?
Exemption notificationGrants exemption subject to its wording and conditions.Are all conditions satisfied and evidenced?
Circular / instructionMay explain departmental interpretation and guide officers within its legal scope.Does it address the exact issue and period? Is it consistent with the statute?
Judicial decision / advance rulingMay interpret law; binding effect depends on the forum, parties, jurisdiction and applicable doctrine.Who is bound, and are the facts materially comparable?
Interpretation discipline: Quote the exact provision or notification entry first. Then explain the facts, apply each condition, and state the conclusion. Avoid relying on a circular summary without checking the underlying notification.

4. A repeatable 10-step classification workflow

  1. Collect the actual invoice, purchase order, agreement, technical specification and product/service literature.
  2. Describe the supply in neutral factual language, avoiding the conclusion you want to prove.
  3. Determine whether goods, services or a combination are supplied.
  4. For goods, identify material, composition, design, function, processing and principal use; for services, identify deliverables, contractual responsibility and performance.
  5. Identify plausible HSN/SAC headings and read relevant section/chapter notes.
  6. Review the rate notification and any exemption notification in force on the relevant date.
  7. Check conditions, exclusions, provisos and definitions incorporated by reference.
  8. Assess whether the transaction is composite, mixed, naturally bundled or multiple independent supplies.
  9. Compare competing classifications and explain why the alternatives are less appropriate.
  10. Approve the conclusion, set an effective date, update the ERP and schedule a review when law or product facts change.

5. HSN for goods and SAC for services: practical distinction

HSN is used for classification of goods; SAC is used for services. Classification is not complete merely because an ERP accepts a code. The code should be supported by the statutory tariff structure and relevant descriptions.

Goods classification evidenceService classification evidence
Technical data sheet, ingredients/material composition, manufacturing process, drawings, intended function, packaging and product sample/photos.Statement of work, service-level agreement, deliverables, work logs, personnel responsibility, software licence terms, acceptance certificate and pricing schedule.
Read heading and subheading text plus relevant section/chapter notes.Distinguish consulting, technical testing, maintenance, licensing, leasing, construction, transport, intermediary and other service categories on facts.
Do not assume a kit or set always follows the most expensive item.Do not assume all charges in one invoice are necessarily one service.

HSN digit requirements: Determine the applicable digit requirement from current rules, turnover category and the specific reporting/invoicing context. Do not hard-code a historic digit requirement without verifying the period-specific rule and current portal instructions.

6. How to read a GST rate notification entry

Read the notification as a legal instrument, not just a rate table copied into a spreadsheet. A rate entry may contain a serial number, chapter/heading, description, rate, unit, explanation, proviso, condition or exclusion. A heading match alone does not establish entitlement to a particular rate.

Entry description

Does the actual product or service fall within the words used?

Exclusions

Does the entry exclude a product, supplier category, premises or transaction?

Conditions

Is the rate conditional on end use, recipient type, certification, value, location or other fact?

Effective date

Was the entry in force on the legally relevant date, considering the notification’s commencement provision?

Recommended working paper: save the notification version, highlight the exact entry, record the date checked, document the facts tested against each condition and retain the approval. Where amendments exist, retain both the previous and amended text.

7. Exemptions and concessional rates: conditions are not optional

An exemption or concessional rate may be conditional. Businesses often focus on the headline phrase but miss a proviso or a condition relating to the recipient, use, premises, funding, registration or documentary evidence.

SituationQuestions to askEvidence to preserve
Supply to government or local authorityDoes the notification refer to a specified recipient, function, activity or contract? Is the recipient actually covered?Work order, recipient status, scope, certificate and contract.
Educational / healthcare related supplyIs the supplier or service category covered by the precise entry? Is the supply itself exempt, or merely connected to an exempt entity?Agreement, service description, recipient details and statutory condition matrix.
Export / SEZ / zero-rated treatmentAre place-of-supply and zero-rating requirements met? Is the transaction an export or merely a domestic supply to a foreign customer?Contract, remittance/export documents, shipping bill or authorised operations evidence as applicable.
End-use based concessionDoes the buyer’s intended use matter, and who must prove it?End-use declaration, purchase order, certificates and usage records.
Exemption claimed based on a circularDoes the circular interpret the same notification and period, and is the entry’s wording satisfied?Copy of applicable notification, circular and facts-to-condition checklist.
Do not assume: A customer’s government ownership, charitable status, educational activity or project funding does not automatically exempt every supply made to that customer.

8. Composite supply vs mixed supply

Section 2(30) defines composite supply and Section 2(74) defines mixed supply; Section 8 addresses the tax treatment. The commercial invoice format is not decisive. Examine whether supplies are naturally bundled and supplied together in the ordinary course of business, and whether one is the principal supply, or whether independent supplies are combined for a single price.

TestComposite supplyMixed supply
RelationshipNaturally bundled in ordinary course; one is principal supply.Two or more individual supplies combined for a single price, not constituting composite supply.
Rate treatmentGenerally follows the principal supply under Section 8(a).Generally follows the supply attracting the highest rate under Section 8(b).
EvidenceIndustry practice, contract, inseparability, customer expectation, pricing and delivery model.Separate utility, ability to buy independently, promotional package design and single-price structure.

Example: A seller supplies a machine with installation that is integral to making the machine operational. Analyse whether installation is naturally bundled and ancillary. By contrast, a festival hamper containing unrelated goods for one consolidated price may require a mixed-supply analysis. These are illustrations only; the actual contract and facts decide.

9. Bundled contracts: AMC, EPC, turnkey, software and installation

Many disputes arise where a contract includes multiple deliverables. Do not decide the treatment merely by the words “composite”, “turnkey” or “package” in the agreement.

EPC / turnkey projectGoods and services, immovable property, works-contract definition, project milestones and subcontracting.Scope, ownership, transfer of property, installation, testing, acceptance and statutory definition.
Software subscription with implementationAccess/licence, customisation, hosting, migration, support and training.IP rights, access period, deliverables, acceptance, renewals and separate commercial value.

10. Commercial description is not the legal classification

Labels such as “consultancy fee”, “reimbursement”, “licence”, “rent”, “handling”, “processing”, “job work”, “subscription” or “management fee” may describe the commercial arrangement but do not settle GST treatment.

  • Read the contractual rights and obligations, not only the ledger narration.
  • Identify what the recipient is entitled to receive and what the supplier is obliged to perform.
  • Check whether the activity is separately supplied or merely incidental to another supply.
  • Examine who bears responsibility for performance, risk, materials, defects and acceptance.
  • Make sure invoice descriptions, contracts, accounting codes and GST returns tell a consistent factual story.

Practical example: An amount booked as “reimbursement” may be part of the taxable value of the supplier’s own service. Rule 33’s pure-agent treatment is conditional; simply charging the expense at cost does not automatically exclude it from value.

11. Worked examples: how facts change the conclusion

Example A — repair with replacement parts

A company pays ₹1,00,000 for repair of industrial equipment, including parts and labour. Do not automatically apply the goods rate to parts or the service rate to labour. Read the agreement, identify whether the supply is a composite supply, review the applicable service entry and determine whether separately supplied goods are genuinely independent.

Example B — supply plus installation

A supplier quotes ₹10,00,000 for equipment and ₹1,00,000 for installation. Separate line items alone do not prove separate supplies; one combined amount alone does not prove a composite supply. Examine contract structure, customer choice, principal obligation, delivery and acceptance.

Example C — product with similar appearance but different composition

Two products have the same market name but different ingredients or technical properties. Compare tariff descriptions and chapter notes with composition, process and function. Keep a technical specification from the manufacturer.

Example D — concessional rate with end-use condition

A buyer provides a declaration that goods will be used in a specified project. The seller should verify whether the notification requires the declaration, a prescribed certificate, recipient eligibility or other conditions. A generic purchase order reference may be insufficient.

Example E — price change after a rate amendment

A contract is signed before a notification changes the rate, but invoice, supply and payment dates fall on different sides of the change. Determine the legally relevant time of supply and any special transitional provision; do not use contract date alone as the automatic rate date.

13. Classification evidence file: what should be retained

Maintain one digital file per product family or service line, not only a spreadsheet of HSN/SAC codes.

  • Approved classification memo with facts, issue, alternative entries, analysis and conclusion.
  • Applicable Act, tariff heading, notification entry and amendment history saved for the relevant date.
  • Technical specification, product composition, drawings, photos, catalogue or service scope.
  • Customer/vendor agreement, purchase order, work order, pricing schedule and acceptance records.
  • Relevant circulars, judicial decisions or advance rulings, with jurisdiction and factual comparability noted.
  • Invoices, credit/debit notes, sample return reporting and ERP master screenshots.
  • Tax impact calculation for past periods and corrective action, if the classification changes.
  • Approval by finance/tax and a scheduled review date.

14. How to use circulars, clarifications and advance rulings

Read a circular alongside the statute and notification it explains. Confirm the subject, issue date, amendments or withdrawal, period covered and whether it addresses the same facts. A clarification on a particular product or situation should not be extended to a materially different supply without analysis.

Advance rulings require special caution. Their statutory binding effect is generally limited to the applicant and the concerned/jurisdictional officer as provided by law. A ruling obtained by another business may be persuasive but should not automatically be treated as binding on every taxpayer.

Good practice: In the memo, separate (1) binding statutory text, (2) applicable notification entry, (3) relevant departmental clarification, (4) judicial interpretation and (5) your factual inference. This makes the reasoning transparent.

15. Rate amendments and effective dates

Rate changes can create disputes where a purchase order, dispatch, delivery, invoice, payment and completion certificate occur on different dates. The relevant date must be determined under the applicable time-of-supply provisions and any specific notification or transitional clause.

EventWhy it mattersControl
Purchase order before amendmentContract date alone may not settle tax rate.Keep supply, invoice and payment dates.
Advance before amendment, supply afterTime-of-supply rules and special provisions may affect result.Analyse each date and applicable statutory rule.
Continuous / milestone supplyDifferent milestones may have different invoicing or time-of-supply implications.Maintain milestone certification and invoice chronology.
Credit note issued laterOriginal supply date and Section 34 requirements may control adjustment.Link the note to original invoice and test statutory time limits.
Notification issued but commencement laterIssue date and effective date are not necessarily identical.Read commencement wording and publication details.

16. Invoice, ERP and return controls

Even a correct legal classification can fail operationally if the ERP master is wrong or the rate change is not deployed consistently.

  • Maintain item/service masters with description, HSN/SAC, rate, source notification, effective-from date and approval.
  • Prevent users from changing tax codes without documented approval.
  • Use separate effective-dated rates where the same item has a legally changed rate over time.
  • Reconcile invoice tax with e-invoice data, GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B.
  • Flag manual overrides, zero-rated/exempt codes, unusual rates and negative tax values.
  • Review debit notes, credit notes, returns, warranty replacements and free-of-cost supplies separately.
  • Keep a change log recording who changed a master, when and why.

17. When the department disputes your classification

  1. Identify the exact allegation: wrong HSN, wrong rate, exemption denied, composite supply, valuation or effective-date issue.
  2. Check the notice period, statutory provision, tax periods and computation.
  3. Ask which entry the department relies on and compare its wording with the taxpayer’s entry.
  4. Prepare a facts table: product/service, contractual scope, technical features, tariff entry, rate entry, condition and supporting evidence.
  5. Address each allegation separately. Do not respond with a generic statement that the classification has always been followed.
  6. Explain why competing entries do or do not apply, with the relevant notes and notification language.
  7. Quantify the demand by period and tax head; separately verify interest and penalty basis.
  8. Request a personal hearing where available and submit indexed documents with proof of filing.
  9. Review the final order for findings, reasoning, limitation, computation and whether submissions were considered.
  10. Consider rectification or appeal within the applicable statutory timeline; do not assume discussions with the officer suspend deadlines.

Sample reply structure: Background and transaction facts → issue framed → applicable law and notification entry → factual comparison → response to department’s alternative classification → supporting evidence index → tax-period-wise calculation → relief requested.

20. Frequently asked questions

Can the same HSN always be used because the trade name is the same?

No. Composition, function, design, tariff wording and relevant notes may change the classification even where trade names are similar.

Does a separate line item mean a separate supply?

Not by itself. Review the contract, commercial reality, bundling and applicable definitions.

Can we rely on an old circular?

Only after checking whether it remains applicable, addresses the same issue and is consistent with the relevant statutory and notification text.

Does a government customer automatically make the supply exempt?

No. The exact exemption entry and its conditions must be satisfied.

What if two entries appear possible?

Compare the wording, tariff notes, objective characteristics and exclusions. Record why one entry is more appropriate and seek specialist advice where material uncertainty remains.

Can we correct a classification error discovered after filing?

Review the transaction period, invoice and return correction mechanisms, time limits, tax difference, interest and possible demand implications. The appropriate route depends on facts and law; do not change historical data without a documented impact assessment.

Is a classification memo legally conclusive?

No. It is an important evidence and governance document, but the conclusion must still be correct under the law and facts.

What should we do when the rate changes?

Identify the effective date and applicable time-of-supply rules, review contracts and open orders, update ERP masters, and document the transition treatment.
