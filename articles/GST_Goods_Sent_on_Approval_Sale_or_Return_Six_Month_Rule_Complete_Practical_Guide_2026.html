1. Executive summary

Goods sent to a customer for inspection, trial, selection or approval are not automatically a completed sale merely because the goods physically leave the seller’s premises. But the GST law places a strict outer limit on invoicing where goods are removed on approval before the supply takes place.

Core rule — Section 31(7)Where goods are sent or taken on approval for sale or return and are removed before supply takes place, the tax invoice must be issued before or at the time of supply, or six months from the date of removal, whichever is earlier.

This creates two separate questions: when does the commercial supply actually take place under the contract, and what is the latest invoice date prescribed by GST law? Finance teams must track both, not wait indefinitely for a customer confirmation.

Commercial question

Has the customer accepted the goods, used them as owner, or otherwise triggered a sale under the contract?

GST compliance question

Has the invoice deadline arrived, even if the customer has not formally approved or returned the goods?

2. Legal framework and key terminology

Provision / conceptWhy it matters
CGST Act, Section 7Determines whether the transaction is a supply in the course or furtherance of business.
CGST Act, Section 31(7)Special invoice timing for goods sent or taken on approval for sale or return.
CGST Rules, Rule 55Delivery challan requirements where goods are transported without an invoice in circumstances permitted by the Rules.
Section 34Credit notes may be relevant where an invoice has been issued and the goods are later returned, subject to statutory conditions and time limits.
Section 16 and related ITC provisionsRecipient credit depends on the statutory conditions; dispatch alone should not be treated as automatic proof that all ITC conditions are met.

Approval basis: goods are delivered so that the prospective buyer can inspect, test, select or decide whether to accept them under agreed terms.

Sale-or-return: goods are delivered on terms allowing the recipient to keep them if accepted, or return them within the agreed conditions.

Delivery challan: a movement document used where the rules permit movement without a tax invoice at that stage. It is not a substitute for issuing the tax invoice when Section 31(7) requires one.

Do not confuse this with job work. Goods sent to a job worker for treatment or processing are governed by a different statutory framework. Label the movement correctly and use the corresponding register and documents.

3. Decision tree: how to classify the movement

Why are goods leaving?→Sale already agreed?→Approval / trial?→Invoice or challan?→Track deadline
  1. Sale already concluded: if the contract and facts show a supply has already taken place, follow the normal invoice rules; do not call it “approval” merely to postpone tax.
  2. Genuine approval arrangement: document the trial/selection terms, period for acceptance, return rights and who bears risk.
  3. Movement before supply: issue the appropriate delivery challan and transport documents where legally applicable.
  4. Acceptance or other supply trigger occurs: issue the tax invoice at that point.
  5. No acceptance and no return by six months: escalate and issue the invoice by the statutory deadline, subject to the actual legal and contractual facts.
Decision checkpointDo not assume that an internal ERP status such as “awaiting approval” postpones the statutory invoice deadline. The date of physical removal must be captured at dispatch.

4. The six-month invoice deadline under Section 31(7)

The statutory rule uses the earlier of two dates: (1) before or at the time supply takes place, and (2) six months from removal of the goods. This is not a general six-month credit period and not a general permission to delay tax on ordinary sales.

EventPractical treatment
Customer approves on day 20Issue invoice at or before supply as determined by the facts and contract; do not wait until month six.
Customer rejects and returns on day 45Document the rejection and receipt of goods; assess whether any supply occurred and whether invoice/credit note treatment is needed based on the facts.
No response by six monthsDo not leave the item open indefinitely; invoice deadline under Section 31(7) is reached.
Customer starts using goods as owner before formal approvalThat conduct may indicate acceptance or a supply under the contract; assess the actual transaction and invoice promptly.

Deadline calculation: maintain the dispatch/removal date and configure a reminder well before the six-month anniversary. For a live transaction, verify the precise date calculation, any applicable statutory amendment and facts before filing.

5. Delivery challan, e-way bill and movement records

Where goods move before the supply takes place, the business should use the document required by the applicable provisions. Rule 55 describes circumstances and particulars for delivery challans, including certain movements for reasons other than supply and other prescribed situations.

  • Unique challan number and date.
  • Name, address and GSTIN/UIN, where applicable, of consignor and consignee.
  • HSN, description, quantity and value of goods.
  • Reason for movement clearly recorded as “approval / sale-or-return / trial”, if factually correct.
  • Dispatch date, transporter/vehicle details and e-way bill details where required.
  • Customer approval/rejection communication and return receipt details.
  • Cross-reference to the eventual tax invoice or return challan.
Documentation warning: a challan that says “approval” does not establish the legal character of a transaction by itself. The purchase order, contract, emails, actual use, payment terms and subsequent conduct should tell a consistent story.

6. When does supply occur before six months?

Section 31(7) does not permit a business to delay invoicing after a supply has already taken place. The supply date depends on the contractual terms and the actual facts, including whether the customer accepted the goods, exercised ownership rights, consumed the goods, resold them or otherwise acted inconsistently with a genuine approval arrangement.

Evidence to review

  • Written acceptance, signed trial report or purchase confirmation.
  • Customer use, installation, consumption or resale of goods.
  • Contract terms describing when title, risk and acceptance pass.
  • Whether the customer can genuinely reject and return the goods.
  • Whether the supplier continued to control the goods and could retrieve them.

There is no universal rule that the GST supply date always equals the date on which title passes under every commercial contract. Apply the GST statute and the complete transaction facts; do not rely on a single clause in isolation.

7. Rejected goods, returns and partial acceptance

Maintain separate outcomes for accepted, rejected, partially accepted, damaged and untraceable goods. The return of goods before any supply has taken place is not necessarily the same as a customer returning goods after a taxable sale has already been invoiced.

ScenarioControl and GST review
Full rejection before supplyRecord rejection, goods receipt and inventory re-entry; verify whether the original movement remained an approval movement.
Partial acceptanceInvoice accepted quantities when supply occurs; continue tracking the unaccepted balance separately.
Return after invoice and supplyConsider Section 34 credit note conditions, statutory reporting cut-off and commercial evidence.
Goods damaged during trialDetermine whether goods are returned, scrapped, compensated or retained; each outcome may have different tax consequences.
Goods never returnedInvestigate immediately; six-month deadline is not an excuse to leave inventory unresolved.

A credit note should not be issued automatically for every physical return. First establish whether an invoice and supply had occurred, whether statutory credit-note conditions are met and whether the permitted adjustment period remains open.

8. Accounting entries and GST reporting workflow

Accounting policies differ by ERP and contract structure. The entries below are illustrative controls, not a substitute for the entity’s accounting policy or the specific tax facts.

EventIllustrative accounting / control
Goods dispatched on genuine approval basisMove quantity to “goods with customers on approval” sub-ledger; maintain inventory ownership and cost records as appropriate.
Supply occurs and invoice is raisedRecognise sales and output GST under the applicable accounting policy; clear approval-stock quantity.
Goods rejected and returned before supplyReconcile physical receipt, quality inspection and inventory return; close approval challan.
Six-month deadline reached without acceptance/returnEscalate to tax owner, determine invoice treatment under Section 31(7), report the transaction in the appropriate return period.

Do not include a mere challan as a normal taxable sales invoice in the outward-supply return unless the legal supply/reporting event has arisen. Conversely, do not omit a supply merely because the ERP still labels it “approval stock”.

9. Worked examples with solutions

Example 1 — Customer approves within one month

Goods worth ₹5,00,000 are dispatched on 10 April on genuine approval terms. The customer signs acceptance on 25 April.

Solution: determine the supply date from the contract and facts; issue the tax invoice at or before the supply. Do not wait for the six-month deadline.

Example 2 — No response after five months

Goods are removed on 1 May. The customer neither accepts nor returns them.

Solution: place the case on the six-month exception report and obtain tax review well before the deadline. Section 31(7) requires invoicing no later than six months from removal if supply has not occurred earlier.

Example 3 — Partial approval

100 units are sent; the customer accepts 70 and rejects 30.

Solution: invoice the accepted quantity when supply occurs; obtain evidence and arrange return of 30 units. Keep separate challan/quantity balances and do not allow the rejected balance to disappear from tracking.

Example 4 — Trial machine is used commercially

A customer receives a machine for a 30-day trial but puts it into commercial production and refuses to sign the acceptance form.

Solution: escalate; actual conduct may be inconsistent with continued approval status. Review the contract, use evidence and supply date; formal sign-off is not the only fact that matters.

Example 5 — Goods returned after a tax invoice

The customer accepted goods, was invoiced, and returns defective goods two months later.

Solution: this is not simply a pre-supply rejection. Review Section 34 and the applicable credit-note rules, return evidence and statutory deadline before reducing output tax.

Example 6 — Exporter sends goods abroad for approval

An exporter dispatches specified goods abroad for customer approval.

Solution: do not automatically treat dispatch as a completed export supply or apply a generic domestic workflow. Check the specific statutory provisions, current CBIC clarifications, customs/export documents and applicable conditions for the particular goods and arrangement.

Example 7 — Approval stock disappears from the register

ERP shows 25 units sent to a distributor; physical count and customer confirmation are missing.

Solution: do not assume sale or no sale without investigation. Reconcile dispatch proof, customer ledger, inventory, correspondence, invoice records and the six-month deadline; document the conclusion and corrective action.

10. Approval basis versus principal-agent arrangements

Goods sent to a customer for approval are not automatically the same as goods supplied to an agent for onward sale on behalf of the principal. Schedule I contains specific rules for certain principal-agent goods transactions. The legal relationship and the actual role of the intermediary matter.

QuestionWhy it matters
Does the recipient buy for itself or sell on behalf of the supplier?Helps distinguish buyer approval from agency.
Who invoices the ultimate customer?Evidence of the commercial structure, though not conclusive alone.
Who bears inventory risk and controls resale price?Helps assess the real relationship.
Is there a commission/agency agreement?May indicate a separate agency supply and different documentation.

Do not use an approval challan to disguise a consignment/agency arrangement or vice versa. Document the intended structure and test it against the statutory definition and Schedule I.

12. ERP design and monthly exception reports

Configure an approval-stock register with, at minimum, these fields:

  • Challan number, removal date, customer GSTIN and delivery address.
  • Item code, HSN, quantity, taxable value estimate and serial/batch number.
  • Contract/PO reference and approval/return terms.
  • Expected approval date, six-month deadline and responsible owner.
  • Accepted quantity/date, invoice number/date and tax period.
  • Rejected quantity/date, return challan and warehouse receipt.
  • Open quantity, days outstanding, ageing bucket and exception status.
Recommended alertsTrigger alerts at 90, 120, 150 and 165 days after removal, plus a daily overdue exception. Escalate items with missing customer response or missing return proof.

Monthly close owner should reconcile approval stock to physical inventory, customer confirmations, sales invoices, credit notes, e-way bills, GST returns and the general ledger.

13. Industry-specific scenarios

Manufacturers

Prototype parts, sample components, machinery demonstrations and trial production lots. Track serial numbers and customer test reports.

Electronics distributors

Demo laptops, phones and devices sent to dealers or institutional customers. Record activation/use and serial-number-level acceptance.

Pharma / medical equipment

Demo equipment and evaluation units require clear distinction between samples, consumables and approval sales; separate rules may apply to other categories.

Jewellery / high-value goods

Customer inspection, consignment and approval arrangements can have material exposure. Record custody, valuation, security and return evidence.

Industrial tools

Tools supplied for a trial or field test may be consumed or used; usage can affect whether the transaction is still genuinely awaiting approval.

Export businesses

Review the specific export/customs framework and any applicable CBIC clarification rather than assuming the domestic approval process answers every export question.

14. Audit and departmental review file

Prepare one folder or digital workpaper per material approval-stock exception containing:

  • Customer contract, PO and approval terms.
  • Delivery challan and transport/e-way bill records, where applicable.
  • Removal date and six-month deadline calculation.
  • Customer acceptance/rejection emails and test reports.
  • Proof of return, receipt, inspection and inventory re-entry.
  • Tax invoice, credit note and GSTR-1/GSTR-3B references, where relevant.
  • Written tax conclusion for overdue, damaged, disputed or missing goods.
  • Reconciliation from opening approval stock + dispatches − accepted/invoiced − returns = closing approval stock.

Ensure the quantity roll-forward reconciles to the stock ledger and that every closed item has a clear outcome.

15. Month-end checklist

  • ☐ Extract all challans for approval/sale-or-return movements.
  • ☐ Confirm dispatch/removal dates and customer details.
  • ☐ Identify approvals, rejections, partial approvals and usage evidence.
  • ☐ Calculate days outstanding and six-month deadlines.
  • ☐ Invoice supplies that have occurred.
  • ☐ Escalate items nearing six months and invoice as required by law.
  • ☐ Close returned quantities only after warehouse receipt evidence.
  • ☐ Review whether any invoiced return needs Section 34 analysis.
  • ☐ Reconcile challans, sales register, inventory, e-way bills and GST returns.
  • ☐ Document unresolved differences and assign owners/deadlines.

16. Frequently asked questions

1. Is GST payable immediately when goods are sent for approval?

Not necessarily. Where the genuine approval movement precedes supply, the invoice timing is governed by Section 31(7), but a supply occurring earlier must be invoiced earlier.

2. Can we keep goods on approval for more than six months?

The commercial custody arrangement may continue, but Section 31(7) sets the invoice deadline for goods removed on approval before supply. Do not treat an extension in the commercial contract as automatically extending the GST deadline.

3. Does the six months run from invoice date?

No. Section 31(7) refers to six months from the date of removal of the goods.

4. Is a delivery challan sufficient for the whole six months?

It documents the permitted movement, but it does not replace the tax invoice when the supply occurs or Section 31(7) requires invoicing.

5. What if the buyer never responds?

Escalate the case and apply the invoice deadline. Silence is not a reason to leave the item open indefinitely.

6. What if only part of the goods is accepted?

Track and invoice the accepted quantity when supply occurs; continue to track the balance until returned or otherwise resolved.

7. If goods are returned before acceptance, must we issue a credit note?

Not automatically. Establish whether a supply/invoice had already occurred. A pre-supply rejection differs from a return after a completed sale.

8. What if goods are returned after invoice?

Review Section 34, the return facts, credit-note conditions and the applicable statutory time limit before adjusting output tax.

9. Does approval stock count as a sale in GSTR-1 on dispatch?

Do not report a mere approval challan as a taxable invoice solely because goods moved. Determine when supply takes place and apply the invoice/return rules.

10. Is approval basis the same as job work?

No. Job work involves treatment or processing of another person's goods and has a distinct statutory framework.

11. Is approval basis the same as agent stock?

No. A principal-agent relationship may trigger separate Schedule I considerations. Review who sells, on whose behalf, and the actual contract.

12. What is the best internal control?

A challan-level register with a six-month deadline, customer confirmation, quantity roll-forward and automated ageing alerts.

Final practical rule: approval stock must never be an unowned exception. Every movement should have a removal date, supporting document, responsible owner, decision deadline and final outcome.
