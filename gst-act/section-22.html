GST ACT — SECTION 22

Persons Liable for Registration

Section 22 is the starting point for ordinary GST registration liability. It establishes the aggregate-turnover threshold, provides the statutory framework for enhanced thresholds for certain suppliers of goods and special-category States, and deals with registration arising from existing registrations, transfer of business, succession, amalgamation and demerger.

1. What Section 22 does

Section 22 answers the first registration question: when does a supplier become liable to obtain GST registration because of turnover?

The provision works together with Sections 23 and 24. Section 22 contains the ordinary turnover-based rule; Section 23 identifies persons who are not liable or who may be exempted by notification; and Section 24 creates specified compulsory-registration situations notwithstanding the ordinary threshold.

Important distinction: ₹40 lakh is commonly described as the GST registration threshold for goods, but it is not the basic number written into Section 22(1). The statutory framework retains ₹20 lakh as the general threshold and permits the Government to enhance it for eligible exclusive suppliers of goods, subject to conditions. The practical ₹40 lakh exemption comes through the notification framework.

The GST Council's 32nd meeting recommended the higher threshold for suppliers of goods, and Notification No. 10/2019-Central Tax dated 7 March 2019 implemented an exemption for eligible persons exclusively supplying goods up to ₹40 lakh from 1 April 2019. citeturn0search0turn0search4

3. Basic registration threshold

Section 22(1) provides that every supplier is liable to be registered in the State or Union territory from which taxable supplies are made when the person's aggregate turnover in a financial year exceeds the applicable threshold.

The basic statutory amount is ₹20 lakh for suppliers in the ordinary framework. The first proviso provides a lower statutory threshold of ₹10 lakh for supplies from special-category States, while subsequent provisos permit the Government, on Council recommendation and subject to conditions, to enhance the threshold.

Do not confuse Section 22 with Section 24. A person may be below the Section 22 turnover threshold but still require registration because Section 24 creates compulsory-registration categories, subject to the exemptions/notifications applicable to that category.

CBIC's registration FAQ explains the basic PAN-level aggregate-turnover concept and notes that compulsory-registration exceptions must also be considered. citeturn1search2

4. Aggregate turnover — the number that drives Section 22

Section 2(6) defines aggregate turnover on an all-India PAN basis. Broadly, it includes the aggregate value of taxable supplies, exempt supplies, exports and inter-State supplies of persons having the same PAN, while excluding specified inward supplies on which tax is payable under reverse charge and the GST/cess components themselves.

Therefore, a business cannot normally calculate the threshold simply from the taxable sales of one GSTIN. The relevant turnover must be tested across the PAN as prescribed.

Example: A company has ₹14 lakh turnover from Telangana, ₹9 lakh from Karnataka and ₹4 lakh from Maharashtra. Even though each location individually is below ₹20 lakh, the PAN-level aggregate turnover is ₹27 lakh. The threshold analysis therefore cannot be performed GSTIN by GSTIN in isolation.

5. ₹40 lakh threshold for eligible exclusive suppliers of goods

This is one of the most misunderstood aspects of Section 22.

The Act permits enhancement of the threshold for a supplier engaged exclusively in the supply of goods, subject to notified conditions. The Government exercised the registration-exemption power through Notification No. 10/2019-Central Tax dated 7 March 2019, effective from 1 April 2019.

The notification provides exemption from registration for a person engaged in exclusive supply of goods whose aggregate turnover does not exceed ₹40 lakh, subject to its conditions and exclusions.

6. Special-category States and the ₹20 lakh enhanced ceiling

Section 22 originally used ₹10 lakh for supplies from special-category States. The 2019 amendments created a mechanism allowing the Government, at the request of a special-category State and on Council recommendation, to enhance the amount up to ₹20 lakh subject to conditions.

This is why historical GST material can show ₹10 lakh, ₹20 lakh and different State combinations depending on the date and the particular notification.

ConceptAmount
Original statutory special-category threshold₹10 lakh
Maximum enhancement permitted by Section 22(1)Up to ₹20 lakh, subject to notification.
Current practical analysisCheck the applicable State, nature of supply and notification rather than relying on a generic “special category = ₹10 lakh” statement.
Historical caution: The expression “special category States” and the State list have changed through statutory amendments and notifications. For a historical registration dispute, identify the State and law applicable on that date.

7. Services — why ₹20 lakh generally remains the key threshold

The ₹40 lakh notification was designed for persons engaged in exclusive supply of goods. It should not be treated as a general ₹40 lakh threshold for service providers.

For ordinary service providers, the ₹20 lakh threshold remains the central benchmark, with the special-category framework and specific notification-based exemptions needing to be checked where relevant.

Example: A consultancy firm with ₹32 lakh turnover cannot ordinarily rely on the ₹40 lakh goods exemption merely because its turnover is below ₹40 lakh. The business is a service supplier and must perform the registration analysis under the service-provider threshold and applicable Section 23/24 notifications.

CBIC's historical registration update specifically distinguished the ₹40 lakh goods threshold from the ₹20 lakh services threshold. citeturn1search27

9. Existing registrations — Section 22(2)

Section 22(2) is principally a transitional provision. A person who, immediately before the appointed day, was registered or held a licence under an existing law was liable to registration under GST with effect from the appointed day.

This provision was important at GST migration because businesses already registered under central excise, service tax, VAT and other predecessor laws needed to transition into the GST registration framework.

For present-day new registrations, Section 22(1) is normally the operational threshold provision. Section 22(2) should mainly be understood in its historical transition context.

10. Transfer or succession of business — Section 22(3)

Where a business carried on by a taxable person registered under GST is transferred, whether by succession or otherwise, to another person as a going concern, the transferee or successor becomes liable to registration from the date of the transfer or succession.

Why this is different from turnover registration

This provision does not wait for the transferee to cross the ordinary turnover threshold. The liability arises because the registered business has been transferred as a going concern.

EventSection 22(3) question
Sale of running businessHas the business been transferred as a going concern?
SuccessionHas another person succeeded to the registered business?
Asset-only saleDoes the transaction actually amount to transfer of the business as a going concern?

11. Amalgamation and demerger — Section 22(4)

Section 22(4) contains a special registration rule for a transfer pursuant to a sanctioned scheme or arrangement for amalgamation or demerger of companies.

The transferee becomes liable to registration from the date on which the Registrar of Companies issues the certificate of incorporation giving effect to the relevant order of the High Court, Tribunal or other competent process referred to in the provision.

Practical implication: In a merger/demerger, registration planning should be linked to the legal effective date rather than waiting for the transferee's turnover to cross the ordinary threshold.

This provision should be coordinated with Section 18(3), Rule 41 and the GST registration/cancellation process so that ITC and business continuity are not disrupted.

12. Job-work turnover — special explanation

Section 22 contains a specific explanation dealing with goods supplied after completion of job work by a registered job worker.

Where the statutory Section 143 framework treats the post-job-work supply as the supply of the principal, the value of those goods is not included in the aggregate turnover of the registered job worker for Section 22 purposes.

Example: A registered job worker processes material belonging to Principal A and supplies the processed goods under the permitted Section 143 arrangement. The statutory explanation prevents the value treated as the principal's supply from artificially inflating the job worker's own aggregate turnover.

13. PAN and multi-State turnover

Registration is State/UT specific, but aggregate turnover is broadly computed on an all-India basis for persons having the same PAN.

14. Section 23 — persons not liable for registration

Section 22 cannot be read alone. Section 23 excludes certain persons from registration, including persons engaged exclusively in supplies that are not liable to tax or are wholly exempt and agriculturists to the extent of produce from cultivation of land.

Section 23(2) also empowers the Government, on Council recommendation, to notify additional categories exempt from obtaining registration.

Practical rule: A turnover calculation can show a business above ₹20 lakh or ₹40 lakh, but the final registration conclusion still requires checking Section 23 and the relevant exemption notifications.

CBIC's statutory text confirms the Section 23 exclusions and notification power. citeturn1search7

15. Section 24 — compulsory registration overrides Section 22

Section 24 begins with a non-obstante clause and identifies categories requiring compulsory registration notwithstanding the normal Section 22 threshold, subject to specific statutory and notification-based exceptions.

Historically, these categories have included specified inter-State suppliers, casual taxable persons, persons liable under reverse charge, non-resident taxable persons, TDS/TCS-related persons, agents, certain e-commerce participants and OIDAR suppliers, among others.

Registration test: “Turnover below threshold” is not by itself a complete answer. Always perform a Section 22 + Section 23 + Section 24 analysis.

CBIC's statutory material sets out the compulsory-registration categories under Section 24. citeturn1search7

16. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — goods trader at ₹38 lakh

An eligible trader exclusively supplying goods has aggregate turnover of ₹38 lakh and satisfies Notification 10/2019 conditions. The ₹40 lakh exemption may apply.

Example 2 — goods trader at ₹42 lakh

Once the relevant aggregate turnover exceeds ₹40 lakh, the exclusive-goods exemption is no longer available and registration should be examined from the date liability arises.

Example 3 — service provider at ₹38 lakh

A consultancy business cannot simply use the ₹40 lakh goods exemption. The ordinary service threshold and applicable notifications must be considered.

Example 4 — PAN has three GST locations

Telangana ₹14 lakh + Karnataka ₹9 lakh + Maharashtra ₹4 lakh = ₹27 lakh aggregate turnover. The threshold test is not performed independently for each GSTIN.

Example 5 — exempt-only business

A person exclusively making wholly exempt supplies may fall within Section 23 even if the gross value is high.

Example 6 — business transfer

A registered business is transferred as a going concern to another entity. The transferee's registration liability arises under Section 22(3) from the transfer/succession date, not merely when its turnover crosses ₹20/₹40 lakh.

Example 7 — merger

A company is amalgamated pursuant to an approved scheme. Section 22(4) links registration liability to the certificate of incorporation giving effect to the order.

Example 8 — job worker

A registered job worker processes ₹30 lakh of principal-owned goods and supplies the processed goods under Section 143. The statutory Section 22 explanation prevents that principal's supply from inflating the job worker's aggregate turnover.

Example 9 — inter-State service provider

A service provider below the normal threshold should separately test the applicable Section 24 provisions and notifications governing inter-State services. A generic “below ₹20 lakh = no registration” conclusion can be wrong.

Example 10 — compulsory category

A person may have only ₹8 lakh turnover but still require registration if a compulsory-registration provision applies and no relevant exemption notification removes the requirement.

23. Common mistakes

  • Calling ₹40 lakh the universal GST registration threshold.
  • Applying the goods threshold to service providers.
  • Calculating turnover separately for each GSTIN instead of applying the PAN-level aggregate-turnover concept.
  • Ignoring exempt and zero-rated supplies in aggregate turnover.
  • Including inward RCM supplies in aggregate turnover.
  • Ignoring Section 23 because turnover is high.
  • Ignoring Section 24 because turnover is low.
  • Assuming every special-category State automatically has a ₹10 lakh threshold without checking later amendments/notifications.
  • Ignoring the conditions in Notification 10/2019-Central Tax.
  • Failing to examine transfer/succession separately from turnover threshold.
  • Missing the ROC effective date in an amalgamation/demerger.
  • Including principal-owned post-job-work supplies in a registered job worker's turnover where the statutory explanation excludes them.

25. Key takeaways

  • Section 22 is the ordinary turnover-based registration provision.
  • ₹20 lakh is the basic statutory threshold framework; ₹40 lakh is not a universal threshold.
  • The ₹40 lakh benefit is for eligible exclusive suppliers of goods subject to the notified conditions.
  • Ordinary service suppliers should generally work with the ₹20 lakh threshold framework, subject to applicable exemptions.
  • Aggregate turnover is a PAN-level concept, while registration is State/UT specific.
  • Section 23 and Section 24 must always be checked before concluding that registration is or is not required.
  • Transfer/succession under Section 22(3) can create liability independently of turnover.
  • Amalgamation/demerger under Section 22(4) has a specific effective-date rule.
  • Job-work turnover receives a specific statutory treatment under the Section 22 explanation.
  • For historical disputes, identify the exact threshold, State list, notification and law applicable to the relevant date.
