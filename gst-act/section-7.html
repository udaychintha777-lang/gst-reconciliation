Section 7 — Scope of Supply

Section 7 is the starting point for deciding whether a transaction enters the GST framework. It defines the breadth of “supply”, identifies transactions that can qualify even without ordinary consideration, separates classification from the existence of a supply, and expressly keeps specified activities outside the concept of supply.

CGST Act, 2017 In force from 1 July 2017 Schedules I, II & III linked Detailed practical guide

1. Why Section 7 is one of the most important GST provisions

GST does not begin merely because money has moved from one person to another. The legal starting point is whether there is a supply within the meaning of the CGST Act. Section 9 is the charging provision, but Section 7 determines the transaction universe on which that charging architecture can operate.

For practical work, Section 7 should therefore be read as a structured decision framework rather than as a short definition. A transaction may enter the framework through an ordinary business supply for consideration, through import of services for consideration, or through specified activities in Schedule I even without consideration. Conversely, Schedule III transactions are expressly kept outside the supply concept, and Section 7(1A) makes clear that Schedule II is primarily a classification mechanism for activities that have already qualified as supply.

Core principle: First establish whether a supply exists. Then determine whether it is goods or services. Only after that should the practitioner move to the applicable charging, rate, place-of-supply, valuation, time-of-supply and compliance provisions.

2. Statutory text — Section 7

7. Scope of supply.

(1) For the purposes of this Act, the expression “supply” includes—

(a) all forms of supply of goods or services or both such as sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease or disposal made or agreed to be made for a consideration by a person in the course or furtherance of business;

(aa) the activities or transactions, by a person, other than an individual, to its members or constituents or vice-versa, for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration.

Explanation.—For the purposes of this clause, it is hereby clarified that, notwithstanding anything contained in any other law for the time being in force or any judgment, decree or order of any Court, tribunal or authority, the person and its members or constituents shall be deemed to be two separate persons and the supply of activities or transactions inter se shall be deemed to take place from one such person to another;

(b) import of services for a consideration whether or not in the course or furtherance of business; and

(c) the activities specified in Schedule I, made or agreed to be made without a consideration;

(1A) Where certain activities or transactions constitute a supply in accordance with the provisions of sub-section (1), they shall be treated either as supply of goods or supply of services as referred to in Schedule II.

(2) Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (1),—
(a) activities or transactions specified in Schedule III; or
(b) such activities or transactions undertaken by the Central Government, a State Government or any local authority in which they are engaged as public authorities, as may be notified by the Government on the recommendations of the Council,
shall be treated neither as a supply of goods nor a supply of services.

(3) Subject to the provisions of sub-sections (1), (1A) and (2), the Government may, on the recommendations of the Council, specify, by notification, the transactions that are to be treated as—
(a) a supply of goods and not as a supply of services; or
(b) a supply of services and not as a supply of goods.

Important drafting point: The present text is materially different from the original 2017 architecture. Clause (d) of Section 7(1) was removed and sub-section (1A) was introduced so that Schedule II classifies a transaction that already qualifies as supply rather than independently creating the taxable event.

4. Section 7(1)(a) — ordinary business supply

Section 7(1)(a) is the provision most frequently used in day-to-day GST analysis. It is deliberately broad: it says “all forms of supply” and then gives examples such as sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease and disposal.

Three central elements must normally be examined together:

  1. There must be goods, services or both. The subject matter must fall within the statutory framework.
  2. There must be consideration. Consideration may be monetary or non-monetary, subject to the definition and surrounding provisions.
  3. The activity must be by a person in the course or furtherance of business. A private or purely personal transaction is not automatically a GST supply merely because something has changed hands.

4.1 “All forms of supply” is wider than a conventional sale

A common mistake is to equate supply with sale. GST deliberately covers a much wider range of commercial arrangements. For example, a lease of commercial premises, a licence to use an asset, a barter arrangement, or a service provided for a non-cash consideration may all require Section 7 analysis.

Example — barter: A contractor provides construction services to another business and receives machinery-use rights instead of cash. The absence of a cash invoice does not automatically take the arrangement outside GST. The consideration and business nexus have to be examined under the Act.

4.2 “Made or agreed to be made”

The wording covers both an actual supply and an agreement to make a supply. This matters in contracts, framework arrangements, long-term service agreements, leases, construction contracts and other commercial arrangements where the legal and tax consequences can arise from the contractual structure even before every performance step has occurred.

4.3 “In the course or furtherance of business”

The expression connects Section 7 to the broad definition of “business” in Section 2(17). It is not restricted to the taxpayer's main line of business. Incidental, ancillary or supporting activities can also require examination where they have the necessary business nexus.

5. Section 7(1)(aa) — transactions between persons and their members or constituents

Clause (aa) was inserted by the Finance Act, 2021 with retrospective effect from 1 July 2017. The provision specifically addresses activities or transactions by a person, other than an individual, to its members or constituents or vice-versa, for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration.

The accompanying Explanation creates a statutory deeming fiction: for this clause, the person and its members or constituents are deemed to be two separate persons, and the inter se supply is deemed to occur from one to the other.

Why this matters: This amendment was aimed at the GST treatment of transactions involving clubs, associations and similar organisations and the doctrine of mutuality. It was not merely a rate amendment; it changed the statutory architecture used to analyse whether the association and its members can be treated as separate persons for this purpose.

5.1 Retrospective operation and effective date

The Finance Act, 2021 inserted clause (aa) with effect from 1 July 2017. Notification No. 39/2021-Central Tax dated 21 December 2021 brought the relevant Finance Act provisions into force from 1 January 2022. The distinction is important: the legal amendment was given retrospective effect, while the notification specified the date from which the enacted provision was brought into force administratively. citeturn2search0turn2search2

5.2 Judicial development — mutuality

The provision has been subject to constitutional litigation. In April 2025, the Kerala High Court in Indian Medical Association, Kerala State Branch v. Union of India declared Section 7(1)(aa), its Explanation and the corresponding provision under the Kerala GST law unconstitutional and void on the grounds stated in that judgment. That decision is an important part of the legal history of the provision, but it should not be treated as silently deleting the provision from the Central Act. The current statutory text available through India Code continued to show Section 7(1)(aa) as part of the Act as at September 2026. citeturn5search0turn5search4turn4search0

Professional caution: For an association/member dispute, do not rely only on a bare-text reading. Check the latest binding appellate position, the precise facts, the relevant State GST provision and the period involved before taking a tax position.
Example — club membership: A non-individual association collects a membership amount and provides facilities to members. Section 7(1)(aa) requires the transaction to be examined under its specific wording, including the nature of the person, the member relationship and the consideration. The mutuality litigation must also be considered for the relevant period.

6. Section 7(1)(b) — import of services for consideration

This limb is intentionally broader than Section 7(1)(a) in one important respect. It covers import of services for a consideration whether or not in the course or furtherance of business.

Therefore, the ordinary business-nexus condition in clause (a) should not simply be copied into clause (b). The practitioner must instead analyse the statutory definition of import of services under the IGST Act, the recipient/supplier relationship, place of supply and the applicable reverse-charge provisions.

Example: An Indian person receives a consultancy service from a foreign supplier for consideration. The analysis does not stop at asking whether the Indian recipient is carrying on business. The import-of-service framework under the IGST Act must be applied together with Section 7.

7. Section 7(1)(c) — Schedule I supplies without consideration

One of the most important practical points is that consideration is not universally required. Section 7(1)(c) expressly brings activities specified in Schedule I into the concept of supply even where they are made or agreed to be made without consideration.

Schedule I includes, subject to its own conditions, categories such as:

  • Permanent transfer or disposal of business assets where input tax credit has been availed on those assets.
  • Supply between related persons or distinct persons in the course or furtherance of business.
  • Certain supplies of goods between principal and agent and agent and principal.
  • Import of services by a taxable person from a related person or from another establishment outside India in the course or furtherance of business.
Important: Do not write “no consideration means no GST” as a general rule. The correct question is whether the transaction is one of the no-consideration supplies specifically brought within Schedule I.
Example — branch transfer: A company transfers goods from one GST registration to another registration of the same legal entity in another State. The parties may not negotiate a normal sale price, but the distinct-person provisions and Schedule I can bring the transaction within supply.

8. Section 7(1A) — Schedule II classifies; it does not independently create supply

This is one of the most important changes in the history of Section 7.

The original 2017 wording included Schedule II activities directly within Section 7(1). This drafting structure created an interpretational problem: it could appear that merely falling within Schedule II was enough to make an activity a supply.

The 28th GST Council process specifically considered this issue. The Council agenda records that Schedule II was intended to classify certain activities as goods or services where they already qualified as supply, and that the earlier drafting could create the unintended result of treating a Schedule II activity as supply even where the requirements of Section 7(1) were not otherwise satisfied. The Council therefore considered inserting Section 7(1A) and removing clause (d) of Section 7(1). citeturn0search1turn4search5

The result is the present architecture: first establish supply under Section 7(1); then use Schedule II to classify the qualifying supply as goods or services.

Example — works/contractual activity: If an activity is listed in Schedule II, that listing should not be treated as a shortcut that automatically creates a supply. The underlying Section 7(1) test must still be considered. Once a supply exists, Schedule II can determine whether the supply is treated as a supply of goods or services.

9. Section 7(2) — transactions treated as neither supply of goods nor supply of services

Section 7(2) begins with a non-obstante clause: “Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (1)”. This is significant because it creates an express statutory override.

Two broad categories are covered:

  1. Schedule III activities or transactions.
  2. Specified activities of Central Government, State Government or local authorities acting as public authorities, where the Government notifies them on the recommendation of the GST Council.

10. Major amendment history of Section 7

1 July 2017 — original GST commencement

Section 7 came into force as part of the main CGST provisions from 1 July 2017. The original architecture included clause (d) in Section 7(1), linking Schedule II directly with the concept of supply.

2018 — restructuring of Section 7

The CGST Amendment Act, 2018 restructured Section 7 by introducing sub-section (1A) and removing the earlier clause (d) from Section 7(1), with retrospective effect from 1 July 2017 and operational commencement from 1 February 2019. The policy reason recorded in GST Council material was to make Schedule II a classification mechanism rather than an independent source of supply.

2021 — Section 7(1)(aa)

Finance Act, 2021 inserted clause (aa) dealing with transactions between a person other than an individual and its members or constituents, together with a separate-person deeming Explanation. The amendment was given retrospective effect from 1 July 2017; Notification No. 39/2021-Central Tax brought the relevant Finance Act provisions into force from 1 January 2022. citeturn2search0turn2search2

2023 — Schedule III changes connected with Section 7

The 2023 amendments changed the actionable-claim framework by replacing the earlier Schedule III wording with the concept of “specified actionable claims”. The 2023 legislative package also inserted Schedule III paragraphs 7 and 8 with retrospective effect from 1 July 2017, dealing with specified offshore and warehoused-goods transactions. These changes were brought into operation through the 2023 notification framework.

2025–2026 — continuing judicial interpretation

Section 7 continues to be litigated because the meaning of supply affects charging, valuation, classification, exemptions, reverse charge, place of supply and the treatment of emerging transaction models. The statutory text available as at September 2026 continues to contain Section 7(1)(aa). Judicial decisions should therefore be tracked separately from the bare statutory text.

11. GST Council deliberations that shaped Section 7

11.1 28th GST Council Meeting — Schedule II architecture

The 28th GST Council agenda contains a detailed proposal for Section 7. It records the concern that the old clause (d) could make Schedule II activities appear to be supplies even when the underlying Section 7(1) requirements were not met. The proposed solution was to insert Section 7(1A) and remove clause (d). This is the clearest policy history for understanding why the current statute separates existence of supply from classification of supply. citeturn0search1turn4search1

11.2 39th GST Council Meeting — business nexus and consideration

GST Council material from the 39th meeting illustrates the practical use of Section 7 in analysing contract manufacturing/bottling arrangements. The discussion identified consideration and the course or furtherance of business as central parameters under Section 7(1)(a). This demonstrates how the provision operates in real commercial contracts rather than only in textbook sale transactions. citeturn4search2

11.3 2021 legislative response to member transactions

The later insertion of Section 7(1)(aa) represents a separate legislative development aimed at transactions between associations and members. It should be analysed independently from the 2018 Schedule II restructuring.

13. Circulars and clarifications relevant to Section 7 analysis

Circular No. 172/04/2022-GST

This circular is not a general rewrite of Section 7, but it contains an important clarification concerning employee perquisites. The circular addresses perquisites provided by employers to employees under contractual arrangements and is relevant when distinguishing transactions occurring in the employment relationship from independent supplies. The GST Council/CBIC circular record identifies the circular as dated 6 July 2022. citeturn2search1turn2search11

Intermediary and other transaction-specific clarifications

Section 7 often operates together with other provisions rather than in isolation. For example, a cross-border service transaction may require simultaneous analysis of Section 7, the IGST Act's import/export and place-of-supply provisions, and the definition of intermediary. The existence of a supply under Section 7 is therefore only the first stage of the overall GST analysis.

14. Practical examples — how to apply Section 7

Example 1 — Sale of goods for cash

A manufacturer sells goods to a distributor for ₹10 lakh plus GST. There is a supply of goods, consideration, and the transaction is in the course of business. Section 7(1)(a) is satisfied. The practitioner then moves to classification, rate, time, place, valuation and invoicing provisions.

Example 2 — Lease of commercial premises

A company leases a commercial building to another business for monthly rent. The arrangement is not a sale of goods, but Section 7(1)(a) expressly includes rental and lease within the forms of supply. The next steps are to determine the nature of service, place of supply, rate/exemption and compliance requirements.

Example 3 — Free transfer of business asset

A registered business permanently transfers a business asset without charging the recipient. The absence of consideration does not end the analysis. Section 7(1)(c) must be read with Schedule I to determine whether the no-consideration transfer is deemed to be a supply.

Example 4 — Employer and employee

An employee receives monthly salary for services under an employment contract. Schedule III excludes services by an employee to the employer in the course of or in relation to employment. The payment is therefore not treated as a GST supply merely because an economic benefit is received by the employee.

Example 5 — Branch transfer between distinct registrations

A company transfers stock from its Telangana GST registration to its Karnataka GST registration. Even though the same legal entity is involved, distinct-person rules and Schedule I require the transaction to be examined as a potential supply. This is a classic case where “no sale invoice in the ordinary commercial sense” does not automatically mean “no GST supply”.

Example 6 — Import of service for consideration

An Indian business obtains technical consultancy from a foreign supplier for a fee. Section 7(1)(b) specifically covers import of services for consideration whether or not in the course or furtherance of business. The IGST Act and reverse-charge provisions must then be considered.

Example 7 — Schedule II classification

An activity appears in Schedule II. The correct approach is not to stop there. First ask whether the transaction constitutes supply under Section 7(1). If yes, Section 7(1A) and Schedule II determine whether it is treated as a supply of goods or services.

Example 8 — Land transaction

A person sells land. Schedule III expressly keeps sale of land outside the concept of supply. Therefore, the transaction should not be analysed as an ordinary taxable supply merely because consideration is received.

15. Professional Section 7 decision workflow

  1. Identify the transaction. Record the parties, contract, goods/services, consideration and actual flow of value.
  2. Identify the supplier and recipient. Confirm legal identity, GST registration and whether distinct-person or related-person rules are relevant.
  3. Test Section 7(1)(a). Check supply, consideration and business nexus.
  4. Test Section 7(1)(aa). If a non-individual person and its members/constituents are involved, examine the special provision and the current judicial position.
  5. Test Section 7(1)(b). For imported services, apply the specific import-of-services framework.
  6. Test Schedule I. If there is no consideration, determine whether Schedule I creates a deemed supply.
  7. Test Section 7(2). Check Schedule III and any notified public-authority exclusions.
  8. Use Schedule II only after supply is established. Use it to classify the qualifying supply as goods or services.
  9. Check Section 7(3) notifications. Determine whether Government has specified a particular classification on Council recommendation.
  10. Only then move to the charging and compliance provisions. Consider Section 8, Section 9, exemptions, valuation, time/place of supply, invoicing, returns and payment.

16. Documents a finance or tax team should retain

  • Executed agreement / purchase order / work order.
  • Commercial correspondence establishing the purpose of the transaction.
  • Evidence of consideration, including non-monetary consideration where applicable.
  • GST registrations of the parties and details of distinct registrations.
  • Related-party/member/constituent relationship documentation where relevant.
  • Accounting entries and ledger extracts.
  • Invoice, debit note, credit note or other tax document, as applicable.
  • Evidence supporting a Schedule I no-consideration transaction.
  • Evidence supporting a Schedule III exclusion.
  • Relevant notification, exemption or classification order relied upon.
  • Tax position paper where the transaction is unusual or potentially litigated.

17. Common mistakes while applying Section 7

  1. “Every receipt is a supply.” Incorrect. The statutory conditions and exclusions must be tested.
  2. “No consideration means no GST.” Incorrect. Schedule I can create supplies without consideration.
  3. “Schedule II itself creates supply.” The current architecture requires the transaction to first qualify as supply under Section 7(1).
  4. “Same legal entity always means no supply.” Distinct-person rules can produce a supply between GST registrations.
  5. “Employee benefits are always taxable.” The employment relationship and Schedule III must be examined.
  6. “Schedule III is an exemption.” It is conceptually different: specified activities are treated as neither supply of goods nor supply of services.
  7. “Section 7 alone determines tax liability.” Supply is the starting point; levy, rate, exemption, place, time, valuation and payment provisions still have to be applied.
  8. “Old case law can be applied without checking amendments.” Section 7 has undergone material restructuring, and the member/association provisions have generated constitutional litigation.

19. Key takeaways for management, accounts teams and auditors

1. Supply is the gateway.
Do not start with the GST rate. First establish whether the transaction is a supply.
2. Consideration is important but not universal.
Section 7(1)(c) and Schedule I cover specified no-consideration supplies.
3. Schedule II is classification.
It should not be used as a shortcut to create a supply independently of Section 7(1).
4. Schedule III is an exclusion.
Specified transactions are treated as neither supply of goods nor supply of services.
5. Member transactions require special care.
Section 7(1)(aa) contains a specific statutory fiction and has been the subject of constitutional litigation.
6. Read the Act with the IGST Act.
Cross-border and inter-State questions cannot be answered from Section 7 alone.
