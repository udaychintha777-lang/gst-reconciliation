GST ACT — SECTION 56

Section 56 CGST Act — Interest on Delayed GST Refund

Section 56 protects a taxpayer when a refund that has become payable is not actually paid within the statutory period. This detailed guide explains the 60-day rule, normal 6% interest, the special 9% rate for refunds arising from final adjudication or appellate/court orders, the calculation period, Rule 94, RFD-05 and practical examples in simple language.

1. Why Section 56 matters

Searches such as “GST refund interest”, “interest on delayed GST refund”, “GST refund after 60 days”, “6% GST refund interest”, “9% GST refund interest”, “Rule 94 GST” and “RFD-05” are all closely connected to Section 56.

Section 54 tells us when a refund can be granted. Section 56 answers a different question: what happens if a refund that should be paid is not paid within the statutory time?

Simple idea: If an eligible refund is not actually paid within 60 days from receipt of the refund application, Section 56 can require the Government to pay interest for the period of delay.

2. Section 56 in very simple language

Suppose a business files a GST refund claim. The claim is valid and the Government ultimately accepts that ₹10 lakh is refundable.

If the law requires the refund to be paid within 60 days but the ₹10 lakh reaches the taxpayer's bank account after that period, the taxpayer should not lose the time value of that money completely.

Section 56 provides interest on the qualifying delay.

The important point is that sanctioning a refund and actually paying the refund are not the same thing. The GST Council's refund guidance states that the refund is treated as paid when the amount is credited to the applicant's bank account. citeturn0search1

3. Why does the Government pay interest?

GST law requires taxpayers to pay tax within prescribed time limits. Refunds are the reverse side of that system.

When the Government has determined that an amount belongs back to the taxpayer, an indefinite delay would financially disadvantage the taxpayer. Section 56 therefore creates a statutory interest consequence for delayed payment.

This is not a penalty imposed on the taxpayer. It is interest payable by the Government when the statutory conditions are met.

5. The 60-day rule

The central test is simple:

Refund application received → 60 days → refund should be paid

If the qualifying refund is not paid within those 60 days, interest begins from the day immediately after expiry of the 60-day period.

Official GST Council guidance explains that Section 54(7) requires the refund sanction order within 60 days and Section 56 provides interest where the sanctioned refund is not paid within that period. citeturn0search1

Do not confuse: the 60-day clock is not simply “60 days from the date the officer finally approves the refund”. Section 56 uses the statutory date of receipt of the relevant refund application.

6. When does interest start?

For an ordinary qualifying refund, interest starts on the day immediately after the expiry of 60 days from receipt of the refund application.

Example:

  • Refund application received: 1 April
  • 60-day statutory period ends: 30 May
  • Interest starts: 31 May

The exact calendar count should be maintained in the refund working paper.

7. When does interest stop?

Interest continues until the date the refund amount is actually refunded.

For practical purposes, the Government's refund guidance treats the refund as having been paid when the amount is credited to the applicant's bank account. citeturn0search1

Example: If the refund order is passed on 20 July but the money is credited to the bank account on 25 July, the payment date for the Section 56 calculation is linked to the actual refund credit, not simply the order date.

8. Interest rates: 6% and 9%

SituationRate
Ordinary delayed refund covered by Section 566% per annum notified under Notification 13/2017-Central Tax.
Refund arising from final adjudication/appellate/tribunal/court order covered by the proviso9% per annum notified for the qualifying category.

The Act sets maximum ceilings of 6% and 9%; the Government's notifications specify the operative rates. GST Council FAQ material confirms 6% for ordinary delayed refunds and 9% for qualifying refunds arising from final orders. citeturn0search5turn0search2

9. Normal delayed refund

Suppose a company files a refund application for ₹20 lakh. The application is complete and ultimately ₹18 lakh is found refundable.

If ₹18 lakh is not paid within the statutory 60-day period, Section 56 interest can apply to the qualifying delayed refund amount.

The calculation should therefore identify:

  1. date of receipt of application;
  2. 60-day expiry date;
  3. actual refund-credit date;
  4. amount actually refundable; and
  5. applicable interest rate.

10. Refund arising from final order

The proviso to Section 56 covers a different situation.

Imagine a taxpayer initially loses a refund claim. The matter proceeds through adjudication/appeal and an appellate authority, tribunal or court ultimately passes an order that becomes final and results in a refund.

If that refund is not paid within 60 days from the relevant consequent application, the special 9% rate can apply under the proviso, subject to the statutory conditions.

The 9% rate is not a general “late refund rate”. It is for the specific category described in the proviso to Section 56.

11. Which application date matters?

Section 56 refers to the date of receipt of the refund application under Section 54(1).

For a professional file, retain:

  • ARN/application number;
  • electronic filing date;
  • acknowledgement/RFD-02;
  • deficiency memo, if any;
  • refund order;
  • payment advice; and
  • bank-credit evidence.

This creates a clear audit trail for the 60-day calculation.

12. Complete application and acknowledgement

The refund Rules provide a scrutiny process. For many refund claims, the officer examines whether the application is complete and may issue RFD-02 or a deficiency communication.

The applicant should therefore distinguish between:

EventMeaning
Application filedClaim is submitted electronically.
AcknowledgementApplication is treated as complete under the prescribed scrutiny process.
DeficiencyApplication has identified deficiencies and may require a fresh/corrected application under the Rules.
SanctionOfficer determines the refundable amount.
Bank creditRefund money is actually received.

The exact limitation/clock consequences must be assessed according to the applicable refund Rules and facts rather than simply treating every portal event as identical.

13. On what amount is interest calculated?

Interest under Section 56 is linked to the tax ordered to be refunded that was delayed.

For the working paper, do not simply apply 6% or 9% to every amount appearing in the refund file. Identify the amount that is actually refundable and delayed.

Example: Claim = ₹25 lakh; final refundable tax = ₹18 lakh; ₹7 lakh rejected. The interest calculation should be based on the qualifying delayed refundable amount, not automatically on the original ₹25 lakh claim.

14. Interest calculation formula

Interest = Qualifying delayed refund × Applicable annual rate × Number of delayed days ÷ 365

Example at 6%:

Refund delayed = ₹10,00,000
Delay after the statutory 60-day period = 30 days

₹10,00,000 × 6% × 30 ÷ 365 = approximately ₹4,932

The calculation should use the actual number of delayed days and the applicable notified rate for the relevant period.

15. Detailed numerical examples

Example 1 — 30-day delay

Refund = ₹10 lakh. Delay after the 60-day period = 30 days. At 6%, interest is approximately ₹4,932.

Example 2 — 90-day delay

Refund = ₹25 lakh. Delay = 90 days. At 6%, interest is approximately ₹36,986.

Example 3 — 9% final-order refund

Final-order refund = ₹50 lakh. Delay after the 60-day period = 45 days. At 9%, interest is approximately ₹55,479.

Example 4 — Refund sanctioned on day 60, paid on day 70

The key issue is that sanction and payment are separate. If the statutory payment deadline is crossed, the qualifying delay must be calculated until actual payment.

Example 5 — Partial refund

Claim = ₹30 lakh; refund finally allowed = ₹20 lakh. The interest calculation should ordinarily focus on the ₹20 lakh qualifying delayed refund, not the rejected ₹10 lakh.

Example 6 — Bank credit date

Order date = 10 August; bank credit = 14 August. The refund-payment audit trail should use the actual bank credit evidence.

Example 7 — Appeal order

An appellate order becomes final and results in ₹12 lakh refund. If the consequent application is not paid within 60 days, the special proviso should be examined for the 9% rate.

Example 8 — No delay

Refund is credited within 60 days. Section 56 interest for delayed refund does not arise merely because the taxpayer expected earlier payment.

Example 9 — Different tax components

A refund order contains CGST and SGST/IGST components. Reconcile each component with the sanction order and actual bank credit before computing any interest shortfall.

Example 10 — Applicant error

If payment is delayed because the applicant supplied incorrect bank details, the treatment of that period must be considered under the applicable Rule 94 framework and current law; do not automatically claim interest for every calendar day.

16. RFD-05 and Rule 94

Rule 94 provides the procedural mechanism for interest on delayed refunds.

Where interest is due under Section 56, the proper officer makes an order and payment advice in FORM GST RFD-05, specifying:

  • the refund amount that was delayed;
  • the period of delay;
  • the interest payable; and
  • the payment details.

The interest is electronically credited to the bank account specified for the refund. Official CBIC refund rules expressly provide this mechanism. citeturn0search0

17. Refund vs interest payment

There are two separate monetary elements:

ElementMeaning
RefundThe principal amount of tax/eligible amount returned to the applicant.
InterestAdditional amount payable under Section 56 because the qualifying refund was delayed beyond the statutory period.

Therefore, a finance team should reconcile both amounts separately in its refund tracker.

18. Section 54 connection

Section 54 and Section 56 work together.

Section 54 → determines/controls refund entitlement and refund process
Section 56 → compensates qualifying delay in payment of refund

Section 56 does not create a new refund entitlement. If an amount is not legally refundable under Section 54 or another applicable provision, Section 56 cannot be used to create that entitlement.

19. Applicant-caused delay

This is an important practical area.

GST Council materials have specifically discussed situations where delay is attributable to the applicant—for example, late replies to RFD-08 notices, requests for additional time/documents or incorrect bank details. The Council considered excluding such applicant-attributable periods from the interest computation through Rule 94 amendments. citeturn0search1

Practical approach: Keep a separate “applicant-caused delay” column in the refund tracker and document the exact dates of notices, replies, additional-document requests and bank-detail corrections.

20. Bank-account problems

A refund can be sanctioned but fail to reach the bank because the account details supplied by the applicant are incorrect or cannot be validated.

This should not be treated casually. GST Council material has specifically identified PFMS bank-validation failures and incorrect bank details as applicant-attributable delay issues. citeturn0search1

Therefore, the refund file should preserve the bank-account details submitted, validation status, error message and date on which corrected details were furnished.

21. Provisional refund and Section 56

Provisional refund under Section 54(6) is different from final refund sanction.

If a provisional refund is paid, the final refund calculation must still be completed. The refund file should therefore track:

  • provisional amount;
  • date of provisional payment;
  • final sanctioned amount;
  • adjustment, if any; and
  • interest, if applicable to the remaining delayed refundable amount.

22. Appeal/court refund

The 9% proviso becomes important when the refund arises from an order passed by an adjudicating authority, Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court and that order has attained finality.

The taxpayer should preserve:

  • original adjudication order;
  • appeal order/tribunal judgment/court judgment;
  • finality evidence;
  • refund application filed consequent to the order;
  • RFD-06/refund order; and
  • bank-credit evidence.

The 14th GST Council material explains the 9% category and its relationship with final adjudication/appellate/court orders. citeturn0search2

23. Pre-deposit refund comparison

Refund of an appellate pre-deposit is related to the broader GST refund system but should not be casually treated as identical to every Section 56 claim.

The GST Council FAQ separately states that the 6% rate applies to refund of pre-deposit and discusses the timing of interest in that context. citeturn0search5

Maintain the pre-deposit payment date, appeal order, refund application and actual bank-credit date as separate milestones.

25. Construction and infrastructure examples

Example — Large project refund

A construction company files a substantial inverted-duty refund claim. The claim is acknowledged, ultimately sanctioned and the money is credited after the statutory 60-day period. The company should independently calculate the potential Section 56 interest and reconcile it with the Government's payment.

Example — Export project services

An engineering/infrastructure company exports qualifying services and claims refund. If the refund is delayed beyond the statutory period, the finance team should track Section 56 interest separately.

Example — Litigation refund

A contractor succeeds in an appellate proceeding resulting in refund of tax. If the consequent refund application is not paid within 60 days, the special 9% proviso should be examined.

28. GST Council developments

The 14th GST Council meeting discussed the original Section 56 framework and distinguished ordinary delayed refunds from refunds arising from final adjudication/appellate/court orders. citeturn0search2

The 48th GST Council agenda later examined whether applicant-attributable delays should be excluded from the computation of refund interest. It specifically considered delayed replies, additional-document requests and incorrect bank details. citeturn0search1

This history is important because it shows why a refund-interest calculation should preserve the complete procedural timeline rather than simply count calendar days from the first filing date.

30. Common mistakes

  • Calculating interest from the refund order date instead of analysing the statutory 60-day period.
  • Stopping interest at the sanction date instead of checking actual bank credit.
  • Applying 9% to every delayed refund.
  • Applying 6% to a qualifying final-order refund without checking the proviso.
  • Calculating interest on the original claim instead of the qualifying refundable amount.
  • Ignoring applicant-caused delay issues.
  • Ignoring incorrect bank-account details.
  • Not retaining ARN/RFD-02/RFD-06/RFD-05 and bank evidence.
  • Confusing refund principal with refund interest.
  • Failing to independently verify the Government's interest calculation.

32. Section 56 GST FAQs

What is Section 56 of the CGST Act?

Section 56 provides interest on qualifying GST refunds that are not paid within the statutory 60-day period.

After how many days is GST refund interest payable?

For the normal Section 56 situation, interest applies when the qualifying refund is not refunded within 60 days from receipt of the refund application.

What is the GST refund interest rate?

The notified normal rate is 6% per annum. A special 9% rate applies to qualifying refunds arising from final adjudication, appellate, tribunal or court orders under the proviso to Section 56.

When does GST refund interest start?

Normally from the day immediately after expiry of 60 days from receipt of the refund application.

When does GST refund interest stop?

It runs until the refund is actually paid, with the practical payment date established by the refund credit/payment record.

Is interest calculated from the date of refund application?

No. The first 60 days are the statutory payment period. Interest generally starts only from the day immediately after those 60 days expire.

Is 9% interest applicable to every delayed refund?

No. The 9% rate belongs to the special category in the proviso to Section 56 involving refunds arising from final adjudicating/appellate/tribunal/court orders.

What is Rule 94 GST?

Rule 94 provides the procedural mechanism for sanction/payment of interest on delayed refunds, including RFD-05.

What is RFD-05?

It is the payment advice used for refund payment and, where applicable, delayed-refund interest.

Does refund interest stop when RFD-06 is issued?

The payment date must be examined. Government guidance states that tax is considered refunded when the amount is credited to the applicant's bank account.

What if the refund is delayed because I gave wrong bank details?

The applicant-attributable delay must be examined under the applicable Rule 94/current framework; it should not automatically be counted as Government delay.

Is refund interest automatic?

The Rules provide for an order/payment advice where interest is due, but the taxpayer should independently track the entitlement and reconcile the amount received.

Can I claim interest if only part of my refund was sanctioned?

The calculation should relate to the qualifying refundable amount that was actually delayed, not the rejected portion of the original claim.

Does Section 56 apply to court-ordered refunds?

Yes, the proviso specifically addresses qualifying refunds arising from final orders of adjudicating/appellate authorities, tribunals or courts.

34. Key takeaways

  • Section 56 deals with interest on delayed GST refunds.
  • The core statutory period is 60 days from receipt of the refund application.
  • Normal delayed-refund interest is notified at 6% per annum.
  • The special proviso permits a notified 9% rate for qualifying refunds arising from final adjudication/appellate/tribunal/court orders.
  • Interest normally starts immediately after the 60-day period ends.
  • The practical payment date is the date the refund is credited to the applicant's bank account.
  • Interest should be calculated on the qualifying delayed refundable amount.
  • RFD-05 and Rule 94 provide the procedural mechanism for delayed-refund interest.
  • Applicant-attributable delays and bank-detail problems require separate analysis.
  • Section 56 does not create a refund entitlement; Section 54 or another applicable provision must first establish the refund.
