1. The short answer — after the Supreme Court decision, this issue must be taken seriously

If a supplier has charged GST but the corresponding tax has not actually been paid to the Government, the buyer cannot assume that a genuine invoice, payment to the supplier or appearance in GSTR-2B by itself guarantees ITC.

2026 legal position: In Bhandari Scrap Traders v. Union of India, decided on 24 July 2026, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of Section 16(2)(c) and declined to read it down. The Court agreed with the Gujarat High Court that a purchasing dealer under the GST regime cannot simply be treated like a bona-fide purchaser under the earlier Delhi VAT framework when the supplier fails to pay the requisite tax.

However, this does not mean that every ITC dispute can be decided merely by saying “supplier did not pay”. The recipient still needs to establish the transaction, the other Section 16 conditions, the period involved, the supplier's default and the statutory mechanism applicable to the case.

Practical conclusion: the buyer's strongest protection is no longer a broad “I am a bona-fide purchaser” argument. The better approach is a documented vendor-compliance, transaction-evidence and statutory-condition file, followed by the correct action if supplier default is established.

2. What exactly does Section 16(2)(c) require?

Section 16(2)(c) contains a condition for ITC: subject to Section 41, the tax charged in respect of the supply must have been actually paid to the Government, either in cash or through utilisation of admissible ITC in respect of that supply.

3. Why the 2026 Supreme Court decision matters

Bhandari Scrap Traders v. Union of India — Supreme Court, 24 July 2026

The Supreme Court dismissed the SLP batch challenging the Gujarat High Court decision in Maruti Enterprise v. Union of India. It held that the distinction between the Delhi VAT framework and the CGST Act means that a purchasing dealer cannot be placed on the same footing as a purported bona-fide purchasing dealer when the GST supplier fails to pay the requisite tax. The Court also noted the statutory route for re-availment after the supplier discharges the tax liability.

Important legal reading: The Supreme Court expressly said there were no grounds to declare Section 16(2)(c) unconstitutional or to read it down. Therefore, older cases that relied on a broad bona-fide-purchaser principle under different VAT statutes must be read carefully and cannot simply be transplanted into the current CGST framework.

Subsequent 2026 decisions have already started applying this position. For example, the Rajasthan High Court in Shree Karni Electrovision v. Union of India treated Section 16(2)(c), subject to Section 41, as a mandatory condition and stated that recovery can follow where ITC was availed while the supplier had not paid the tax.

4. Do not confuse these four different supplier problems

A. Supplier did not pay tax

Primary Section 16(2)(c) issue. Focus on actual tax payment and the statutory consequence.

B. Supplier did not upload invoice

Primarily a Section 16(2)(aa)/return-reconciliation issue. GSTR-2B may not contain the document.

C. Supplier registration cancelled

Separate registration/cancellation issue. Do not automatically equate retrospective cancellation with proof that the underlying transaction was fake.

D. Supplier is alleged to be bogus

This is a transaction-genuineness issue involving identity, movement, receipt, payment, transport and commercial evidence.

Do not use one argument for all four cases. “Invoice is in 2B” is not the complete answer to supplier tax non-payment; equally, “supplier registration was cancelled” is not by itself a factual finding that every historical transaction was fictitious.

5. The 15-minute diagnostic workflow for an ITC dispute

  1. Identify the exact invoice, supplier GSTIN, tax period and ITC amount.
  2. Check the supplier's registration status and relevant cancellation dates.
  3. Check whether the invoice was reported by the supplier and whether it appears in the relevant GSTR-2B.
  4. Verify purchase order, invoice, GRN/service completion evidence and ledger.
  5. For goods, verify e-way bill, LR/transport documents and receipt at the site/warehouse.
  6. Verify payment to the supplier and GST component separately where possible.
  7. Check supplier GSTR-1/1A and GSTR-3B position through legally available records and correspondence.
  8. Identify whether the department's allegation is “tax not paid”, “invoice not reported”, “bogus transaction”, “wrong GSTIN”, “non-receipt” or another defect.
  9. Map the facts to Section 16(2)(a), (aa), (b), (c), Section 41 and other applicable provisions.
  10. Only then decide: claim, hold, reverse, defend or re-avail.

6. What evidence should the buyer maintain?

Because the burden of proving ITC eligibility can fall on the claimant, maintain an invoice-level evidence pack rather than relying only on the accounting ledger.

Commercial

PO/WO, quotation, approval, vendor onboarding, contract and correspondence.

Tax

Tax invoice, GSTIN verification, GSTR-2B, reconciliation and relevant supplier return evidence.

Receipt

GRN, delivery challan, gate entry, stock record, service completion or consumption evidence.

Transport

E-way bill, LR/consignment note, vehicle details, weighbridge or transporter records where applicable.

Payment

Bank statement, payment voucher, supplier ledger and settlement trail.

Internal control

Purchase approval, three-way match, reconciliation sign-off and vendor compliance review.

  • Keep documents linked to the invoice number and supplier GSTIN.
  • For recurring vendors, retain monthly compliance review evidence.
  • Preserve original records before responding to a departmental notice.

7. Case 1 — Supplier filed GSTR-1, invoice is in GSTR-2B, but supplier did not pay GST

Example: Purchase ₹10,00,000 + GST ₹1,80,000. Invoice appears in GSTR-2B. Goods were received and paid through bank. Later it is found that the supplier filed GSTR-1 but did not discharge the corresponding tax through GSTR-3B.

After Bhandari Scrap Traders, the buyer should not rely on the argument that GSTR-2B automatically settles the Section 16(2)(c) condition. The buyer should establish all other conditions and determine the precise supplier default and applicable statutory consequence.

Accounts action: mark the invoice as “supplier tax-payment risk”, obtain vendor clarification/evidence, quantify exposure and follow the applicable reversal/re-availment mechanism rather than silently continuing to treat the amount as risk-free ITC.

8. Case 2 — Supplier has not filed GSTR-1, so invoice is missing from GSTR-2B

This is a different problem. The immediate question is not merely whether the supplier paid tax; the buyer also needs to address the requirement relating to communication of invoice details under Section 16(2)(aa), along with the applicable time limits and other conditions.

FindingSuggested buyer action
Invoice not reported but supplier confirms mistakeFollow up for correct reporting/amendment and keep evidence of correspondence.
Supplier says invoice was reported under wrong GSTINCorrect the underlying reporting error; do not duplicate ITC.
Supplier disputes the invoiceStop automatic credit and investigate the commercial transaction.
Supplier inactive/non-responsiveEscalate vendor risk and review whether credit should be deferred/reversed.

9. Case 3 — Supplier later pays the tax

The Supreme Court's reasoning in Bhandari Scrap Traders expressly refers to the CGST framework under which a purchasing dealer can re-avail reversed ITC after the supplier is made to discharge the tax liability. This makes vendor follow-up important even after a reversal.

Supplier default identified → assess/reverse as legally required → supplier discharges tax → obtain evidence → verify eligibility → re-avail through the applicable mechanism
Do not re-avail merely because the supplier sends a WhatsApp message saying “tax paid”. Obtain reliable evidence and reconcile the relevant tax period and invoice before re-availment.

10. Case 4 — Supplier is insolvent or has stopped business

Supplier insolvency can make recovery and verification difficult, but it does not remove the statutory condition. The buyer should separate two questions: (1) what happened to the supplier's tax liability, and (2) what is the buyer's ITC position under Section 16(2)(c) and Section 41?

In Shree Karni Electrovision, the Rajasthan High Court considered a supplier that had become insolvent and still treated Section 16(2)(c) as a mandatory condition subject to Section 41. The Court rejected the argument that the insolvency framework by itself removed the GST ITC condition.

Practical control: For high-value vendors, do not wait until insolvency to discover compliance risk. Vendor-risk scoring should be part of the monthly GST close.

11. Case 5 — Supplier is accused of being bogus or non-existent

This is substantially more serious than a supplier tax-payment mismatch. The department may examine whether there was any real supply at all.

13. The buyer's practical defence strategy after receiving an ITC notice

  1. Read the allegation precisely. Do not answer “ITC is genuine” when the actual allegation is non-payment of supplier tax.
  2. Create invoice-wise reconciliation. Invoice number, GSTIN, taxable value, tax, GSTR-2B status, receipt and payment.
  3. Separate undisputed facts from disputed facts.
  4. Submit primary evidence first. Avoid an unnecessarily long narrative without documents.
  5. Address Section 16(2)(c) directly. Explain what is known about supplier tax payment and what steps were taken.
  6. Address Section 155. Demonstrate why the buyer's eligibility is supported by evidence.
  7. Where supplier default has been cured, provide reliable evidence and explain the applicable re-availment position.
  8. Do not make unsupported constitutional arguments against Section 16(2)(c) after Bhandari.

16. Monthly GST close checklist for Section 16(2)(c) risk

  • Download/reconcile GSTR-2B with purchase register.
  • Identify vendors with unusual filing gaps or repeated amendments.
  • Track high-value vendors separately.
  • Match goods receipt/service completion with invoices.
  • Maintain supplier-wise compliance tracker.
  • Escalate supplier tax-payment concerns to tax leadership.
  • Maintain reversal/re-availment tracker for supplier-default cases.
  • Document management decisions for material ITC exposure.
  • Retain evidence in an invoice-indexed folder.

17. Common mistakes buyers should avoid

  • “It is in GSTR-2B, so ITC is automatically safe.”
  • “We paid the supplier by bank, so Section 16(2)(c) cannot apply.”
  • “The supplier's GST registration was active, therefore tax must have been paid.”
  • “Supplier cancellation means the transaction was fake.”
  • Using old bona-fide-purchaser VAT cases without analysing Bhandari Scrap Traders.
  • Providing only invoices when the department disputes actual movement/receipt.
  • Re-availing credit based only on supplier correspondence without verifying the statutory position.
  • Responding to a notice with a generic explanation instead of invoice-wise evidence.

18. A simple decision tree for accountants

START → Is the invoice genuine and supply actually received?
NO → Investigate immediately; do not defend only on invoice/payment.
YES → Was invoice detail communicated/reported as required? → NO → address reporting/16(2)(aa) issue.
YES → Is there evidence that supplier tax was actually paid? → YES → continue normal ITC conditions review.
NO / UNCERTAIN → Section 16(2)(c) risk → quantify exposure → obtain supplier evidence → apply the legally applicable reversal/re-availment mechanism → document management decision.
SUPPLIER LATER PAYS → verify reliable evidence → assess re-availment under the applicable statutory mechanism.

19. How this differs from retrospective supplier registration cancellation

A supplier registration cancellation dispute asks a different question: what is the effect of cancellation, particularly retrospective cancellation, on historical transactions? A Section 16(2)(c) dispute asks whether the tax charged on the supply was actually paid to the Government.

The two can overlap in the same notice, but they should be pleaded separately. A strong reply should have separate headings for:

  1. registration status on the transaction date;
  2. cancellation effective date and reason;
  3. genuineness and receipt of the supply;
  4. invoice reporting and GSTR-2B;
  5. supplier tax payment under Section 16(2)(c);
  6. other Section 16 conditions;
  7. reversal/re-availment, if applicable.

For the separate cancellation issue, see your supplier registration cancellation and buyer ITC case-law guide.

20. Final takeaway — change the way you review vendor ITC

The practical lesson after Bhandari Scrap Traders: ITC review cannot stop at “invoice available + 2B available + payment made”. For material vendors, finance teams should also manage supplier tax-compliance risk and maintain evidence that the underlying supply is genuine and received.

The strongest approach is an invoice-level control system that connects the purchase register, GSTR-2B/IMS, supplier compliance, GRN/service evidence, transport records, payment trail and reversal/re-availment history.

Next step: Add a supplier-risk column to your monthly GST reconciliation and identify every invoice where Section 16(2)(c) exposure could become material.

22. Frequently Asked Questions

Does GSTR-2B prove that the supplier paid GST?

No. GSTR-2B is an important statement for reconciliation, but it does not by itself establish satisfaction of every ITC condition, including Section 16(2)(c).

What did Bhandari Scrap Traders decide?

On 24 July 2026, the Supreme Court affirmed the Gujarat High Court position and upheld Section 16(2)(c), declining to declare it unconstitutional or read it down.

Can a bona-fide buyer rely on older VAT cases?

Not automatically. The Supreme Court specifically distinguished the Delhi VAT framework from the CGST scheme in Bhandari Scrap Traders.

Can ITC be re-availed if the supplier later pays tax?

The Supreme Court referred to the statutory framework permitting re-availment after the supplier discharges the tax liability, subject to the applicable provisions and procedure.

Does bank payment prove ITC eligibility?

No. Payment is useful evidence but is not by itself conclusive proof of genuine supply or compliance with every Section 16 condition.

What if the supplier registration is cancelled retrospectively?

Treat cancellation as a separate issue from supplier tax non-payment and examine the transaction, cancellation date, invoice reporting and all applicable ITC conditions.

What if the supplier is alleged to be bogus?

Build a complete transaction evidence file including KYC, PO, invoice, receipt, transport, GRN/service completion, stock/consumption and payment evidence.

What should a finance team track monthly?

At minimum: vendor filing behaviour, GSTR-2B reconciliation, high-value suppliers, receipt evidence, disputed invoices and reversal/re-availment cases.
