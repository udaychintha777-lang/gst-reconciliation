Section 126 of the CGST Act, 2017

General disciplines related to penalty — a detailed public-friendly guide to minor breaches, easily rectifiable documentation mistakes, proportionality, hearing rights, reasoned penalty orders, voluntary disclosure and the important exception for fixed-sum or fixed-percentage penalties.

At a glance

What is Section 126 of the CGST Act?

Section 126 lays down the general disciplines that apply when penalties are imposed under the CGST Act. It is designed to prevent disproportionate penalty for qualifying minor breaches and easily rectifiable documentation mistakes, require consideration of the facts and severity of the breach, provide an opportunity of hearing, require the penalty order to identify the breach and applicable provision, and permit voluntary disclosure to be considered as a mitigating factor.

In simple language: GST law does not treat every small compliance mistake in exactly the same way as a serious deliberate violation. Section 126 provides a framework for how penalties should be approached.

Section 126 contains six sub-sections. The first five create the principal disciplines; sub-section (6) creates an important limitation: the Section 126 safeguards do not apply where the penalty specified under the Act is a fixed sum or expressed as a fixed percentage. citeturn0search0turn0search19

Law

1Current statutory text

Section 126 — General disciplines related to penalty.

(1) No officer under this Act shall impose any penalty for minor breaches of tax regulations or procedural requirements and in particular, any omission or mistake in documentation which is easily rectifiable and made without fraudulent intent or gross negligence.

Explanation. For the purpose of this sub-section—

(a) a breach shall be considered a “minor breach” if the amount of tax involved is less than five thousand rupees;

(b) an omission or mistake in documentation shall be considered to be easily rectifiable if the same is an error apparent on the face of record.

(2) The penalty imposed under this Act shall depend on the facts and circumstances of each case and shall be commensurate with the degree and severity of the breach.

(3) No penalty shall be imposed on any person without giving him an opportunity of being heard.

(4) The officer under this Act shall while imposing penalty in an order for a breach of any law, regulation or procedural requirement, specify the nature of the breach and the applicable law, regulation or procedure under which the amount of penalty for the breach has been specified.

(5) When a person voluntarily discloses to an officer under this Act the circumstances of a breach of the tax law, regulation or procedural requirement prior to the discovery of the breach by the officer under this Act, the proper officer may consider this fact as a mitigating factor when quantifying a penalty for that person.

(6) The provisions of this section shall not apply in such cases where the penalty specified under this Act is either a fixed sum or expressed as a fixed percentage.

Layman explanation

2What does Section 126 mean in ordinary language?

Think of Section 126 as a rulebook for imposing GST penalties. It does not itself prescribe a new penalty amount for a particular offence. Instead, it tells the officer how penalty provisions under the Act should generally be administered.

Small breach

Qualifying minor breaches and certain easily rectifiable documentation mistakes without fraudulent intent or gross negligence should not attract a penalty under the rule stated in sub-section (1).

Proportionate penalty

The penalty should depend on the facts and circumstances and be commensurate with the degree and severity of the breach.

Right to be heard

A person must be given an opportunity of being heard before a penalty is imposed.

Reasoned order

The penalty order must specify the nature of the breach and the applicable law, regulation or procedure.

Voluntary disclosure

If the person discloses the breach before the officer discovers it, the officer may consider that fact as a mitigating factor when quantifying penalty.

Important exception

The Section 126 disciplines do not apply where the penalty specified under the Act is a fixed sum or fixed percentage.

Important distinction: Section 126 is not a blanket promise that no penalty can ever be imposed for a small mistake. The exact statutory wording, the type of penalty and the facts must be checked, including the fixed-sum/fixed-percentage exception in sub-section (6).
Sub-section 126(1)

3What is a “minor breach” under GST?

Section 126(1) says that no officer shall impose a penalty for minor breaches of tax regulations or procedural requirements and, in particular, for an omission or mistake in documentation that is easily rectifiable and made without fraudulent intent or gross negligence.

The Explanation provides a specific statutory benchmark: a breach is considered a “minor breach” if the amount of tax involved is less than ₹5,000. This does not mean that every issue involving less than ₹5,000 is automatically penalty-free in every context; sub-section (6) and the precise penalty provision must also be considered.

PointSection 126 explanation
Minor breach thresholdAmount of tax involved is less than ₹5,000.
Documentation mistakeMust be an error apparent on the face of the record to qualify as “easily rectifiable” under the Explanation.
Fraudulent intentThe protection in 126(1) is specifically framed around mistakes made without fraudulent intent.
Gross negligenceThe protection also refers to the absence of gross negligence.
Fixed penalty exceptionSection 126 does not apply where the penalty under the Act is a fixed sum or fixed percentage.
Very important: The phrase is “less than five thousand rupees”, not “₹5,000 or less.” Therefore, ₹4,999 is within the stated threshold, while ₹5,000 itself is not less than ₹5,000.
Documentation mistakes

4What is an easily rectifiable mistake?

The Explanation to Section 126(1)(b) says an omission or mistake in documentation is considered easily rectifiable if it is an error apparent on the face of the record.

This is important because GST compliance involves thousands of invoices, e-way bills, returns, registers, reconciliations and supporting documents. Not every clerical or documentary error has the same compliance significance.

Examples of errors that may require a Section 126 analysis

  • A typographical error in a document where the correct information is apparent from the underlying record.
  • A minor documentary omission that can be corrected without changing the substantive transaction.
  • An obvious clerical mistake where the underlying books and supporting records consistently show the correct position.
  • A procedural documentation error that can be corrected and does not involve fraudulent intent or gross negligence.
Do not overgeneralise: “Documentation mistake” does not mean every invoice, return or record error is automatically protected. The error must be examined against the statutory wording, its tax impact, intent, negligence and the applicable penalty provision.
Intent and conduct

5Why fraudulent intent and gross negligence matter

Section 126(1) specifically refers to mistakes made without fraudulent intent or gross negligence. This means the surrounding facts matter, not merely the existence of an error.

SituationWhy it matters
Accidental typo, immediately correctedMay support the argument that the issue is an easily rectifiable documentation mistake, depending on the facts.
Repeated unexplained disregard of a known requirementCould raise a different question from a one-time clerical error.
Deliberate manipulation of recordsNot the kind of conduct Section 126(1) is designed to protect.
Obvious mistake but no corrective actionThe officer may examine the full circumstances and whether the issue remained uncorrected.
System-generated error with supporting technical evidenceThe evidence can help establish how the error occurred and whether it involved intent or gross negligence.

Section 126 does not define every possible form of “fraudulent intent” or “gross negligence.” Those concepts must be applied to the facts and the relevant legal context rather than assumed from the mere presence of an error.

Sub-section 126(2)

6Penalty must be proportionate to the breach

Section 126(2) provides that the penalty imposed under the Act shall depend on the facts and circumstances of each case and shall be commensurate with the degree and severity of the breach. citeturn0search19turn0search20

In simple terms, the penalty should be assessed in relation to what actually happened. A minor procedural lapse and a deliberate, serious compliance violation should not automatically be treated as equivalent merely because both can technically be described as “contraventions.”

Factors a business should document

FactorQuestions to ask
Nature of breachWhat exact requirement was not followed?
Tax involvedWas any tax amount involved, and how much?
DurationWas it a one-time error or continuing conduct?
IntentWas the issue accidental, deliberate or otherwise caused?
NegligenceWas there ordinary error or evidence of gross negligence?
RectificationWas the mistake corrected and when?
DisclosureDid the taxpayer disclose the issue before departmental discovery?
Business impactDid the breach affect tax collection, records or compliance?
Practical point: When replying to a penalty notice, do not merely say “please waive the penalty.” Explain the factual circumstances that make the breach minor, rectifiable, accidental or otherwise less severe, if those facts are true and can be supported by evidence.
Sub-section 126(3)

7Opportunity of being heard

Section 126(3) states that no penalty shall be imposed on any person without giving that person an opportunity of being heard. citeturn0search0

This is a fundamental procedural protection. A taxpayer should have an opportunity to understand the allegation and place the relevant facts, documents and legal submissions before the penalty is imposed.

What should a taxpayer do with the opportunity?

  1. Read the exact allegation and statutory provision.
  2. Check whether the notice identifies the specific breach.
  3. Prepare a factual chronology.
  4. Attach supporting documents.
  5. Explain any minor or easily rectifiable error.
  6. Address intent, negligence and corrective action where relevant.
  7. Explain whether a specific penalty already exists or whether the proposed provision is legally applicable.
  8. Attend the hearing where required or requested and preserve the hearing record.
Do not treat a hearing as a formality. If important facts were not explained in the original reply, the hearing can be used to clearly place those facts and supporting documents on record, subject to the applicable procedure.
Sub-section 126(4)

8What should a penalty order specify?

Section 126(4) requires the officer, while imposing a penalty in an order for a breach of a law, regulation or procedural requirement, to specify the nature of the breach and the applicable law, regulation or procedure under which the amount of penalty has been specified. citeturn0search19

Order elementPlain-language meaning
Nature of breachWhat exactly did the taxpayer allegedly do or fail to do?
Applicable lawWhich section/rule/procedure was violated?
Penalty basisWhy does the cited provision permit the penalty?
AmountWhat penalty has actually been imposed?
Facts and circumstancesThe order should be read with the Section 126 proportionality requirement.

This is especially useful for businesses because a vague statement such as “GST violation — penalty ₹25,000” does not itself explain the legal and factual basis. The taxpayer should examine the complete order and applicable procedural framework.

Sub-section 126(5)

9Voluntary disclosure before departmental discovery

Section 126(5) says that when a person voluntarily discloses the circumstances of a breach to an officer before the officer discovers the breach, the proper officer may consider that disclosure as a mitigating factor when quantifying the penalty. citeturn0search19

There are three important ideas here:

Voluntary

The taxpayer comes forward rather than waiting for the officer to discover the breach.

Before discovery

The statutory wording specifically refers to disclosure before discovery by the officer.

May consider

It is a mitigating factor that the officer may consider; the provision does not say that disclosure automatically cancels the penalty.

Example

A company discovers that it has not followed a GST procedural requirement. Before any departmental inspection, notice or discovery of that specific breach, the company approaches the appropriate officer, explains what happened, supplies the relevant records and takes corrective action. The fact of voluntary disclosure may be considered as a mitigating factor when penalty is quantified.

Evidence matters: If relying on voluntary disclosure, preserve the date, communication, acknowledgement, supporting documents and evidence showing that the disclosure preceded departmental discovery.
Sub-section 126(6)

10Important exception: fixed-sum and fixed-percentage penalties

Section 126(6) states that the provisions of Section 126 do not apply where the penalty specified under the Act is either a fixed sum or expressed as a fixed percentage. citeturn0search0turn0search19

This is one of the most important points to understand because it prevents a taxpayer from assuming that every penalty can be reduced or avoided simply by invoking “minor breach” or “proportionality” under Section 126.

Penalty wording/typeSection 126 issue
Penalty determined under a provision allowing an amount up to a ceilingSection 126 may be relevant, subject to the provision and facts.
Penalty specified as a fixed sumSection 126 expressly does not apply.
Penalty expressed as a fixed percentageSection 126 expressly does not apply.
Specific penalty with its own statutory formulaRead the exact wording and check whether it is fixed or percentage-based for purposes of 126(6).
Do not assume every “specific penalty” is fixed. The exact statutory wording must be checked. Section 126(6) refers specifically to a penalty that is a fixed sum or expressed as a fixed percentage.
Practical application

11Detailed real-world examples

Example 1 — ₹3,000 tax involved in a minor procedural breach

Suppose a qualifying procedural breach involves tax of ₹3,000. Because the amount is less than ₹5,000, the statutory Explanation describes it as a “minor breach.” If the provision is otherwise within Section 126(1) and the conduct is not excluded by Section 126(6), the officer should not impose a penalty for that minor breach.

Example 2 — exactly ₹5,000 tax involved

The Explanation says “less than five thousand rupees.” Therefore, ₹5,000 is not within that particular numerical definition of “minor breach.” The taxpayer should not describe ₹5,000 as automatically qualifying under the less-than-₹5,000 definition.

Example 3 — ₹4,500 tax involved but deliberate fraud

The fact that the amount is below ₹5,000 does not make every form of conduct automatically harmless. Section 126(1) specifically concerns minor breaches and easily rectifiable mistakes without fraudulent intent or gross negligence, and Section 126(6) must also be checked.

Example 4 — obvious invoice typing error

An invoice contains an obvious typographical error while the underlying contract, accounting entry, payment and other records consistently show the correct information. If the error is apparent on the face of the record and the statutory conditions are met, the Section 126 documentation principle may become relevant.

Example 5 — deliberate document manipulation

A taxpayer knowingly changes supporting documents to create a false record. This is materially different from an accidental typo. Section 126(1)'s protection for an omission or mistake made without fraudulent intent would not describe deliberate manipulation.

Example 6 — tax team discovers a procedural breach first

The internal GST team discovers that a procedural requirement was not followed. Before the officer discovers it, the company voluntarily discloses the circumstances and begins corrective action. Under Section 126(5), voluntary disclosure may be considered a mitigating factor in quantifying penalty.

Example 7 — officer discovers the breach first

If the officer discovers the breach before the taxpayer voluntarily discloses it, the specific voluntary-disclosure mitigation in Section 126(5) is not triggered by those facts because the provision requires disclosure prior to discovery. Other facts may still be relevant under the applicable law.

Example 8 — penalty amount is disproportionate

A notice concerns a minor procedural issue, but the proposed penalty is at the upper end of the available range. The taxpayer should explain the actual tax impact, nature of the breach, corrective action, intent, compliance history where relevant and other facts supporting proportionality.

Example 9 — no hearing

A taxpayer receives an order imposing a penalty without having been given the opportunity of being heard required by the applicable statutory process. The taxpayer should examine the order and procedural record and obtain appropriate advice on the available remedy.

Example 10 — vague penalty order

An order simply says “procedural violation — penalty imposed” without clearly specifying the nature of the breach and applicable law/procedure. Section 126(4) should be considered when examining whether the order satisfies the statutory requirement.

Example 11 — fixed percentage penalty

A specific provision prescribes a penalty as a fixed percentage. Section 126(6) expressly says the Section 126 provisions do not apply to a penalty specified as a fixed percentage. The taxpayer therefore cannot simply rely on the general Section 126 disciplines as if the penalty were discretionary.

Example 12 — fixed amount penalty

A specific provision prescribes a penalty as a fixed sum. Section 126(6) similarly excludes such a penalty from the operation of Section 126.

Example 13 — Section 125 general penalty

A person is alleged to have contravened a GST Rule for which no separate penalty is provided and Section 125 is invoked. Because Section 125 permits a penalty up to ₹25,000 rather than stating a fixed amount, Section 126's general disciplines are highly relevant to the assessment, subject to the precise facts and statutory framework.

Example 14 — multiple errors discovered in reconciliation

A company reconciles its GST records and discovers several documentation mismatches. It corrects the records, identifies root causes and voluntarily communicates a relevant breach before departmental discovery. The company should maintain a complete reconciliation trail so the nature and timing of the corrections can be demonstrated.

Example 15 — system migration error

During ERP migration, a mapping issue creates a documentation discrepancy. The company retains migration logs, identifies the error, corrects it and records the control improvement. Whether Section 126 applies depends on the specific statutory breach and penalty provision, but the evidence can be relevant to whether the mistake was accidental and easily rectifiable.

Penalty notice

14How to respond to a GST penalty notice using Section 126

  1. Read the allegation carefully. Identify the exact breach rather than relying on the description “GST violation.”
  2. Check the statutory provision. Confirm which section/rule creates the underlying obligation.
  3. Identify the penalty provision. Determine whether the penalty is discretionary, fixed sum or fixed percentage.
  4. Calculate the tax involved. Check whether the amount is less than ₹5,000 for the specific minor-breach definition.
  5. Analyse the documentation error. If applicable, explain why the error is apparent on the face of the record and easily rectifiable.
  6. Address intent and negligence. Explain the facts demonstrating absence of fraudulent intent or gross negligence where factually correct.
  7. Address proportionality. Explain why the nature and severity of the breach do not justify the proposed amount, where supported by facts.
  8. Use voluntary disclosure evidence. If the breach was disclosed before departmental discovery, provide the disclosure chronology.
  9. Request the hearing. Use the statutory opportunity to present facts and evidence.
  10. Preserve the final record. Keep the notice, reply, annexures, hearing record and order.
Suggested reply structure: Background → exact breach → applicable law → penalty provision → Section 126(1) analysis → tax amount → rectifiability → intent/negligence → proportionality → voluntary disclosure → evidence → prayer.

Common misunderstandings

16Common mistakes about Section 126

“Any tax amount below ₹5,000 means no penalty under GST.”

Too broad. The ₹5,000 figure is part of the statutory definition of “minor breach” for Section 126(1). Section 126(6) and the exact penalty provision must also be checked.

“₹5,000 is included because the Act says less than ₹5,000.”

No. ₹5,000 is not less than ₹5,000. The wording is specifically “less than five thousand rupees.”

“Every documentation mistake is protected.”

No. The documentation principle concerns an omission or mistake that is easily rectifiable, defined as an error apparent on the face of the record, and the opening language also refers to absence of fraudulent intent or gross negligence.

“Section 126 cancels every GST penalty.”

No. Section 126 contains general disciplines, and sub-section (6) expressly excludes fixed-sum and fixed-percentage penalties.

“Voluntary disclosure automatically removes the penalty.”

No. Section 126(5) says the proper officer may consider voluntary disclosure as a mitigating factor when quantifying penalty.

“A hearing is optional.”

No. Section 126(3) states that no penalty shall be imposed without giving the person an opportunity of being heard.

“The officer only needs to mention the amount.”

No. Section 126(4) requires the order to specify the nature of the breach and the applicable law, regulation or procedure under which the amount has been specified.

“Proportionality means the penalty must always be reduced.”

No. Section 126(2) requires the penalty to depend on the facts and circumstances and be commensurate with the degree and severity of the breach. It does not prescribe a universal percentage reduction.

FAQ

19Frequently asked questions on Section 126

1. What is Section 126 of the CGST Act?

It lays down general disciplines related to penalties, including safeguards for qualifying minor breaches and easily rectifiable documentation mistakes, proportionality, hearing, reasoned penalty orders and voluntary disclosure as a possible mitigating factor.

2. What is a minor breach under Section 126?

The Explanation says a breach is considered a minor breach if the amount of tax involved is less than ₹5,000.

3. Is ₹5,000 itself a minor breach?

The statutory wording says “less than five thousand rupees.” Therefore ₹5,000 itself is not within that numerical definition.

4. What is an easily rectifiable documentation mistake?

The Explanation says an omission or mistake is easily rectifiable if it is an error apparent on the face of the record.

5. Does every small documentation error become penalty-free?

No. The full statutory conditions must be examined, including the nature of the breach, fraudulent intent/gross negligence and the fixed-sum/fixed-percentage exception in Section 126(6).

6. Can GST penalty be imposed without a hearing?

Section 126(3) states that no penalty shall be imposed without giving the person an opportunity of being heard.

7. What does proportionality mean in GST penalty?

Section 126(2) requires the penalty to depend on the facts and circumstances and to be commensurate with the degree and severity of the breach.

8. What should a GST penalty order specify?

Section 126(4) requires the officer to specify the nature of the breach and the applicable law, regulation or procedure under which the amount has been specified.

9. What is voluntary disclosure under Section 126(5)?

Where a person voluntarily discloses the circumstances of a breach before the officer discovers it, the proper officer may consider that disclosure as a mitigating factor when quantifying penalty.

10. Does voluntary disclosure automatically waive penalty?

No. The provision says it “may” be considered as a mitigating factor. It is not an automatic waiver.

11. Does Section 126 apply to fixed penalties?

No. Section 126(6) states that the provisions of Section 126 do not apply where the penalty specified under the Act is a fixed sum or expressed as a fixed percentage.

12. What is the relationship between Sections 125 and 126?

Section 125 provides a general penalty up to ₹25,000 where no separate penalty is provided. Section 126 lays down general disciplines for penalty administration.

13. Does Section 126 itself prescribe a penalty amount?

No. It is primarily a discipline/safeguard provision rather than a provision prescribing a general penalty amount.

14. Can a serious breach be called a minor breach just because tax involved is below ₹5,000?

The statutory ₹5,000 definition is important, but the opening language of Section 126(1), the exact penalty provision and Section 126(6) must also be considered. The facts cannot be reduced to the number alone.

15. What evidence helps establish an easily rectifiable mistake?

The original record, corrected record, underlying transaction documents, ERP/system logs and reconciliation working can help demonstrate what the mistake was and whether it was apparent and easily corrected.

16. What if the officer discovered the breach before voluntary disclosure?

The specific mitigation in Section 126(5) requires disclosure before discovery by the officer. If discovery occurred first, that particular statutory wording is not satisfied.

17. Does Section 126 apply to every penalty in the CGST Act?

No. Section 126(6) expressly excludes penalties specified as a fixed sum or fixed percentage.

18. Can Section 126 be used to challenge a penalty?

Section 126 can be relevant to the analysis of a penalty where its provisions apply. The actual remedy depends on the notice/order, penalty provision, procedural stage and applicable appeal framework.

19. What is the best way to reply to a Section 126 issue?

Identify the exact breach, tax amount, penalty provision, minor-breach/rectifiability facts, intent and negligence, proportionality, voluntary disclosure and supporting evidence. The response should be factual and tied to the statutory wording.

How to use this page

This page is prepared for public education, GST compliance awareness and practical workflow support. It is not a substitute for the CGST Act, CGST Rules, notifications, circulars, departmental instructions or judicial decisions applicable to a specific person, transaction or tax period.

For an actual penalty proceeding, verify the law applicable to the relevant date, the exact penalty provision, whether the penalty is fixed or discretionary, the facts surrounding the breach and the current procedural requirements. Professional advice may be appropriate for disputed or high-impact matters.
