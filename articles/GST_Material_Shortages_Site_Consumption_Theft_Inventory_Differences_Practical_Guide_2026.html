GST on Material Shortages, Site Consumption, Theft, Damage & Inventory Differences – Complete Practical Guide

A material shortage in the stores register does not automatically mean that GST is payable. Equally, calling every difference “normal wastage” does not protect the ITC. The correct answer depends on what physically happened to the goods, why the quantity differs, how the material was used and whether the records prove the explanation. This guide starts with everyday examples and builds up to the legal and accounting treatment.

1. The everyday problem: why does the store balance not match?

Imagine that your company purchases 10,000 bags of cement for a road project. The purchase register shows 10,000 bags received. The store ledger shows 9,650 bags issued to work fronts. At month-end, the physical count shows only 180 bags remaining.

On paper, 170 bags are missing: 10,000 received less 9,650 issued less 180 physically available. What happened to those 170 bags?

There are several possible explanations. They may have been consumed in concrete or other work but the issue slips were not posted. Some bags may have torn during handling. Some cement may have hardened because of water ingress. Bags may have been stolen. The site may have transferred them to another location without a transfer note. Or the opening balance or purchase receipt itself may be wrong.

These explanations do not have the same GST result. The first task is therefore not to pass a journal entry. The first task is to establish the facts.

Quantity problemBooks show a different quantity from the physical count.
Cause problemThe business must identify where the goods went.
GST problemOnly after identifying the cause can ITC and any supply implications be assessed.
Golden ruleDo not treat every stock difference as theft, and do not treat every stock difference as normal wastage. Investigate first, classify second, account third, and decide GST last.

2. What exactly is an inventory difference?

Inventory difference is simply the gap between the quantity or value recorded in the books and the quantity or value that should exist based on physical verification and properly recorded movements.

Type of differenceSimple meaningExample
Unrecorded consumptionMaterial was actually used, but the issue slip or consumption entry is missing.Steel issued to a work front but not entered in the store system.
Measurement varianceDifference arises because the measurement method or conversion factor is inaccurate.Loose sand measured by volume in one record and weight in another.
Normal process lossA small, expected quantity is consumed, evaporates or becomes unusable as an inherent part of the activity.Cutting loss while cutting reinforcement bars to required lengths.
Abnormal lossLoss is beyond the expected level or caused by an unusual event.Large quantity of cement damaged because a store was left open during heavy rain.
Theft or pilferageGoods are taken away without the owner's authority.Diesel siphoned from a storage tank.
Damage or destructionGoods physically exist no longer in usable condition, or are destroyed.Steel damaged in a major fire.
Transfer not recordedGoods moved to another site or store but the transfer was not entered.Material moved from Site A to Site B using a vehicle without an inter-site transfer document.
Book errorReceipt, issue, unit, rate or opening balance was entered incorrectly.One truckload entered twice in the ERP.

Notice that some differences are only documentation or measurement issues. They do not mean that goods were lost. A shortage should not be labelled as a GST loss until the company has ruled out these explanations.

4. Genuine site consumption: material used to perform the contract

Suppose a contractor purchases 500 tonnes of steel for a bridge project. The store issues steel to the reinforcement team. The steel is cut, bent, tied and embedded in the structure. The balance quantity in the store reduces because the material has been used to execute the taxable works contract.

This is not the same thing as goods being stolen or destroyed. The material has been consumed in the course of carrying out the business activity. The fact that it is no longer lying in the store does not mean it has been lost.

Example: steel issued and incorporated into the work

Facts
Steel received: 100,000 kg. Steel issued to site teams: 82,000 kg. Steel physically remaining: 18,000 kg. Measurement books and bar-bending schedules support the 82,000 kg issue and use in the project.

Conclusion
There is no shortage merely because 82,000 kg is no longer in the store. It has been issued and consumed in project execution. Maintain a link between material issue, work-front records and the relevant RA bill/measurement records.

Example: diesel consumed by project machinery

Diesel is issued to excavators, tippers, pavers, generators and other equipment. Consumption can be supported by machine-wise logbooks, running hours, kilometres, fuel issue slips and project activity reports. If the diesel is genuinely consumed in business operations, it should not be treated as stolen merely because it is not physically available at month-end.

Practical controlFor high-value materials, capture the issue at the point of use. A store issue entry without a receiving signature or work-front reference is weaker evidence than an issue slip linked to a machine, work package, location and date.

What if the contractor has a material-at-site clause?

Material lying at site may be owned by the contractor, may be covered by a client certification, or may be subject to contractual vesting conditions. These ownership and billing questions should be checked separately. A material being present at site does not automatically mean it has been consumed; equally, material incorporated into work should not remain indefinitely in the store ledger.

5. Normal wastage and process loss: what is the difference?

In everyday language, people use “wastage” to describe almost every quantity that does not appear in the final output. In accounting and GST analysis, that is too broad. A business should separate at least three things:

ConsumptionMaterial is used to perform the work or manufacture the output.
Recoverable scrapA residue remains and can be sold, reused or measured.
Inherent process lossA quantity is inevitably consumed, evaporated or lost as part of the normal process.

The Madras High Court in ARS Steels & Alloy International Pvt. Ltd. v. State Tax Officer (24 June 2021) considered loss inherent in a manufacturing process. It held, on the facts before it, that consumption loss inherent to the manufacturing process could not simply be equated with the cases of goods lost, stolen, destroyed or written off under Section 17(5)(h). The Court discussed process loss such as evaporation and by-products and rejected proportionate ITC reversal merely because the finished product contained a lower quantity than the total inputs consumed.

Read the decision carefully: ARS Steels dealt with inherent manufacturing-process loss. It should not be used as a blanket defence for unexplained shortages, theft, accidental destruction, diversion or poor stock records. Its reasoning is strongest where the loss is technically inherent, expected and evidenced.

Construction examples

There is no universal GST percentage that can be applied to every material as “permitted wastage”. Do not create a standard 2%, 3% or 5% GST allowance unless it is supported by the actual process, technical evidence, contract and relevant industry data. A quantity that is normal for one activity may be abnormal for another.

6. Abnormal wastage and unexplained shortage

Abnormal loss means the actual loss is materially higher than the reasonable level expected from the activity, or it arises because of an unusual event, negligence, poor storage, mishandling or an unexplained disappearance. It needs a separate investigation.

Example: cement damaged by water

Facts
A site received 2,000 bags of cement. Due to a leaking temporary shed, 120 bags became hard and unusable. The site team records the bags as “wastage”.

GST analysis
First establish whether the cement was genuinely destroyed/unusable, whether any part can be recovered, and whether it has been written off. If the goods have been destroyed or written off, Section 17(5)(h) is directly relevant to the ITC attributable to those goods. A label such as “site wastage” does not change the underlying facts.

Example: unexplained steel shortage

Books show 50 tonnes of steel at a site, but physical verification identifies only 43 tonnes. The seven-tonne difference is not supported by issue slips, transfer notes, cutting records or scrap records.

Management should not immediately book the seven tonnes as normal wastage. Recheck weighment, opening balance, receipts, transfers, returns, work-front issues and scrap. If the quantity still cannot be explained, investigate possible pilferage, diversion, unrecorded sale or destruction. Document the conclusion and assess ITC and other GST implications on the established facts.

Red flag: Repeated month-end stock adjustments posted as “consumption” without supporting issue notes. This can weaken the taxpayer's explanation and may invite questions under the accounts-and-records provisions.

7. Theft, pilferage and missing stock

Theft is expressly covered by Section 17(5)(h). If goods on which ITC was availed are stolen, ITC in respect of those goods is not available. The fact that the theft occurred during business hours, at a project site, or despite reasonable security arrangements does not remove the express wording of the clause.

Example: diesel theft from a site tank

Facts
Diesel purchased: 10,000 litres. Verified machine consumption: 7,200 litres. Closing tank stock: 2,100 litres. After calibration and measurement checks, 700 litres remain unexplained and the investigation establishes siphoning by an unknown person.

GST treatment
The established stolen quantity is within Section 17(5)(h). Identify the purchase invoices and calculate the ITC attributable to the stolen diesel. Do not reverse ITC on the full 10,000 litres if 7,200 litres were consumed in business and 2,100 litres remain in stock.

What should the company do?

  1. Secure the site and preserve available CCTV, access logs and tank/stock records.
  2. Record the incident date, location, material, quantity and estimated value.
  3. Make a police complaint/FIR where appropriate to the nature and facts of the incident.
  4. Obtain a joint verification report from stores, project, security and finance teams.
  5. Trace the stolen quantity to purchase invoices and ITC claimed.
  6. Obtain approval for the stock adjustment and ITC reversal.
  7. Keep insurance claim and recovery records separate from the GST ITC analysis.

A police complaint is valuable evidence, but it is not a substitute for quantity reconciliation. Similarly, the absence of an FIR does not automatically prove that theft did not occur. The complete facts and supporting documents matter.

8. Fire, flood, accident, breakage and other damage

Section 17(5)(h) also covers goods destroyed. Destruction can arise from fire, flood, cyclone, collapse, accident, contamination or another event. If the goods are destroyed, ITC attributable to those goods is blocked under the clause.

IncidentGST questionEvidence to retain
Fire in warehouseWhich goods were actually destroyed and what ITC was availed?Fire report, surveyor report, stock list, photographs, purchase invoices.
Flood at project siteWere goods destroyed, merely wet, or still usable after drying/reconditioning?Site report, technical inspection, disposal/recovery records.
Vehicle accident in transitDid goods reach the recipient? Were they destroyed or recovered?Transporter's report, police/incident record, insurance survey, e-way bill.
Breakage during handlingWas the material destroyed or is there recoverable scrap?Breakage register, weighment, scrap sale/return evidence.
Chemical contaminationCan the goods be reprocessed, or have they become unusable and written off?Quality report, laboratory report, approval and disposal records.

Do not assume that every damaged item is automatically “destroyed”. If damaged goods are repaired, reconditioned or sold as scrap, record what actually happened. The GST treatment should follow the factual outcome.

Insurance point: An insurance claim or receipt of compensation does not, by itself, restore ITC that is blocked under Section 17(5)(h). Assess the credit on the goods separately from the tax treatment of the insurance settlement.

9. Obsolete, expired and written-off stock

Sometimes goods are still physically present but are no longer useful. Examples include expired chemicals, obsolete spare parts, damaged electrical items, cement that has hardened, or material that cannot meet project specifications.

Section 17(5)(h) expressly refers to goods “written off”. Therefore, a business should distinguish a mere provision for diminution in value from an actual write-off of the goods in its inventory records.

Accounting situationGST review
Provision created for slow-moving stock, but goods remain recognised and available for use/saleDo not assume that a provision alone is the same as writing off the goods. Review the actual accounting and inventory treatment.
Goods approved for write-off and removed from stock recordsSection 17(5)(h) is relevant. Identify ITC attributable to the goods written off.
Goods sold as scrapAnalyse the outward supply and applicable GST rate/classification. Do not treat a sale as if goods simply disappeared.
Goods returned to supplierFollow the return/credit-note documentation and adjust ITC as legally applicable.
Practical caution: Do not postpone an actual stock write-off merely to avoid an ITC question. Correct books, approvals and a defensible GST position are more important than keeping unusable goods artificially on the stock register.

10. Scrap, off-cuts and recoverable residue

Scrap is not the same as a missing quantity. If steel cutting creates off-cuts, the off-cuts may still have value and may be sold, reused or returned to the store. The business should record them as scrap or recoverable residue rather than burying them in a general wastage percentage.

Example: steel reconciliation
Steel issued for work: 10,000 kg
Steel incorporated into structure: 9,200 kg
Recoverable off-cuts returned to scrap yard: 650 kg
Documented cutting/process residue: 100 kg
Unexplained difference: 50 kg

How to read it
9,200 kg is used in work. 650 kg remains as recoverable scrap and should be tracked. The 100 kg needs technical support as genuine process loss. The remaining 50 kg should be investigated; it should not automatically be absorbed into the 100 kg process-loss figure.

When scrap is sold, determine the correct classification, rate, place of supply and invoice treatment based on the nature of the scrap and the transaction. This article is focused on the inventory/ITC side and does not replace a product-specific rate analysis.

11. ITC under Section 17(5)(h): the central GST rule

Section 17(5)(h) of the CGST Act provides that input tax credit is not available in respect of goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off or disposed of by way of gift or free samples. The clause operates notwithstanding the general entitlement provisions in Section 16(1) and Section 18(1).

For a business, the practical question is: what quantity of goods falls within one of these specified events, and how much ITC relates to that quantity?

SituationSection 17(5)(h) relevancePractical treatment
Goods consumed in taxable project executionNot automatically a loss merely because they are no longer in stock.Retain consumption and project-use evidence.
Inherent process lossMay be distinguishable from goods lost/destroyed, as considered in ARS Steels for manufacturing loss.Prove the technical process, expected yield and actual consumption.
Goods stolenExpressly covered.Reverse ITC attributable to stolen goods.
Goods destroyedExpressly covered.Reverse ITC attributable to destroyed goods.
Goods written offExpressly covered.Identify goods actually written off and corresponding credit.
Gifts/free samplesExpressly covered by the clause.Analyse the goods disposed of and applicable ITC restriction.

How to calculate the ITC attributable to the lost quantity

Where the same item was purchased in different lots at different rates, do not simply multiply the shortage by an arbitrary average GST rate. Link the quantity to the relevant inward invoices and the ITC actually availed. Use a consistent and supportable inventory valuation/issue method, and document the calculation.

Illustration only
1,000 units purchased at taxable value ₹100 each, GST at 18% = ₹18,000 ITC. If 40 units are established as stolen and all units carry the same tax incidence, the attributable ITC is ₹720 (40 × ₹18). If purchases have different rates, batches or credit status, calculate from the relevant invoice/batch records instead of using this simple illustration.

For goods on which no ITC was availed, there is no input credit to reverse for that purchase. Maintain the invoice and credit ledger trail to demonstrate the position.

Do not over-reverse: If 100 bags were purchased, 70 were consumed in business, 20 remain in stock and 10 were stolen, the ITC analysis should focus on the 10 stolen bags—not automatically the entire purchase lot.

12. Section 35(6): what if goods are not accounted for?

Section 35 of the CGST Act requires registered persons to maintain true and correct accounts, including stock of goods and ITC. Section 35(6) addresses a situation where a registered person fails to account for goods or services in accordance with Section 35(1). In such a case, the proper officer may determine the tax payable on the goods or services not accounted for as if they had been supplied by that person, subject to the statutory framework and the express reference to Section 17(5)(h).

This is why unexplained shortages deserve serious attention. A taxpayer should not assume that an unexplained quantity is automatically a taxable sale; however, failure to maintain and produce a credible stock trail can create a separate exposure under the accounts-and-records provisions.

Example: unexplained shortage during departmental inspection

During an inspection, the department finds that the physical stock is lower than the stock register by ₹8 lakh. The officer asks the company to explain the difference. If the company can produce site-wise issue slips, work records, transfer notes and scrap registers, it may establish that the goods were consumed or moved. If no reliable explanation is available, the officer may examine the shortage under Section 35(6), Section 17(5)(h) and other relevant provisions depending on the facts.

Important distinction: Section 35(6) is not a rule that every physical shortage automatically attracts GST at the normal outward supply rate. The statutory conditions, facts, nature of goods, evidence and applicable demand procedure must be examined. Do not confuse ITC reversal under Section 17(5)(h) with a determination that unaccounted goods are deemed to have been supplied under Section 35(6).

13. Detailed practical examples

Case 1: cement consumed but store issue entry not posted

Site engineer confirms 300 bags were used in a slab pour. The concrete pour card, batch sheet and measurement book support the consumption. The store ledger was not updated before month-end. Correct the store issue record with an authorised backdated reference and retain the approval trail. This is a book/documentation correction, not automatically a theft or destruction event.

Case 2: reinforcement steel off-cuts

Cutting 5,000 kg of bars creates 180 kg of off-cuts. If those off-cuts are reusable or saleable, record them as scrap/recoverable material. The remaining technical cutting loss should be supported by the cutting schedule and site engineer's certification. Do not call all 180 kg “consumed” if it is physically recoverable.

Case 3: diesel consumption exceeds standard

Actual diesel use is 12% above the budget. A higher-than-budget variance does not itself prove theft. Compare equipment hours, idle running, fuel efficiency, route distance, generator load, fuel density/temperature, tank calibration and issue logs. If the investigation establishes genuine operating consumption, record it. If it establishes siphoning, treat the stolen quantity separately for Section 17(5)(h).

Case 4: 40 bags of cement damaged by rain

Inspect whether the bags are fully hardened, partly usable or capable of recovery. Obtain the site report and disposal approval. If the goods are destroyed or written off, identify the ITC attributable to those bags and review reversal under Section 17(5)(h). If a portion is sold as scrap or recovered, document that separate outcome.

Case 5: goods moved to another project

Site A shows a shortage of 500 metres of cable. Site B has the same additional quantity, and the project manager confirms an emergency transfer. Obtain the transfer note, vehicle details, receiving acknowledgement and applicable e-way bill analysis. If the goods are simply at another location, correct the stock location rather than recording a loss. Also review whether the movement is between distinct persons/registrations and whether a taxable supply or documentation requirement arises.

Case 6: stock written off after quality rejection

Electrical cables fail a quality test and are approved for write-off. The goods remain physically segregated until disposal. The company should preserve the test report, rejection note, write-off approval, stock register adjustment and disposal evidence. Section 17(5)(h) specifically covers goods written off; assess the ITC attributable to the written-off goods.

Case 7: goods lost in transit

A supplier dispatches goods and the transporter reports an accident before delivery. Establish who owned the goods at the time, whether the recipient had received them for Section 16 purposes, whether any ITC was actually availed, and what the contract and insurance documents say. Do not mechanically apply a single answer without resolving receipt, ownership and credit entitlement.

Case 8: scrap returned to stores but not recorded

Steel off-cuts are returned to the scrap yard but no receipt voucher is prepared. The stock report then shows a shortage. Conduct a physical check, prepare a supported correction referencing the original issue, and record scrap separately. If the scrap is subsequently sold, account for the outward supply correctly.

15. GSTR-3B and ITC working: how should finance report the reversal?

Where ITC is determined to be ineligible under Section 17(5)(h), the company should identify the credit, make the required reversal in the appropriate return period and retain a reconciliation between the stock incident and the return. The return disclosure should follow the current GSTR-3B instructions and applicable reporting requirements.

In the commonly used GSTR-3B format, ineligible ITC under Section 17(5) is reported in Table 4(B)(1) as permanent reversal. Verify the form and instructions applicable to the tax period being filed, particularly if the portal or return format has changed.

16. Documentation: what should be kept to defend the treatment?

Good GST documentation begins at the site, not when the auditor asks for it. For every material variance, create one incident file that contains the following information as applicable.

Operational evidence
  • Physical verification sheet
  • Store ledger and stock card
  • Material receipt and issue slips
  • Site transfer notes
  • Work-front consumption report
  • Machine/equipment logbooks
Technical evidence
  • Engineer certification
  • Standard consumption/yield norms
  • Bar-bending or cutting schedules
  • Batch/production reports
  • Quality rejection report
  • Weighbridge and calibration records
Incident evidence
  • Incident/accident report
  • Police complaint/FIR where relevant
  • Fire service or authority report
  • Photographs and CCTV extracts
  • Insurance surveyor report
  • Disposal/destruction certificate
Finance and GST evidence
  • Purchase invoice and ITC ledger
  • Item-wise ITC computation
  • Write-off approval
  • Journal voucher
  • GSTR-3B reversal working
  • Management review and sign-off

Where records are electronic, retain the underlying source data and audit trail. A spreadsheet prepared after an audit begins is less persuasive than a dated site report created when the event occurred.

17. ERP and site controls: prevent the shortage before month-end

A multi-site business should not manage material only through one consolidated stores ledger. The ERP should identify the location, project, store, item, unit, batch and movement type.

Suggested monthly stock reconciliation

  1. Download opening stock, receipts, issues, returns, transfers and closing stock by site and item.
  2. Obtain physical verification for high-value and high-risk materials.
  3. Match store issues with work-front consumption and machinery logs.
  4. Identify recoverable scrap separately from process loss.
  5. Prepare a variance report showing quantity, value, reason and supporting document.
  6. Classify each variance as book error, transfer, consumption, normal process loss, theft, destruction, write-off or unexplained.
  7. Route all theft, destruction, write-off and unexplained items to finance for GST review.
  8. Close the month only after the site manager and finance reviewer sign off.
Useful ERP designUse mandatory reason codes for every stock adjustment. A user should not be able to select “wastage” without entering the technical reason, approved norm, quantity, project and supporting document reference.

18. GST audit checklist for material shortages

Use this checklist during monthly review, statutory audit, internal audit or departmental GST audit.

  1. Does the company maintain item-wise and location-wise stock records as required by Section 35 and Rule 56?
  2. Are opening balance, inward receipts, outward issues, losses, theft, destruction, write-offs, scrap and wastage separately identifiable?
  3. Can each major consumption entry be linked to a project, work order, machine, production batch or work front?
  4. Are inter-site transfers supported by movement records and receiving acknowledgements?
  5. Are standard consumption norms approved by engineering/operations and periodically reviewed?
  6. Are normal process loss and recoverable scrap separately quantified?
  7. Are abnormal variances investigated rather than automatically posted as consumption?
  8. Are theft, fire, flood and damage events supported by incident documentation?
  9. Has ITC attributable to stolen, destroyed or written-off goods been identified and reviewed under Section 17(5)(h)?
  10. Have goods with no ITC availed been distinguished from goods on which credit was claimed?
  11. Has the company considered Section 35(6) where goods remain unaccounted for after investigation?
  12. Do the stock register, general ledger, ITC working and GSTR-3B agree?
  13. Are all write-offs and GST reversals supported by authorised approvals?
  14. Are unresolved differences carried forward with an owner and due date rather than silently cleared?
Audit file recommendationKeep one monthly “Material Variance and GST Impact Register” for all projects. This gives the finance team one place to monitor the cause, quantity, value, ITC impact, journal entry, return treatment and closure status.

19. Frequently asked questions

1. Is ITC required to be reversed on every material shortage?

No. First establish the cause. A posting error, transfer or genuine business consumption is not automatically a Section 17(5)(h) event. Theft, destruction and write-off are expressly covered. Unexplained shortages require careful investigation and may create additional exposure.

2. Is normal wastage in construction allowed without ITC reversal?

There is no blanket percentage. Where the quantity is genuinely consumed as an inherent part of the process, distinguish it from goods lost or destroyed. Support the position with technical norms and actual records. ARS Steels is a relevant Madras High Court decision on inherent manufacturing loss, but its facts should not be stretched to unrelated shortages.

3. Do we reverse ITC on steel cutting scrap?

Not merely because steel is cut. Recoverable off-cuts should be recorded as scrap and tracked. The quantity genuinely consumed or lost in the process should be supported by cutting schedules and technical records. Review GST on any later scrap sale separately.

4. Cement bags became hard due to rain. Is ITC blocked?

If the bags are destroyed/unusable and written off, Section 17(5)(h) is relevant. Establish whether any portion is recoverable or still usable, and document the actual disposal/write-off.

5. What if diesel is missing from the tank?

Reconcile tank calibration, opening/closing dip, receipts, machine consumption and transfers. If the investigation establishes theft, the stolen quantity is covered by Section 17(5)(h). Do not treat every unexplained dip variation as theft without investigation.

6. Does insurance recovery allow the company to retain ITC on destroyed goods?

Do not assume so. The ITC restriction on destroyed goods must be analysed under Section 17(5)(h) independently of the insurance settlement.

7. Is a provision for obsolete stock the same as a write-off?

Not necessarily. Examine whether the goods remain recognised in inventory or have actually been written off/removed from stock. The accounting treatment and supporting records matter.

8. Does every stock shortage become a deemed sale under Section 35(6)?

No automatic conclusion should be drawn from a variance alone. Section 35(6) addresses failure to account for goods in accordance with Section 35(1). The facts, explanation, statutory conditions and proper officer's determination matter.

9. Can the company reverse ITC only on the quantity stolen?

The calculation should identify the goods actually stolen and the ITC attributable to those goods. Where purchase batches have different rates or credit histories, use invoice/batch-level records and a consistent method.

10. Which GSTR-3B table is used for Section 17(5) reversal?

Under the commonly used GSTR-3B format, permanent reversal under Section 17(5) is reported in Table 4(B)(1). Verify the instructions applicable to the relevant tax period before filing.

11. What if goods were moved to another GST registration of the same company?

First establish whether the locations are under the same GSTIN or separate registrations. A transfer between distinct persons may have a separate supply and documentation analysis. A location-only correction within the same registration is a different situation.

12. How long should the records be retained?

Section 36 generally requires retention for 72 months from the due date of furnishing the annual return for the relevant year, with a longer period where appeal, revision, other proceedings or investigation conditions apply. Check the current statutory text for the specific records and case.
