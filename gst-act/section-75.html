GST ACT — SECTION 75

Section 75 CGST Act — General Provisions Relating to Determination of Tax

Section 75 is the procedural backbone of GST demand adjudication. It explains how limitation is affected by court stays, what happens when a Section 74 fraud allegation fails, when an appellate direction must be implemented, the right to personal hearing, limits on adjournments, what a GST order must contain, why an order cannot exceed the notice, how modified tax changes interest and penalty, when proceedings conclude, how pending appeals affect limitation, and how self-assessed tax is recovered.

1. Why Section 75 matters

Searches such as “Section 75 CGST Act”, “Section 75 GST”, “GST personal hearing”, “GST demand order”, “GST SCN order”, “GST adjudication”, “Section 75(4) GST”, “Section 75(7) GST”, “Section 75(10) GST” and “Section 75(12) GST” all point to this provision.

Sections 73, 74 and 74A tell the department which demand framework applies. Section 75 supplies many of the general rules governing how that determination is handled.

Simple idea: Section 75 is the “rules of the game” for GST tax determination proceedings. It protects procedural fairness while also setting boundaries for the proper officer.

2. Section 75 in very simple language

Suppose a GST officer issues a demand notice saying that ₹20 lakh tax is payable. Section 75 answers several practical questions:

  • What happens if a court stayed the proceedings?
  • What if a Section 74 fraud allegation fails on appeal?
  • When must a hearing be given?
  • How many adjournments can be granted?
  • Can the final order demand more than the notice?
  • Can the officer introduce a completely new ground in the order?
  • What happens to interest if an appellate authority reduces the tax?
  • When is the adjudication proceeding considered concluded?
  • What happens to self-assessed tax that remains unpaid?
Notice → reply/hearing → reasoned order → appeal/modification → interest/penalty adjusted according to the final tax

4. Court stay and limitation

If service of a notice or issuance of an order is stayed by an order of a court or Appellate Tribunal, Section 75(1) requires the period of the stay to be excluded when calculating the specified limitation periods under Sections 73, 74 and the relevant Section 74A provisions.

This prevents a taxpayer or department from losing statutory time merely because the proceeding was legally prevented from moving forward during a judicial stay.

A stay period should be calculated carefully from the actual judicial order. Do not simply subtract the entire duration of a litigation unless the order legally stayed the relevant service/issuance covered by Section 75(1).

5. Section 74 converted to Section 73

One of the most important protections in Section 75 is subsection (2).

If an Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court concludes that a Section 74 notice is not sustainable because fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax has not been established, the proper officer must determine the tax by treating the notice as if it had been issued under Section 73.

CBIC Circular No. 185/17/2022-GST specifically explains the operation of Section 75(2), including the time limit for re-determination after an appellate direction. citeturn0search5turn0search1

Fraud allegation fails → Section 75(2) → treat notice as Section 73 → re-determine tax under applicable Section 73 framework

6. Orders after appellate directions

Section 75(3) provides a separate rule where an order has to be issued in pursuance of a direction of an Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court.

The order is to be issued within two years from the date of communication of the direction.

This is different from the ordinary limitation period applicable to the original demand proceeding.

Always identify whether the officer is issuing an original demand order or a re-determination order following an appellate direction. The limitation rule can be different.

7. Right to personal hearing

Section 75(4) requires an opportunity of hearing where:

  • the person chargeable with tax or penalty requests it in writing; or
  • the proper officer contemplates an adverse decision against that person.

This is a major procedural safeguard. A taxpayer should not assume that a written reply alone is always the end of the process.

Practical hearing preparation

  • Prepare an issue-wise summary.
  • Carry the reconciliation supporting the reply.
  • Identify disputed figures clearly.
  • Explain documents rather than merely submitting them.
  • Record the main submissions made during the hearing.

8. Adjournment limits

Section 75(5) allows the proper officer to grant time and adjourn a hearing where sufficient cause is shown, with reasons recorded in writing.

The proviso limits adjournments to three times to a person during the proceedings.

An adjournment should therefore be requested with a genuine and documented reason—such as the need to compile substantial records or unavoidable circumstances—not merely to delay proceedings.

9. What a GST order must contain

Section 75(6) requires the proper officer's order to set out the relevant facts and the basis of the decision.

A proper adjudication order should therefore allow the taxpayer to understand:

  • what facts were accepted;
  • what facts were rejected;
  • which legal provision was applied;
  • how the taxable amount was calculated;
  • how interest/penalty was determined; and
  • why the taxpayer's submissions were accepted or rejected.

10. Order cannot exceed SCN

Section 75(7) contains two important limits:

  1. The amount of tax, interest and penalty demanded in the order cannot be more than the amount specified in the notice.
  2. No demand can be confirmed on grounds other than the grounds specified in the notice.
SCN grounds + SCN amount = outer boundary of adjudication order

This is one of the most practically important provisions when reviewing a GST demand order.

Example

If the notice alleges ₹10 lakh tax on a particular classification issue, the final order cannot simply introduce a completely different undisclosed ground and create a new demand outside the notice framework.

11. Effect of appellate modification

Under Section 75(8), if an Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court modifies the tax amount determined by the proper officer, the corresponding interest and penalty are also required to be modified by taking the revised tax amount into account.

This prevents the interest and penalty from remaining artificially linked to a tax amount that has already been reduced on appeal.

12. Interest is payable

Section 75(9) provides that interest on tax short paid or not paid is payable whether or not the interest amount is specifically mentioned in the order determining the tax liability.

This means a taxpayer should not assume that omission of a separate interest figure in an adjudication order automatically extinguishes the statutory interest liability.

Interest should be calculated with reference to Section 50 and the applicable Rule 88B methodology.

13. When adjudication concludes

Section 75(10) provides that adjudication proceedings are deemed to be concluded if the applicable order is not issued within the period specified under the relevant demand provision—historically Section 73 or 74 and, under the current law, the relevant Section 74A provision.

The exact deadline must therefore be calculated using the tax period, the applicable demand section and any valid exclusion such as a court stay under Section 75(1) or other statutory exclusion.

14. Pending higher appeal and limitation

Section 75(11) addresses a special situation where an appellate decision is prejudicial to the revenue, an appeal against that decision is pending before the next appellate forum, and a new proceeding is initiated under the specified demand provisions.

The period spent between the relevant appellate decisions is excluded while computing the applicable adjudication period, subject to the statutory conditions.

This provision prevents a pending higher appeal on an important legal issue from unfairly consuming the department's statutory adjudication period in another proceeding where the same issue is involved.

15. Self-assessed tax under Section 75(12)

Section 75(12) creates an important distinction between a disputed departmental demand and self-assessed tax that the taxpayer has already declared but has not paid.

If an amount of self-assessed tax reported in a Section 39 return remains unpaid, wholly or partly, or interest payable on that tax remains unpaid, it is recovered under Section 79, notwithstanding Sections 73, 74 or 74A.

Simple meaning: If you have already declared the tax yourself but have not paid it, the department can move into the recovery framework rather than treating the amount as an ordinary disputed determination case.

16. GSTR-1 liability and Section 75(12)

The Explanation to Section 75(12) expands the meaning of “self-assessed tax” for this purpose to include tax payable in respect of outward-supply details furnished under Section 37 but not included in the return furnished under Section 39.

In practical terms, this is important where a taxpayer reports taxable outward supplies through the outward-supply reporting system but fails to include the corresponding tax in the return.

This provision makes GSTR-1 vs GSTR-3B reconciliation particularly important. A difference should be investigated immediately rather than waiting for recovery action.

17. One act, no double penalty

Section 75(13) provides that where a penalty is imposed under Section 73, Section 74 or Section 74A, no penalty for the same act or omission shall be imposed on the same person under another provision of the CGST Act.

This is an important protection against double penalisation for the same underlying conduct.

SituationApproach
Same act, two penalty provisionsSection 75(13) prevents a second penalty for the same act/omission where its conditions apply.
Different acts/omissionsThe analysis may be different; each provision must be examined separately.

Section 75 therefore should be read together with the specific demand section rather than in isolation.

19. Section 75 with Section 74A

Section 74A is the newer determination framework for specified periods beginning with FY 2024-25. The current Section 75 text has been updated to expressly refer to Section 74A in subsections such as 75(1), 75(2A), 75(10), 75(11), 75(12) and 75(13). citeturn0search0turn0search6

This is important for current GST practice: Section 75 is not frozen in the pre-2024 Sections 73/74 structure. Its procedural rules now also operate with the Section 74A framework.

21. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Court stay

A court stays service of a notice for 120 days. The relevant stay period can be excluded from the statutory computation under Section 75(1), subject to the exact terms of the order.

Example 2 — Section 74 allegation fails

An appellate authority concludes that fraud/suppression was not established. Section 75(2) requires the tax to be determined by treating the notice as one under Section 73.

Example 3 — Appellate remand

An appellate authority directs re-determination. The officer must apply the special two-year period under Section 75(3) from communication of the direction.

Example 4 — Hearing requested

The taxpayer submits a written request for personal hearing. Section 75(4) provides the hearing safeguard.

Example 5 — Excess adjournments

A taxpayer repeatedly seeks adjournments without sufficient cause. Section 75(5) limits adjournments to three during the proceedings.

Example 6 — Order exceeds notice

The SCN proposes ₹5 lakh tax, but the order confirms ₹8 lakh. Section 75(7) requires the demand not to exceed the amount specified in the notice.

Example 7 — New ground in order

The SCN is based on valuation, but the final order creates a demand on an unrelated classification issue without that ground being in the notice. Section 75(7) becomes important.

Example 8 — Tax reduced on appeal

Tax is reduced from ₹20 lakh to ₹12 lakh. Interest and penalty should correspondingly be modified based on the revised tax.

Example 9 — Unpaid self-assessed tax

A taxpayer reports ₹10 lakh output tax in GSTR-3B but pays only ₹7 lakh. The unpaid self-assessed amount falls into the Section 75(12)/Section 79 recovery framework.

Example 10 — GSTR-1 vs GSTR-3B

A taxpayer reports taxable outward supplies in GSTR-1 but omits the corresponding tax from GSTR-3B. The Explanation to Section 75(12) makes this difference particularly important.

22. Construction and infrastructure examples

RA bill reported in GSTR-1 but not GSTR-3B

Reconcile the RA bill register, invoices, GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B immediately. An unexplained difference can become a self-assessed-tax recovery issue.

Mobilisation advance

Maintain advance receipts, tax invoices, adjustment against RA bills and ledger reconciliation.

Subcontractor ITC dispute

Keep work orders, invoices, measurement records, payment evidence and 2B/IMS reconciliation to explain the ITC position.

Project-wise turnover

For large infrastructure businesses, maintain GSTIN-wise and project-wise reconciliations so that the department can trace the tax calculation without relying on incomplete summaries.

Inter-state projects

Maintain place-of-supply workings, contracts, customer GSTINs and tax-head reconciliation to support CGST/SGST versus IGST treatment.

24. Practical safeguards

  • Identify the exact demand provision and tax period.
  • Calculate limitation independently.
  • Check every allegation against the SCN.
  • Request personal hearing in writing where appropriate.
  • Ensure the final order is compared line-by-line with the SCN.
  • Check whether the order introduces a new ground.
  • Check whether the amount exceeds the notice.
  • Recalculate interest and penalty after any appellate reduction.
  • Track communication dates for appellate directions.
  • Reconcile GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B every month.

27. Common mistakes

  • Thinking Section 75 itself creates a new tax liability.
  • Ignoring the tax period and applicable demand section.
  • Not requesting a hearing in writing.
  • Assuming unlimited adjournments are available.
  • Not comparing the final order with the SCN.
  • Accepting a new ground introduced only in the order.
  • Ignoring limitation/stay periods.
  • Failing to recalculate interest after an appellate reduction.
  • Ignoring unpaid self-assessed tax because there is no separate SCN.
  • Failing to reconcile GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B.

29. Section 75 GST FAQs

What is Section 75 of the CGST Act?

Section 75 contains general provisions relating to determination of tax, including hearing, adjournments, order limits, appellate directions, interest, limitation and recovery of self-assessed tax.

What is Section 75(4)?

It provides an opportunity of hearing where the person chargeable with tax or penalty requests it in writing or where the officer contemplates an adverse decision.

How many adjournments are allowed under Section 75?

Where sufficient cause is shown, the officer may adjourn the hearing, but the statutory proviso limits adjournments to three during the proceedings.

Can a GST order exceed the SCN?

Section 75(7) says the amount of tax, interest and penalty in the order cannot exceed the amount specified in the notice, and no demand can be confirmed on grounds other than those specified in the notice.

What must a GST order contain?

Section 75(6) requires the order to set out the relevant facts and the basis of the decision.

What happens if a Section 74 fraud allegation fails?

Section 75(2) provides that the tax is determined by deeming the notice to have been issued under Section 73. CBIC Circular 185/17/2022-GST explains the re-determination process.

What is the two-year rule under Section 75(3)?

Where an order must be issued pursuant to a direction of an Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or court, Section 75(3) provides a two-year period from communication of the direction.

Does interest have to be mentioned in the order?

Section 75(9) provides that interest on tax short paid or not paid remains payable whether or not it is specifically specified in the order determining the tax liability.

What is Section 75(12)?

It provides that unpaid self-assessed tax and applicable interest are recovered under Section 79, notwithstanding Sections 73, 74 or 74A.

What does the Explanation to Section 75(12) do?

It includes tax payable on outward supplies furnished under Section 37 but not included in the Section 39 return within the meaning of self-assessed tax for this purpose.

What is Section 75(13)?

Where penalty is imposed under Section 73, 74 or 74A, no penalty for the same act or omission is to be imposed on the same person under another provision of the CGST Act.

Does Section 75 apply to Section 74A?

Yes. The current text expressly incorporates Section 74A references in several subsections.

What is the difference between Section 75 and Section 73?

Section 73 is a specific historical demand provision; Section 75 contains general procedural rules that govern determination under the applicable demand framework.

31. Key takeaways

  • Section 75 is the procedural backbone of GST tax determination.
  • Stay periods can be excluded from specified limitation calculations.
  • If a Section 74 fraud allegation fails, Section 75(2) provides the Section 73 re-determination mechanism.
  • Appellate directions generally carry a two-year order period under Section 75(3).
  • Personal hearing is an important statutory safeguard.
  • Adjournments are limited to three.
  • The order must state relevant facts and the basis of the decision.
  • The final demand cannot exceed the notice amount or travel beyond the grounds stated in the notice.
  • Appellate reduction of tax correspondingly affects interest and penalty.
  • Unpaid self-assessed tax is recovered under Section 79, including the specified GSTR-1/GSTR-3B situation.
  • Section 75 now expressly works with the Section 74A framework for newer periods.
  • No second penalty should be imposed for the same act or omission where Section 75(13) applies.
