1. Current Statutory Text of Section 132

132. Punishment for certain offences.

(1) Whoever commits, or causes to commit and retain the benefits arising out of, any of the following offences, namely—

(a) supplies any goods or services or both without issue of any invoice, in violation of the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder, with the intention to evade tax;

(b) issues any invoice or bill without supply of goods or services or both in violation of the provisions of this Act, or the rules made thereunder leading to wrongful availment or utilisation of input tax credit or refund of tax;

(c) avails input tax credit using the invoice or bill referred to in clause (b) or fraudulently avails input tax credit without any invoice or bill;

(d) collects any amount as tax but fails to pay the same to the Government beyond a period of three months from the date on which such payment becomes due;

(e) evades tax or fraudulently obtains refund and where such offence is not covered under clauses (a) to (d);

(f) falsifies or substitutes financial records or produces fake accounts or documents or furnishes any false information with an intention to evade payment of tax due under this Act;

(h) acquires possession of, or in any way concerns himself in transporting, removing, depositing, keeping, concealing, supplying, or purchasing or in any other manner deals with, any goods which he knows or has reasons to believe are liable to confiscation under this Act or the rules made thereunder;

(i) receives or is in any way concerned with the supply of, or in any other manner deals with any supply of services which he knows or has reasons to believe are in contravention of any provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder;

(l) attempts to commit, or abets the commission of any of the offences mentioned in clauses (a) to (f) and clauses (h) and (i) of this section,

shall be punishable—

(i) in cases where the amount of tax evaded or the amount of input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised or the amount of refund wrongly taken exceeds five hundred lakh rupees, with imprisonment for a term which may extend to five years and with fine;

(ii) in cases where the amount of tax evaded or the amount of input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised or the amount of refund wrongly taken exceeds two hundred lakh rupees but does not exceed five hundred lakh rupees, with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and with fine;

(iii) in the case of an offence specified in clause (b), where the amount of tax evaded or the amount of input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised or the amount of refund wrongly taken exceeds one hundred lakh rupees but does not exceed two hundred lakh rupees, with imprisonment for a term which may extend to one year and with fine;

(iv) in cases where he commits or abets the commission of an offence specified in clause (f), (g), (j) or (k), he shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months or with fine or with both.

(2) Where any person convicted of an offence under this section is again convicted of an offence under this section, then, he shall be punishable for the second and for every subsequent offence with imprisonment for a term which may extend to five years and with fine.

(3) The imprisonment referred to in clauses (i), (ii) and (iii) of sub-section (1) and sub-section (2) shall, in the absence of special and adequate reasons to the contrary to be recorded in the judgment of the Court, be for a term not less than six months.

(4) Notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, all offences under this Act, except the offences referred to in sub-section (5) shall be non-cognizable and bailable.

(5) The offences specified in clause (a) or clause (b) or clause (c) or clause (d) of sub-section (1) and punishable under clause (i) of that sub-section shall be cognizable and non-bailable.

(6) A person shall not be prosecuted for any offence under this section except with the previous sanction of the Commissioner.

Explanation.—For the purposes of this section, the term “tax” shall include the amount of tax evaded or the amount of input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised or refund wrongly taken under the provisions of this Act, the State Goods and Services Tax Act, the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act or the Union Territory Goods and Services Tax Act and cess levied under the Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act.
Reading note: The current text has omitted clauses (g), (j) and (k), and the punishment structure in clause (iii) was narrowed by the 2023 amendment. The omitted letters are therefore intentionally absent from the current offence list.

2. What Section 132 Means in Simple Language

Section 132 is the main CGST Act provision dealing with criminal punishment for specified GST offences. It is different from ordinary late fees, interest and many departmental penalties.

The section identifies particular conduct that Parliament has treated as sufficiently serious to attract prosecution. If the offence is proved and the person is convicted, imprisonment and/or fine can follow depending on the applicable clause.

Three things should not be mixed up

ConceptMeaning
GST demandDetermination/recovery of tax, interest and applicable penalty.
Departmental penaltyMonetary consequence imposed under a tax provision.
Prosecution under Section 132Criminal proceeding for a specified offence, ultimately decided through the criminal justice process.
Important: Receiving a GST notice or even facing a departmental penalty is not the same thing as being convicted under Section 132. Criminal liability requires the applicable offence, evidence and the prescribed prosecution process.

3. Which GST Offences Are Covered?

The current Section 132 offence list should be read carefully because amendments have removed some older offence clauses. The principal current categories are:

ClauseConduct coveredKey feature
(a)Supplying goods/services without invoice in violation of GST law, with intention to evade tax.Requires the specified invoice violation plus intention to evade tax.
(b)Issuing an invoice/bill without an underlying supply, where it leads to wrongful ITC or refund.Focuses on fake/accommodation invoicing and resulting wrongful credit/refund.
(c)Availing ITC using the invoice/bill covered by clause (b), or fraudulently availing ITC without invoice/bill.Targets fraudulent ITC.
(d)Collecting an amount as tax and failing to pay it to Government beyond three months after it becomes due.Focuses on tax collected but retained beyond the statutory period.
(e)Evading tax or fraudulently obtaining refund where the conduct is not covered by clauses (a)–(d).Residual serious tax-evasion/refund category.
(f)Falsifying/substituting financial records, producing fake accounts/documents or giving false information with intention to evade tax.Requires the specified conduct and tax-evasion intention.
(h)Dealing with goods known or reasonably believed to be liable to confiscation.Knowledge/reason to believe is important.
(i)Dealing with services known or reasonably believed to contravene GST law.Knowledge/reason to believe is relevant.
(l)Attempting or abetting specified offences in clauses (a)–(f), (h) and (i).Attempt and abetment are separately covered.

4. Punishment Thresholds Under Section 132(1)

The statutory punishment depends principally on the amount of tax evaded, ITC wrongly availed or utilised, or refund wrongly taken, subject to the specific offence covered by the relevant punishment clause.

CategoryAmount involvedMaximum punishment
Highest categoryExceeds ₹5 croreUp to 5 years' imprisonment and fine.
Second categoryMore than ₹2 crore and up to ₹5 croreUp to 3 years' imprisonment and fine.
Clause (b) categoryMore than ₹1 crore and up to ₹2 crore, where the offence is specified in clause (b)Up to 1 year imprisonment and fine.
Specified conduct in clause (f)Independent of the above monetary bandsUp to 6 months, or fine, or both, subject to the current statutory wording and applicable amendment.
Very important: The ₹1 crore–₹2 crore punishment category is currently specifically tied to an offence under clause (b). Do not copy older articles that describe it as applying to “any other offence.” That wording was changed with effect from 1 October 2023.

The five-year, three-year and one-year bands are maximum terms. They are not statements that a person will automatically receive the maximum sentence.

5. Clause-by-Clause Explanation in Layman Language

5.1 Clause (a) – Supply without invoice with intention to evade tax

This provision is aimed at a supply where the supplier does not issue the required invoice and the conduct is connected with an intention to evade tax.

A simple billing mistake should not automatically be described as a Section 132(a) offence. The statutory ingredients, including the intention to evade tax, matter.

5.2 Clause (b) – Invoice without actual supply

This covers issuing an invoice or bill without the corresponding supply where it leads to wrongful availment or utilisation of ITC or refund.

This is particularly important in cases involving accommodation invoices, paper transactions and transactions where invoices are circulated without the underlying supply.

5.3 Clause (c) – Wrongful ITC

Clause (c) addresses availing ITC using an invoice/bill covered by clause (b), as well as fraudulent ITC availed without an invoice or bill.

The finance team should therefore distinguish between an ordinary reconciliation mismatch and evidence of fraudulent ITC. A mismatch is a compliance issue; the criminal provision has additional statutory requirements.

5.4 Clause (d) – Tax collected but not paid

If a person collects an amount as tax and does not pay it to the Government beyond three months from when payment becomes due, the conduct can fall within clause (d).

This makes collection of GST a particularly important cash-flow control. A business should not treat GST collected from customers as unrestricted operating cash.

5.5 Clause (e) – Other tax evasion or fraudulent refund

Clause (e) covers tax evasion or fraudulent refund where the conduct is not covered by clauses (a) to (d). It is therefore broader than a single invoice or ITC mechanism, but the exact facts and evidence determine whether the clause applies.

5.6 Clause (f) – Falsified records, fake documents and false information

This clause is particularly relevant to accounting and finance teams because it expressly addresses falsification or substitution of financial records, fake accounts/documents and false information when accompanied by an intention to evade tax.

For a company, internal controls over ERP access, invoice numbering, journal entries, document retention and audit trails can therefore become important evidence.

5.7 Clause (h) – Dealing with goods liable to confiscation

This covers a person who deals with goods liable to confiscation and who knows or has reason to believe that the goods are liable to confiscation.

The wording is broader than simply being the owner of the goods. It refers to activities such as transporting, removing, keeping, concealing, supplying, purchasing or otherwise dealing with such goods.

5.8 Clause (i) – Dealing with contravening services

This clause concerns dealing with supplies of services where the person knows or has reason to believe that the supply contravenes GST law.

5.9 Clause (l) – Attempt and abetment

Section 132 does not restrict liability only to a completed offence. The current wording also covers attempts and abetment relating to specified clauses.

6. Important Changes to Section 132

2021 changes

The Finance Act, 2021 changed the wording of Section 132. Among other changes, clause (c) was substituted, and the words relating to fraudulent ITC were removed from clause (e). These changes became effective from 1 January 2021.

2023 changes

The Finance Act, 2023 made important changes effective from 1 October 2023. Clauses (g), (j) and (k) were omitted. The attempt/abetment clause was correspondingly narrowed from the older reference to clauses (a) to (k) to clauses (a) to (f) and (h) and (i).

The punishment provision for the ₹1 crore–₹2 crore range was also changed so that clause (iii) specifically applies to an offence under clause (b), rather than “any other offence.”

These amendments matter when reading older GST articles, notices, internal manuals or compliance checklists because many older explanations still reproduce the pre-October-2023 structure.

Practical rule: Always identify the version of Section 132 applicable to the relevant date of the alleged conduct. Do not rely on an old offence list simply because it appears in a historical GST article.

7. Cognizable, Non-Cognizable, Bailable and Non-Bailable

Section 132(4) provides the general rule that offences under the CGST Act are non-cognizable and bailable except those falling within Section 132(5).

Section 132(5) identifies a narrower category as cognizable and non-bailable: offences specified in clauses (a), (b), (c) or (d) of sub-section (1) when punishable under the highest punishment category in clause (i), i.e. where the relevant amount exceeds ₹5 crore.

CategoryGeneral treatment under Section 132
Most GST offencesNon-cognizable and bailable, subject to the Act.
Specified high-value offences under (a)–(d)Cognizable and non-bailable when punishable under Section 132(1)(i).
Layman explanation: “Non-cognizable and bailable” does not mean “not serious.” It describes the legal classification and procedural treatment of the offence. Likewise, “cognizable and non-bailable” does not itself establish guilt; guilt must still be determined through the legal process.

8. Previous Sanction of the Commissioner

Section 132(6) contains an important procedural safeguard: a person cannot be prosecuted for an offence under Section 132 without the previous sanction of the Commissioner.

This means a prosecution is not supposed to begin merely because an investigating officer or another person alleges that an offence has occurred. The statutory sanction requirement must be satisfied.

Why this matters

  • It creates a formal approval stage before prosecution.
  • It separates investigation/enforcement activity from the statutory decision to prosecute.
  • It is important when reviewing the validity and chronology of a prosecution case.
  • It should be distinguished from the merits of the underlying allegation.
Do not confuse sanction with conviction. Commissioner sanction permits prosecution to proceed under the statutory framework; it is not a judicial finding that the accused is guilty.

9. Repeat Conviction Under Section 132(2)

Section 132(2) deals with a person who has already been convicted of an offence under Section 132 and is subsequently convicted again of an offence under Section 132.

For the second and every subsequent offence, the punishment can extend to five years' imprisonment and fine.

This provision is therefore concerned with a repeat conviction, not merely the fact that a person has received multiple notices or investigations.

Example – Repeat conviction

A person is convicted for a Section 132 offence. In a later case, the same person is again convicted for an offence under Section 132. Section 132(2) becomes relevant to the punishment for the second and subsequent offence.

10. Intention, Knowledge and Evidence

Several parts of Section 132 contain important words such as “intention to evade tax,” “fraudulently,” “knows,” and “has reason to believe.” These words are not decorative. They can materially affect the legal analysis.

ExpressionWhy it mattersPractical evidence that may become relevant
Intention to evade taxRelevant to specified offences such as clause (a) and clause (f).Transaction pattern, communications, records, instructions, repeated conduct.
FraudulentlyRelevant to specified ITC/refund conduct.Invoice chains, payment trails, supplier/customer records, system logs.
KnowsRequires consideration of actual knowledge where the clause uses that term.Emails, messages, approvals, acknowledgements, records of instructions.
Reason to believeRequires analysis of circumstances from which the relevant belief may arise.Due diligence records, transport documents, unusual transaction indicators.

For a genuine business, this is why contemporaneous documentation matters. A tax investigation may examine not only the final invoice but also the commercial substance, approvals, payment trail, inventory records, ERP history and communications surrounding the transaction.

11. Practical Examples

Example 1 – Genuine invoice error

An employee enters the wrong HSN code on an otherwise genuine invoice. There is no evidence of an intention to evade tax. The company should correct the error and maintain evidence. A routine error should not automatically be labelled a Section 132 offence.

Example 2 – Supply without invoice with evasion intent

A business deliberately makes taxable supplies without issuing invoices and maintains a parallel record to conceal turnover. The facts may raise serious Section 132(a) issues because the provision expressly refers to intention to evade tax.

Example 3 – Invoice without supply

Company A issues invoices to Company B even though no goods or services are supplied, and the invoices are used to generate wrongful ITC or refund. Clause (b) may become relevant.

Example 4 – ITC based on fake invoices

A recipient knowingly uses invoices from an accommodation billing arrangement to claim ITC without the underlying supply. Clause (c) may become relevant depending on the evidence and statutory conditions.

Example 5 – GST collected but retained

A business collects GST from customers but does not pay the collected amount to Government beyond the statutory three-month period after it becomes due. Clause (d) addresses this type of conduct.

Example 6 – Falsified financial records

An employee deliberately changes accounting records and creates fake supporting documents to conceal taxable turnover. If the statutory requirements are established, clause (f) may become relevant.

Example 7 – Goods known to be liable to confiscation

A person knowingly purchases or transports goods that the person knows or has reason to believe are liable to confiscation under GST law. Clause (h) can become relevant.

Example 8 – Attempt

A person takes substantial steps toward committing a specified offence but the completed offence does not occur. The attempt provision may become relevant depending on the exact conduct and applicable law.

Example 9 – Abetment

A person does not personally issue a false invoice but knowingly assists another person in a specified Section 132 offence. The abetment provision may become relevant if its legal requirements are established.

Example 10 – Penalty versus prosecution

A company receives a departmental penalty for a GST contravention. That does not by itself establish that the company or its officers have been convicted under Section 132. The prosecution provisions and criminal process are separate.

12. Practical GST Compliance Workflow for Companies

Step 1 – Build controls around high-risk transactions

  • Invoice generation should be system-controlled wherever possible.
  • Credit notes and debit notes should have documented approval.
  • GST collected should be separately monitored in cash-flow planning.
  • Vendor onboarding should include GSTIN and business verification controls.
  • ITC should be reconciled with purchase records and GST data.
  • Manual journal entries affecting GST ledgers should have approval and audit trails.

Step 2 – Preserve the audit trail

Keep source invoices, contracts, purchase orders, delivery records, e-way bills, payment evidence, inventory records, GST returns, reconciliation workings and relevant ERP logs.

Step 3 – Separate ordinary mismatches from suspected fraud

A missing invoice in GSTR-2B, a date mismatch or a clerical error can have many explanations. Do not jump from a reconciliation exception directly to a criminal allegation. Investigate the transaction and preserve evidence.

Step 4 – If a prosecution-related matter arises

  1. Identify the exact alleged offence and relevant period.
  2. Obtain the notice/order/investigation communication.
  3. Map the alleged conduct to the statutory clause.
  4. Quantify the tax/ITC/refund amount using a documented working.
  5. Review the evidence supporting intention, fraud, knowledge or reason to believe where relevant.
  6. Track the Commissioner sanction and prosecution chronology where applicable.
  7. Obtain qualified legal advice before making factual admissions.

14. Why Section 132 Matters to Finance and Accounts Teams

Section 132 may appear to be a legal department issue, but many of the underlying facts originate in finance and accounting records.

  • GST collected from customers must be tracked and remitted.
  • Sales invoices should correspond to actual supplies.
  • Purchase invoices should represent genuine transactions.
  • ITC workings should be supported by commercial documents.
  • Manual changes to financial records should be controlled.
  • GST return figures should reconcile with books and source records.
  • Supporting documents should be retained for the applicable period.
  • Employees should understand that deliberately creating or altering records can create risks beyond an ordinary accounting error.
Management takeaway: Good GST compliance is not only about filing returns on time. Strong source-document controls, reconciliation, approval workflows and audit trails can become important evidence when the department examines whether conduct was accidental, negligent, deliberate or fraudulent.

15. Common Misunderstandings

“Any GST mistake can result in imprisonment.”

No. Section 132 covers specified offences and has statutory ingredients and punishment thresholds. Ordinary mistakes should not automatically be equated with criminal offences.

“A GST notice means prosecution has started.”

No. A departmental notice, audit query or demand proceeding is not automatically a criminal prosecution under Section 132.

“Crossing ₹5 crore automatically means five years in jail.”

No. The provision states a maximum imprisonment term. The actual sentence, if there is a conviction, is determined through the criminal process and applicable law.

“Every Section 132 offence is non-bailable.”

No. Section 132(4) establishes non-cognizable and bailable treatment as the general rule, subject to the specific exception in Section 132(5).

“Commissioner sanction means guilt is proved.”

No. Sanction is a statutory prerequisite for prosecution. It is not a judicial conviction.

“Old GST articles can be used without checking amendments.”

This is risky. The 2023 amendments removed clauses (g), (j) and (k) and changed other parts of Section 132. Always check the version applicable to the relevant date.

16. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 132 of the CGST Act?

It is the CGST Act provision prescribing criminal punishment for specified GST offences.

2. What is the maximum punishment under Section 132?

For the highest specified category involving an amount exceeding ₹5 crore, imprisonment may extend to five years and fine.

3. What is the punishment when the amount is above ₹2 crore but not above ₹5 crore?

Section 132(1)(ii) provides imprisonment that may extend to three years and fine.

4. What happens for an amount above ₹1 crore but not above ₹2 crore?

The current Section 132(1)(iii) specifically applies to an offence under clause (b) and provides imprisonment that may extend to one year and fine.

5. Are all GST offences under Section 132 cognizable and non-bailable?

No. The general rule is non-cognizable and bailable, subject to the specific high-value exception in Section 132(5).

6. Is Commissioner sanction required for prosecution?

Yes. Section 132(6) requires previous sanction of the Commissioner before prosecution under the section.

7. Does paying GST tax automatically prevent prosecution?

Payment can have consequences under specific provisions, but Section 132 prosecution must be analysed under the applicable offence and the statutory framework. Payment alone should not be assumed to answer every prosecution issue.

8. Does paying a penalty automatically close a Section 132 issue?

No automatic rule should be assumed. Section 131 itself states that confiscation or penalty does not prevent other punishment to which a person is liable.

9. What is a repeat offence under Section 132?

Section 132(2) applies where a person convicted under Section 132 is again convicted under Section 132. The second and subsequent offence can attract imprisonment up to five years and fine.

10. What is the minimum imprisonment mentioned in Section 132(3)?

For imprisonment under the specified clauses, the statutory term is not to be less than six months unless the Court records special and adequate reasons to the contrary in its judgment.

11. What does “tax” include for Section 132?

The Explanation includes tax evaded, ITC wrongly availed or utilised, or refund wrongly taken under the CGST, SGST, IGST and UTGST laws, as well as specified compensation cess.

12. Were GST offence clauses removed in 2023?

Yes. Clauses (g), (j) and (k) were omitted with effect from 1 October 2023, and the attempt/abetment and punishment provisions were correspondingly amended.

13. Is Section 132 applicable only to companies?

No. The wording applies to “whoever” commits the specified offence. Company-related liability also needs to be read with Section 137.

14. Does Section 132 itself decide guilt?

No. It defines offences and punishment. A conviction is determined through the applicable criminal process.

15. What should a company do if it receives a prosecution-related communication?

Identify the exact provision and alleged facts, preserve records, reconcile the amount involved, review the evidence and obtain appropriate professional legal advice.

16. Is a GSTR-2B mismatch automatically a Section 132 offence?

No. A reconciliation mismatch by itself does not establish the ingredients of a criminal offence. The underlying transaction, evidence and statutory requirements must be examined.

17. Can an employee's conduct create company-related GST prosecution risk?

Potentially, depending on the facts and the provisions applicable to companies and responsible persons. Section 137 should be read where a company is involved.

17. Quick Revision – Section 132

  • Section 132 = punishment for certain GST offences.
  • It is a criminal prosecution provision, not simply a routine penalty provision.
  • Current principal offence categories are (a)–(f), (h), (i) and (l).
  • Clauses (g), (j) and (k) were omitted effective 1 October 2023.
  • Highest threshold: amount exceeding ₹5 crore → imprisonment up to 5 years and fine.
  • More than ₹2 crore up to ₹5 crore → imprisonment up to 3 years and fine.
  • More than ₹1 crore up to ₹2 crore → current one-year category specifically concerns clause (b).
  • Section 132(3) contains a six-month minimum rule for specified imprisonment, subject to recorded special and adequate reasons to the contrary.
  • General rule: offences are non-cognizable and bailable.
  • Specified high-value offences under clauses (a)–(d) are cognizable and non-bailable under Section 132(5).
  • Previous sanction of the Commissioner is required before prosecution.
  • Repeat conviction can attract imprisonment up to five years and fine.
  • Always check the law applicable to the date of the alleged conduct.
