CGST Section 119 – Sums Due to Government Notwithstanding Appeal

Understand Section 119 of the CGST Act, 2017: why amounts due to the Government can remain payable even after an appeal is filed before the High Court or Supreme Court, how the provision connects with GST appellate orders, and what finance and tax teams should track.

Bare Act

119 Sums due to be paid notwithstanding appeal, etc.

Section 119: Notwithstanding that an appeal has been preferred to the High Court or the Supreme Court, sums due to the Government as a result of—

(1) an order passed by the National or Regional Benches of the Appellate Tribunal under sub-section (1) of section 113; or

(2) an order passed by the State Bench or Area Benches of the Appellate Tribunal under sub-section (1) of section 113; or

(3) an order passed by the High Court under section 117,

as the case may be, shall be payable in accordance with the order so passed.

Core message: Section 119 addresses the effect of a further appeal on amounts that have become due to the Government as a result of the specified appellate orders. Simply filing an appeal to the High Court or Supreme Court does not, by itself, change the payment position stated in the section.
Plain-language explanation

What does Section 119 mean in simple language?

Section 119 is a short provision, but it has an important practical effect for GST litigation. It deals with a situation where a GST appellate order has resulted in an amount becoming due to the Government and the aggrieved party then takes the matter further to the High Court or Supreme Court.

In simple terms: An appeal is a legal challenge to an order. Section 119 says that merely preferring the further appeal does not, by itself, make the amount due disappear or automatically suspend the payment obligation. The amount remains payable in accordance with the relevant order, subject to any separate stay or other applicable legal protection.

This distinction is important because businesses sometimes record a case as “appealed” and then assume that the underlying demand can simply be ignored until the litigation ends. Section 119 requires a more careful approach.

Scope of Section 119

When does Section 119 apply?

Section 119 refers to sums due to the Government resulting from specified orders of the Appellate Tribunal or the High Court.

Important distinction: Section 119 does not say that a party is prohibited from seeking a stay. It says that the mere fact that an appeal has been preferred does not, by itself, alter the payment position described in the provision.

Practical legal control

Section 119 and a stay order

Section 119 should be read as a payment-consequence provision. Whether recovery is protected in a particular case can depend on a separate order granting a stay, the wording of that order, statutory preconditions, deposits already made and the procedural stage of the dispute.

What should a company verify?

  • Exact amount stated as payable in the impugned order.
  • Whether any admitted amount has already been paid.
  • Whether statutory pre-deposit requirements were met at the relevant appellate stage.
  • Whether a separate stay application was filed.
  • Whether a stay order has actually been granted.
  • Exact wording and conditions of the stay order.
  • Whether the stay is time-bound or subject to the next hearing.
  • Whether any further payment is required despite the stay.
  • Whether the order has subsequently been modified, vacated or replaced.
  • Whether the GST electronic liability / recovery records require reconciliation with the court order.
Do not confuse pre-deposit with a complete stay of every amount. Different appellate stages have their own statutory payment/pre-deposit provisions. The tax team should identify exactly what has been paid, what remains disputed and what protection is available under the relevant order.
Practical examples

Section 119 examples for GST professionals

Example 1 – Tribunal order followed by High Court appeal

A company has a GST dispute. The Appellate Tribunal passes an order resulting in ₹80 lakh becoming payable to the Government. The company files an appeal before the High Court.

Section 119 relevance: The filing of the High Court appeal, by itself, does not change the statutory statement that sums due as a result of the specified Tribunal order remain payable in accordance with that order.

Example 2 – High Court order followed by Supreme Court appeal

The High Court decides a Section 117 appeal and the resulting order leaves ₹1.20 crore payable. The taxpayer seeks to take the matter to the Supreme Court.

Section 119 relevance: The provision specifically covers sums due as a result of an order passed by the High Court under Section 117, notwithstanding an appeal to the Supreme Court.

Example 3 – Appeal filed but no stay

A company files an appeal but has not obtained a separate stay. The accounts team marks the demand as “under appeal” and stops all internal monitoring.

Control issue: This is risky as an internal process. “Under appeal” and “recovery stayed” should be recorded separately.

Example 4 – Stay granted with conditions

A court grants interim protection subject to specified conditions. The company must record the exact conditions and ensure that the conditions are complied with. The litigation tracker should contain the order date, conditions, next hearing and expiry/review date.

Example 5 – Appeal ultimately succeeds

Suppose an amount is ultimately held not payable after further appellate proceedings and a refund becomes legally due. The finance/tax team should then separately examine the refund mechanism, applicable interest provisions and the effect of the final order. Section 119 itself is concerned with the payment position notwithstanding the further appeal; it is not a standalone refund provision.

Example 6 – Partial dispute

A company accepts ₹10 lakh of a demand but disputes ₹40 lakh. The internal litigation register should separately identify admitted and disputed components and record payments, pre-deposits and any stay orders rather than using one combined “appeal pending” figure.

Internal SOP

GST demand and appeal workflow under Section 119

Appellate order→ Calculate amount due→ Appeal decision→ Check stay→ Track payment/recovery→ Monitor final order

Step 1 – Capture the order

Save the complete appellate order in the litigation folder and record the date of receipt.

Step 2 – Reconcile the amount

Break the amount into tax, interest, penalty, fee and other components. Compare the order with the GST liability/recovery records and the company's books.

Step 3 – Record appeal status separately

Record whether an appeal has been filed and, separately, whether recovery/payment has been stayed.

Step 4 – Record every payment

Maintain challan/reference details, date, amount and the reason for payment. Distinguish statutory pre-deposit from voluntary or demand payments.

Step 5 – Monitor stay conditions

If protection is conditional, assign an owner for each condition and keep documentary evidence of compliance.

Step 6 – Reassess after every judicial order

Whenever the Tribunal, High Court or Supreme Court passes a further order, update the amount payable, stay status, accounting position and recovery status.

Internal audit point: “Appeal pending” should never be used as the only status field. A proper litigation register should separately capture the amount due, payment made, disputed amount, stay status and the latest binding order.
SEO-friendly legal explanation

Section 119 CGST Act: key points to remember

Section 119 of the CGST Act, 2017 deals with sums due to be paid notwithstanding appeal. It applies where an appeal has been preferred to the High Court or Supreme Court, but amounts are due to the Government as a result of specified orders of the Appellate Tribunal or a High Court order under Section 117.

The practical point is that filing a GST appeal does not, by itself, mean that every amount arising from the impugned order can be ignored. The taxpayer should separately determine the amount payable, the statutory payments already made, whether a stay has been granted and what conditions apply to that stay.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions on Section 119 of the CGST Act

1. What is Section 119 of the CGST Act?

Section 119 deals with sums due to the Government notwithstanding that an appeal has been preferred to the High Court or Supreme Court. It applies to the specified Tribunal and High Court orders identified in the section.

2. Does filing a GST appeal automatically stop recovery?

Section 119 states that sums due remain payable notwithstanding that an appeal has been preferred. A taxpayer should therefore not treat the mere filing of an appeal as an automatic stay.

3. What does “notwithstanding appeal” mean in Section 119?

It means that the payment position described by the provision is not automatically displaced merely because a further appeal has been filed.

4. Does Section 119 prevent a taxpayer from obtaining a stay?

The provision does not itself say that a taxpayer cannot seek or obtain separate judicial protection. The actual effect of a stay depends on the competent forum's order, its wording and applicable law.

5. Does Section 119 apply to a High Court order?

Yes. Section 119 specifically refers to sums due as a result of an order passed by the High Court under Section 117.

6. Does Section 119 apply when an appeal is filed before the Supreme Court?

Yes. The opening words of Section 119 expressly refer to an appeal preferred to the High Court or the Supreme Court.

7. Does “appeal pending” mean the GST demand should be removed from the books?

No. Appeal status and accounting treatment are separate questions. Finance should assess the matter under the applicable accounting framework, facts and legal position rather than automatically removing the exposure.

8. Is a statutory pre-deposit the same as a complete stay?

Not necessarily. The tax team should identify exactly what amount has been paid as pre-deposit, what remains payable and what recovery protection exists under the relevant statutory provision or judicial order.

9. What should a company maintain for a Section 119 dispute?

Maintain the appellate order, demand reconciliation, payment records, appeal filing proof, stay application/order, conditions of stay, next hearing and updated financial exposure.

10. Why is Section 119 important for finance teams?

Because a pending appeal can still have cash-flow, recovery, accounting, disclosure and compliance implications. A litigation register should therefore distinguish an appeal from an actual stay.
