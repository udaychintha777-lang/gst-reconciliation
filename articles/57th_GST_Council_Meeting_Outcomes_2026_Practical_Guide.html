1. Quick summary of the 57th GST Council meeting

The 57th GST Council meeting took place in New Delhi on 8 October 2026. According to the official press release, the meeting concentrated mainly on process reforms and clarifications—particularly GST registration, returns, refunds, adjudication, ITC reporting and movement of goods—rather than another broad rate-rationalisation exercise.

Registration

Recommended clearer documentation, a more guided REG-01 experience, simplified amendments and a more automated cancellation process in specified cases.

ITC & returns

Recommended improved reporting and correction mechanisms to align GSTR-3B credit reporting with GSTR-2B and related portal statements.

Refunds

Recommended risk-based automation, including proposed provisional processing of 90% of eligible zero-rated and inverted-duty-structure refund claims in Phase 1.

Enforcement

Recommended changes to arrest/prosecution provisions, e-way bill interception and a chance to object to certain Rule 86A credit blocks.

Exports

Recommended changes affecting services provided to/through foreign branches and certain supplies delivered to SEZ/FTWZ for overseas buyers.

Small taxpayers

Approved in principle a concept note for an optional Annual Return Quarterly Payment (ARQP) scheme for specified small B2C taxpayers.

How to read this article: These are the Council’s recommendations as reported in the official press release. A recommendation is not, by itself, enough to change a return, claim a refund or disregard an existing compliance requirement. Check the relevant notification, statutory amendment, circular, commencement date and GST portal functionality before acting.

2. The most important point: recommendation versus law

The GST Council press release itself states that the recommendations will be given effect through relevant circulars, notifications or amendments to law, and that those formal instruments alone will have the force of law.

Wording in an updateWhat it means for a taxpayerPractical response
“Recommended amendment”A legal change is proposed, but the amendment and commencement must be checked.Do not change the tax position solely on the meeting headline.
“Circular to be issued”Clarification is expected on interpretation or administrative practice.Continue to document the existing position and review the circular when issued.
“Approved in principle”The concept has support but design and implementation may remain open.Do not assume the scheme is available for immediate election or filing.
“Phase 1 / Phase 2”Implementation is planned in stages.Track commencement dates, eligibility and portal changes separately.
Compliance caution: This guide describes the 57th meeting’s published recommendations, not a blanket statement that every item is already effective. Before filing a return, adjusting ITC, charging a different rate or claiming a refund, verify the latest applicable official notification/circular and its effective date.

3. GST registration, amendments and cancellation

3.1 Clearer documents and a guided registration application

The Council recommended a comprehensive circular and FAQs on documents and information required for registration, changes to Form GST REG-01 using prescribed-document selections, and a more user-friendly portal with dropdowns and contextual guidance. The aim is to reduce avoidable queries and rejections caused by incomplete or inconsistent applications.

Practical exampleA new engineering consultancy applies for GST registration. Its rent agreement names the landlord, while the electricity bill carries the building owner’s name. Under a clearer checklist, the applicant can identify which address-proof and consent documents are required before submission rather than receiving repeated deficiency notices.

What a business should do now: maintain a standard registration file containing constitution documents, PAN, proof of principal place of business, consent/lease documents, electricity or property evidence where applicable, bank details when required, authorised-signatory proof and consistent address details. The exact documents must follow the operative rules and portal requirements.

3.2 Automatic acceptance of specified registration amendments

The Council recommended amendment of Rule 19 to provide automatic acceptance of amendments to registration particulars, except principal place of business, for the general category; for taxpayers under the Rule 14A route, the recommendation extends automatic acceptance to all particulars, including principal place of business. The recommendation must be read with the final rule and its effective date.

Example: A company changes its contact email and authorised signatory. A more automated amendment process could reduce officer interaction. A change in the actual business premises still requires careful evidence and should not be treated as approved merely because an application was submitted.

3.3 Cancellation process

The Council recommended a phased system-based cancellation process. In Phase 1, specified applications may be automatically accepted once pending returns are filed and dues paid, including defined cases linked to the amount of ITC passed on and filing of the final return. Phase 2 envisages broader automatic processing, subject to the final framework.

Practical control: Before applying for cancellation, reconcile all GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B returns, outstanding tax/interest/late fee, electronic cash and credit ledgers, stock/capital goods implications where relevant, and the final return requirement. A “system-based” process does not mean pending liabilities disappear.

4. GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, IMS and ITC reporting

The Council recommended changes to improve how liability and ITC are corrected and reported, including an alternate mechanism for amending liability and ITC in returns, improvements linked to IMS, an Electronic Credit Reversal and Reclaim Statement, and alignment of ITC reported in GSTR-3B with credit made available through GSTR-2B and related statements. The press release indicated a proposed start from the return period of April 2027, subject to the required implementation and consultation.

4.1 Why this matters

Many mismatches arise because a purchase invoice appears in a later GSTR-2B, a supplier amends an invoice, a credit note is actioned incorrectly in IMS, or the recipient reclaims credit without preserving the original reversal trail. A structured correction mechanism is intended to make the reporting trail more consistent and reduce avoidable mismatch notices.

4.2 Worked example: invoice appears late

4.3 Recommended month-end controls

  • Reconcile purchase register to GSTR-2B using supplier GSTIN, invoice number, date and tax amounts.
  • Keep separate exception categories: missing in 2B, value mismatch, GSTIN mismatch, duplicate invoice, credit note, amendment, RCM and ineligible ITC.
  • Record why an invoice was accepted, rejected or kept pending in IMS where those options are applicable.
  • Maintain evidence for each reversal and later reclaim; avoid claiming the same credit twice.
  • Have a reviewer sign off the GSTR-3B ITC working before filing.

5. Faster, system-based GST refunds

The Council recommended amendments to Section 54 and related rules to automate refund processing in phases for excess cash-ledger balances, zero-rated supplies and inverted duty structure (IDS) claims.

5.1 Proposed Phase 1 measures

  • Full refund of eligible excess balance in the electronic cash ledger through system-based sanction without officer intervention.
  • Reduce the period for issuing an acknowledgement or deficiency memo from 15 days to 10 days, with a proposed deemed-acknowledgement mechanism if neither is issued in that period.
  • For zero-rated and inverted-duty-structure refund claims, proposed automatic provisional sanction of 90% of the claimed amount, based on system risk assessment.

5.2 Proposed Phase 2

Phase 2 envisages automated acknowledgement after system verification and automated sanction of the full eligible zero-rated refund in qualifying cases, after adjustment of pending dues and risk evaluation.

5.3 Numerical example: provisional refund

Suppose an exporter files an eligible refund claim of ₹20,00,000. If the proposed 90% provisional mechanism is brought into force and the claim qualifies under the notified risk-based process, the provisional amount could be ₹18,00,000 (₹20,00,000 × 90%). The balance ₹2,00,000 would remain subject to final processing. This is an illustration of the recommendation, not a guarantee that 90% is currently payable on every refund application.

Preparation tip: Keep invoice-level export data, shipping bills/LUT records as relevant, foreign remittance evidence, GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B reconciliations, electronic credit ledger data, turnover calculations and a clear explanation of any difference between books and returns.

5.4 Other refund-related recommendations

  • Make RFD-01 information more system-readable and potentially reduce scanned-document uploads for specified zero-rated and IDS claims.
  • Remove the proposed cap linking zero-rated goods turnover to 1.5 times the value of like domestic goods under the specified refund formula.
  • Clarify the ₹1,000 minimum threshold by considering the aggregate refund under CGST, SGST/UTGST and IGST together.
  • Clarify interest treatment for refund of appeal pre-deposits.

Each item requires checking the final amendment, formula, eligibility and effective date before use.

6. GST notices, adjudication and dispute resolution

The Council recommended a comprehensive circular for officers on the quality and timing of demand notices and orders, proper invocation of fraud/suppression allegations based on the facts of each case, and observance of natural justice—including personal hearings.

6.1 Proposed minimum threshold for show-cause notices

The Council recommended a ₹10,000 minimum tax-amount threshold (CGST + SGST/IGST + cess) for issuing notices under the specified demand provisions, with transitional treatment proposed for certain pending notices and appeals below the threshold. This should not be interpreted as permission to ignore a notice already issued or to assume every kind of GST proceeding is covered.

6.2 Example: mismatch notice for a small amount

A business receives a proposed demand based on an ITC mismatch of ₹7,500. Under the recommendation, the relevant demand provisions would be amended to introduce a minimum threshold. The taxpayer should still check the legal provision cited, the nature of the proceeding, whether the amendment has commenced, and whether any transitional clause applies. Until that review is done, do not discard the notice or miss its reply deadline.

6.3 How to review a notice professionally

  1. Record the GSTIN, tax period, section, form number, date of service and response deadline.
  2. Identify whether the issue is a return mismatch, classification/rate dispute, ITC eligibility, tax short-payment, late filing or alleged fraud/suppression.
  3. Reconcile the allegation to filed returns, ledgers, invoices, e-way bills, contracts and prior correspondence.
  4. Prepare a point-by-point response with an evidence index and calculation annexures.
  5. Request a personal hearing where appropriate and retain proof of submission.
  6. Track the order and appeal limitation separately; a reply does not automatically stop limitation periods.
Do not assume the ₹10,000 threshold is a universal immunity. Verify the final text and scope of the amendment, the tax components counted, transitional treatment and whether the proceeding is within the specified sections.

7. Export of services and cross-border supplies

The Council recommended changes to the IGST framework aimed at facilitating certain service exports, including removal of the condition that the supplier and recipient must not be establishments of the same person, clarification on permitted receipt of export proceeds, and changes to the place-of-supply rule for certain services where the recipient makes goods physically available to the supplier.

7.1 Services supplied to or through a foreign branch

Illustrative case: An Indian company provides centralised support to its overseas branch. The company currently needs to test the export-of-services conditions carefully, including the distinct-person condition. The recommended change would remove that condition for qualifying services, potentially facilitating refund access. Until the amendment is operative, the business must not assume the new treatment applies retrospectively or immediately.

7.2 Payment in foreign currency or permitted Indian rupees

The Council recommended a circular to clarify that export proceeds may be received in foreign exchange or in Indian rupees where permitted under RBI rules. For example, if a service exporter receives payment through a permitted INR settlement route, it should preserve bank advice, contract, invoice, remittance/settlement evidence and the applicable RBI permission or framework.

7.3 Place of supply when customer-owned goods are made available

The recommendation would shift specified cases from the special rule for goods physically made available to the default recipient-location rule under Section 13(2) of the IGST Act. This may affect whether a service supplied to a foreign recipient can qualify as export, but only after applying all export conditions and the final statutory wording.

7.4 Delivery to SEZ/FTWZ for an overseas buyer

The Council recommended a clarification for specified goods sold to an overseas buyer but delivered to an SEZ or Free Trade and Warehousing Zone for warehousing or further processing, subject to the recommended statutory conditions and receipt of payment in convertible foreign exchange or Indian rupees where permitted. Manufacturers should retain the overseas purchase order, delivery instructions, SEZ/FTWZ documents, proof of receipt and payment records.

8. Arrest powers and prosecution threshold

The Council recommended omission of Section 69 of the CGST Act to withdraw GST arrest powers, and recommended raising the prosecution monetary threshold from ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore. It also recommended narrowing specified offence wording and rationalising punishment for certain offences under Section 132.

What this does—and does not—meanThe recommendations signal a proposed shift in the enforcement framework. They should not be read as a present licence to disregard summons, investigations, document requests, tax demands or existing statutory duties. Formal law changes and commencement must be verified.

Practical example

A company is under investigation regarding alleged wrongful ITC of ₹3 crore. A headline about the recommended ₹5 crore prosecution threshold does not, by itself, decide the case. The company must establish the nature of the allegation, the applicable offence, the relevant period, evidence of receipt of goods/services, invoices, payment trail and the law in force for that proceeding. Tax demand, interest, penalty and prosecution are distinct issues and must be analysed separately.

Finance-team controls

  • Preserve purchase orders, tax invoices, e-way bills, goods-receipt notes, transport documents and bank-payment evidence.
  • Maintain vendor onboarding and GSTIN verification controls.
  • Do not create, alter or backdate records after an investigation begins.
  • Escalate summons and investigation communications promptly to authorised management and professional advisers.

9. E-way bills, transit inspections and detention

The Council recommended amendments to Sections 68, 129 and 130 intended to make interception more intelligence-led and reduce unnecessary disruption during transit.

  • A vehicle carrying goods could be intercepted based on specific intelligence and authorisation from an officer not below Joint Commissioner rank.
  • Inspection and further action would generally be linked to the supplier or recipient being located/registered in the intercepting State, rather than a transit State.
  • Where no e-way bill has been generated or the conveyance carries no document showing origin/destination, inspection, detention or seizure could still occur irrespective of jurisdiction.
  • The Council recommended that confiscation under Section 130 should not apply to goods/conveyances in transit.

Example: Hyderabad to Bengaluru through another State

A registered supplier dispatches goods from Hyderabad to a registered customer in Bengaluru. The truck passes through a third State. Under the proposed framework, ordinary transit through that State would not by itself be the intended basis for interception; however, missing mandatory documents or no e-way bill may trigger the stated exception. Until the amendment is effective, transporters must follow current law and carry all required documents.

Dispatch checklist

Before dispatch
  • Correct tax invoice/delivery challan
  • Valid e-way bill, where required
  • Correct GSTIN, place of supply and addresses
  • Vehicle details and Part B updates, as applicable
During transit
  • Keep invoice and e-way bill accessible
  • Retain transporter/vehicle change records
  • Document breakdown or route deviation
  • Escalate detention orders immediately

10. Rule 86A: opportunity to object before ITC remains blocked

The Council recommended an amendment to Rule 86A to allow a taxpayer to submit an objection against an amount blocked in the electronic credit ledger and to receive a personal hearing before the proper officer decides the objection.

Example: credit ledger block disrupts working capital

A contractor has ₹12 lakh in its electronic credit ledger, but ₹5 lakh is blocked under Rule 86A. The company believes the invoices are genuine and has purchase orders, tax invoices, e-way bills, goods-receipt notes and payment evidence. Under the recommended process, it would have a defined opportunity to object and seek a personal hearing, subject to the final rule.

The objection file should clearly state the amount blocked, order/communication details, invoice-wise facts, why the statutory conditions are not met, supporting documents and the practical impact. A taxpayer should not assume that filing an objection automatically releases the credit; await the decision under the operative procedure.

11. Small taxpayers: late-fee relief and the proposed ARQP concept

11.1 Recommended late-fee waiver condition

The Council recommended waiver of late fee for delayed returns under Section 39(1) for taxpayers whose aggregate turnover in the preceding financial year is up to ₹5 crore, if the delayed return is filed by the end of the month in which it was due. The scope, relevant return periods and effective date depend on the final notification.

Example: A qualifying small taxpayer’s return is due on the 20th of a month. The taxpayer files it on the 28th of that same month. If the final notification covers that return and the taxpayer satisfies all conditions, the recommended waiver may apply. Filing in a later month, or assuming the waiver applies to interest/tax as well as late fee, could produce a different result. The recommendation concerns late fee; it does not automatically waive tax or interest.

11.2 Annual Return Quarterly Payment (ARQP)

The Council approved in principle a concept note for an optional ARQP scheme for taxpayers with aggregate turnover up to ₹5 crore in the preceding financial year who make supplies exclusively to unregistered persons (B2C). It remains a concept for further consideration, not an immediately available scheme merely because it was approved in principle.

Example: A small consumer-facing service provider with ₹3.8 crore preceding-year turnover and only B2C supplies may appear to fit the broad concept. It should not change its return/payment frequency until the final eligibility criteria, return structure, payment deadlines and portal process are notified.

15. Frequently asked questions

When was the 57th GST Council meeting held?

It was held on 8 October 2026 in New Delhi.

Are all 57th Council meeting recommendations effective immediately?

No. The official release says the recommendations will be implemented through relevant circulars, notifications or law amendments. Check the formal instrument and commencement date for each measure.

Does the proposed 90% refund mean every exporter gets 90% automatically?

No. It is a recommended risk-based provisional mechanism for specified eligible zero-rated and inverted-duty-structure refund claims, subject to implementation, eligibility and system evaluation.

Can a taxpayer ignore a GST notice below ₹10,000?

No. The Council recommended a minimum threshold for specified demand notices and related transitional treatment. Do not ignore a notice without verifying the final provision, scope, effective date and response deadline.

Is the ₹5 crore prosecution threshold already applicable?

The Council recommended raising the threshold from ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore. Check the enacted amendment and commencement date before applying the change to a case.

Can a small B2C business start using ARQP now?

Not on the basis of the in-principle concept note alone. Wait for the final scheme design, eligibility, commencement and portal process.

What should a taxpayer do first?

Identify which recommendations affect the business, track the official legal instruments, preserve supporting records and change return or ERP processes only after confirming the operative law.
