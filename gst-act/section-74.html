GST ACT — SECTION 74

Section 74 CGST Act — GST Demand for Fraud, Wilful Misstatement & Suppression

Section 74 is the historical GST demand provision for tax not paid or short paid, erroneous refunds and input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised where the department alleges fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. This detailed guide explains the allegation standard, show cause notice, voluntary payment, penalty, limitation, adjudication and the transition from Section 74 to Section 74A.

1. Why Section 74 matters

Searches such as “Section 74 CGST Act”, “Section 74 GST”, “GST fraud demand”, “GST suppression of facts”, “GST wilful misstatement”, “Section 74 GST notice”, “GST penalty under Section 74”, “DRC-01 Section 74” and “Section 74 vs Section 73” are directly connected with this provision.

Section 74 is one of the most serious historical GST demand provisions because it is not merely about finding a tax difference. The department must proceed on the statutory basis involving fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.

Simple idea: A tax difference and a fraud allegation are not the same thing. Section 74 adds a specific statutory allegation that materially changes the demand and penalty framework.

2. Section 74 in very simple language

Imagine the department finds that a business did not pay ₹10 lakh of GST. Under a normal error case, the non-fraud demand framework may be relevant. But if the department alleges that the business deliberately concealed facts or made a wilful misstatement to evade tax, the historical Section 74 framework can become relevant.

Tax issue → allegation of fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression → SCN → reply/hearing → adjudication → demand/penalty/appeal

The allegation must be examined carefully. The mere existence of a tax shortfall does not by itself prove fraud or suppression.

3. What Section 74 covers

The historical Section 74 framework covers situations where it appears to the proper officer that:

  • tax has not been paid;
  • tax has been short paid;
  • tax has been erroneously refunded; or
  • input tax credit has been wrongly availed or utilised;

and the relevant circumstances involve fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.

The section then provides special rules for voluntary payment, notice, penalty, adjudication and limitation.

4. What fraud, wilful misstatement and suppression mean

ExpressionPractical meaning
FraudDeliberate deception or dishonest conduct intended to obtain an unlawful tax benefit or evade tax.
Wilful misstatementA deliberate/intentional incorrect statement rather than an innocent mistake.
Suppression of factsDeliberate non-disclosure of relevant information where the statutory ingredients for suppression are established.
These are legal allegations that must be supported by facts and evidence. A reconciliation difference, accounting error or interpretational dispute should not automatically be labelled fraud.

5. Four types of demand

Tax not paid
Tax liability exists but was not discharged.
Tax short paid
Only part of the tax liability was paid.
Erroneous refund
Refund was granted in excess of legal entitlement.
Wrong ITC
ITC was wrongly availed or utilised.

The additional Section 74 element is the alleged fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.

6. Payment before show cause notice

Section 74 contains a statutory mechanism allowing payment before service of the notice, consisting of tax, applicable interest and a specified penalty, subject to the conditions and payment stage prescribed in the section.

Where the statutory conditions are satisfied, payment before notice can avoid issuance of notice for the amount so paid.

Because the penalty under Section 74 is materially different from Section 73, the timing of payment can be financially significant.

7. Show cause notice under Section 74

A Section 74 show cause notice is not simply a statement saying “tax is payable”. It should be examined for the factual and legal basis of the fraud/suppression allegation.

The taxpayer should specifically analyse:

  • the exact tax period;
  • the disputed transaction;
  • the tax calculation;
  • the alleged conduct;
  • the evidence relied upon;
  • the connection between the conduct and tax shortfall;
  • interest;
  • penalty;
  • limitation; and
  • documents supporting the department's conclusion.

8. Reply and personal hearing

A Section 74 reply should deal with both the tax issue and the fraud/suppression allegation.

Recommended structure

  • brief facts and chronology;
  • issue-wise response;
  • tax calculation reconciliation;
  • explanation of accounting/tax treatment;
  • evidence showing actual transactions;
  • response to each fraud/suppression allegation;
  • limitation analysis;
  • interest calculation;
  • penalty objection/position; and
  • specific relief requested.

Where a personal hearing is requested in accordance with the applicable statutory framework, the adjudicating authority must provide the hearing required by law.

9. Statement for other periods

Section 74 historically contains a mechanism for issuing a statement for other periods where the same grounds as the notice apply, subject to statutory conditions.

This means a taxpayer should not analyse a notice only invoice-by-invoice. If the department alleges a continuing practice, the taxpayer should review the complete period-wise facts and the limitation applicable to each period.

10. Adjudication order and limitation

Under the historical Section 74 framework, the department had a longer limitation period than under Section 73 because of the seriousness of the fraud/suppression allegation.

The historical structure generally provided that the order had to be issued within five years from the due date for furnishing the annual return for the relevant financial year, subject to the statutory rules on notice and exclusion of certain periods.

The notice had to be issued at least six months before the deadline for the order.

Current-law warning: Finance Act 2024 restricted Sections 73 and 74 to determination of tax pertaining to periods up to FY 2023-24 and introduced Section 74A for FY 2024-25 onward. Therefore, the tax period must be established before applying the historical five-year Section 74 limitation.

11. Penalty under Section 74

The historical Section 74 penalty is significantly more severe than the ordinary Section 73 framework.

Under the historical structure, the basic penalty was generally equivalent to the tax, subject to reduced-penalty opportunities where payment was made at specified stages.

StageHistorical consequence
Before noticePayment of tax + applicable interest + specified penalty can have a statutory settlement effect.
Within prescribed period after noticeA reduced penalty can apply if the statutory payment conditions are satisfied.
Within prescribed period after orderThe law historically allowed a further reduced penalty where the full tax, interest and applicable reduced penalty were paid within the specified period.
After the statutory reduced-payment windowsThe full penalty consequence can apply subject to the order and applicable law.

12. Interest under Section 50

Interest remains separate from the penalty. Section 74 requires interest under Section 50 along with the tax in the relevant payment provisions.

The calculation should identify:

  • tax amount;
  • date tax became payable;
  • date of payment;
  • payments already made;
  • applicable interest rate; and
  • Rule 88B calculation method.

Do not combine interest and penalty into one unexplained figure.

13. DRC-01, DRC-03 & DRC-07

FormCommon role
DRC-01Electronic summary of the show cause notice.
DRC-03Intimation/payment of tax, interest, penalty or other amounts in prescribed circumstances.
DRC-07Electronic summary of the adjudication order creating the demand.

These forms communicate the proceeding but do not eliminate the need to examine the underlying Section 74 notice, evidence and statutory provisions.

14. Section 74 vs Section 73

Section 73Section 74
Historical non-fraud framework.Historical fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression framework.
Lower penalty structure.Higher penalty structure.
Historical three-year order limitation.Historical five-year order limitation.
No fraud/suppression allegation required.Fraud/wilful misstatement/suppression to evade tax is the defining additional element.
The difference is not simply “small demand versus large demand”. The legal character of the conduct is central.

15. Section 74 vs Section 74A

This is one of the most important current-law points.

The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 amended Section 74 so that its provisions apply to determination of tax pertaining to periods up to FY 2023-24. It then inserted Section 74A for determination relating to FY 2024-25 onward. The official 2024 legislative text expressly records this transition. citeturn0search12

Tax periodPrimary demand framework
Up to FY 2023-24Sections 73/74 remain relevant depending on whether the case is non-fraud or fraud/suppression.
FY 2024-25 onwardSection 74A is the principal determination framework; the specific statutory penalty and notice provisions under Section 74A must be analysed.
SEO/legal accuracy: A page ranking for “Section 74 GST” must clearly explain this transition rather than presenting Section 74 as the universal current demand section.

16. Section 128A and Section 74

Section 128A is specifically connected with certain Section 73 demands for FY 2017-18, FY 2018-19 and FY 2019-20. It is therefore important not to describe Section 128A as a general waiver for Section 74 demands.

The GST Council recommended the procedure and Rule 164 framework for Section 128A, and the provision was brought into force from 1 November 2024. citeturn0search14

Important: If the demand is a Section 74 demand, Section 128A cannot simply be assumed to waive its interest/penalty. The statutory eligibility must be checked.

17. Why evidence matters

Because Section 74 involves a serious allegation, evidence is particularly important.

For example, if the department alleges suppression of sales, the taxpayer should examine:

  • sales registers;
  • invoices;
  • GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B;
  • e-invoice data;
  • e-way bills;
  • bank receipts;
  • customer ledgers;
  • contract records;
  • accounting entries; and
  • correspondence explaining the transaction.

The taxpayer should answer the allegation with evidence rather than only making a general denial.

18. Detailed practical examples

Example 1 — Deliberately omitted turnover allegation

The department alleges that sales were intentionally omitted from GST returns. The business should reconcile invoices, e-invoices, e-way bills, books and bank receipts and specifically answer the alleged suppression.

Example 2 — Wrong ITC with alleged concealment

The department alleges that ineligible ITC was deliberately claimed. The taxpayer should establish invoice-level facts, receipt of goods/services, eligibility and the actual accounting process.

Example 3 — Classification dispute

A business applied a lower GST rate based on its interpretation of a notification. A classification dispute does not automatically establish fraud; the factual and legal basis must be examined.

Example 4 — Erroneous refund

A refund was granted on the basis of information later found incorrect. The department may examine whether the statutory ingredients for Section 74 are actually established.

Example 5 — RCM omission

A business missed reverse-charge tax for several months. The existence of an omission alone does not answer the separate question whether fraud/suppression to evade tax is established.

Example 6 — Construction billing

A contractor's RA bills were reported differently in books and GST returns. The taxpayer should establish the billing chronology, tax invoices, contract terms and accounting reconciliation.

Example 7 — Related-party valuation

The department alleges that related-party transactions were intentionally undervalued. Agreements, valuation workings and Rule 28 analysis become critical evidence.

Example 8 — Cash sales allegation

The department alleges suppressed cash sales. Bank, cash book, stock movement and invoice records should be reconciled transaction by transaction.

Example 9 — Multiple periods

An alleged practice spans several years. Each year's facts and limitation should be separately analysed rather than accepting one common calculation.

Example 10 — Voluntary payment

During investigation, a business identifies a genuine tax issue. Before making payment, it should calculate tax, interest and the statutory penalty consequences and document the basis for payment.

19. Construction and infrastructure examples

RA bill suppression allegation

Reconcile measurement books, RA bills, tax invoices, GSTR-1, GSTR-3B and customer books to determine whether any difference is an actual taxable supply omission.

Mobilisation advance

Trace advances, receipts, invoices and adjustments before accepting an allegation of concealed turnover.

Subcontractor ITC

Maintain agreements, work orders, RA bills, invoices, payment records, GST returns and ITC reconciliation.

Material purchases

Match purchase invoices, GRNs, e-way bills, site consumption and payment records.

Multiple GST registrations

Prepare GSTIN-wise turnover and ITC reconciliations so that transactions between registrations are not incorrectly treated as suppressed external supplies.

21. Practical safeguards

  • Identify the financial year and tax period first.
  • Check whether Section 74 legally applies to that period.
  • Read the exact fraud/suppression allegation.
  • Demand evidence supporting the allegation.
  • Reconcile the tax calculation independently.
  • Check limitation separately.
  • Separate tax, interest and penalty.
  • Do not make a voluntary payment without understanding its statutory consequences.
  • Use the personal hearing opportunity effectively.
  • Take specialist legal advice where the notice contains serious fraud/criminal allegations.

24. Common mistakes

  • Assuming every tax difference is fraud.
  • Ignoring the exact fraud/suppression allegation.
  • Applying Section 74 to FY 2024-25 onward without checking Section 74A.
  • Accepting a five-year limitation calculation without verifying the relevant period.
  • Responding only to the tax amount and not the fraud allegation.
  • Failing to provide transaction evidence.
  • Ignoring interest and penalty calculations.
  • Making payment without analysing the statutory stage.
  • Confusing DRC-01 with the complete legal notice.
  • Missing personal hearing or appeal deadlines.

26. Section 74 GST FAQs

What is Section 74 of the CGST Act?

Section 74 is the historical demand provision for tax not paid/short paid, erroneous refund or wrongly availed/utilised ITC where fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax is involved.

What is a Section 74 GST notice?

It is a show cause notice proposing recovery of tax, interest and applicable penalty under the Section 74 framework and setting out the department's allegations.

What is the difference between Section 73 and 74?

Section 73 historically deals with non-fraud cases, while Section 74 historically applies where fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax is alleged and established under the statutory framework.

Does a tax mismatch automatically mean Section 74?

No. A mismatch or tax shortfall does not by itself establish fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression.

What is the penalty under Section 74?

The historical Section 74 framework generally provides a penalty equal to the tax, with reduced-penalty opportunities at specified payment stages subject to statutory conditions.

What was the Section 74 limitation period?

Historically, the order was generally required within five years from the due date for the annual return for the relevant financial year, with the notice required at least six months before the order deadline, subject to statutory exclusions.

Does Section 74 apply to FY 2024-25?

Section 74 was restricted by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 to determination of tax pertaining to periods up to FY 2023-24. Section 74A is the newer framework for FY 2024-25 onward.

Can Section 74 be challenged?

Yes. A taxpayer can contest the tax calculation, fraud/suppression allegation, evidence, limitation, interest, penalty and other legal grounds through the statutory adjudication and appeal process.

Can tax be paid before a Section 74 notice?

Yes. Section 74 contains a voluntary-payment mechanism with tax, interest and specified penalty consequences, subject to the statutory conditions and stage.

What is DRC-01 under Section 74?

DRC-01 is the electronic summary associated with the show cause notice in the GST demand framework.

Is Section 128A available for Section 74 demands?

Section 128A is specifically designed for certain Section 73 demands for FY 2017-18 to FY 2019-20. It should not be treated as a general waiver for Section 74 demands.

What should I do after receiving a Section 74 notice?

First identify the tax period, read the fraud/suppression allegation, reconcile the demand with your records, collect evidence, check limitation and obtain professional advice where the allegation has serious legal or criminal implications.

28. Key takeaways

  • Section 74 is the historical GST demand framework involving fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax.
  • A tax mismatch by itself does not prove fraud.
  • The department's allegation and supporting evidence should be examined carefully.
  • Section 74 historically carried a higher penalty and longer limitation period than Section 73.
  • Payment timing can materially affect penalty consequences.
  • Tax, interest and penalty should be calculated separately.
  • Sections 73 and 74 now have a period-based historical role because Section 74A was introduced for FY 2024-25 onward.
  • Section 128A is a special relief for specified Section 73 demands, not a general Section 74 waiver.
  • A strong response should address both the tax calculation and the fraud/suppression allegation.
  • For a live Section 74 notice, obtain professional legal advice before making admissions or payments.
