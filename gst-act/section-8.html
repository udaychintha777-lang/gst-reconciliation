Section 8 — Tax Liability on Composite and Mixed Supplies

Section 8 provides the rate-and-treatment mechanism where a transaction contains two or more supplies bundled together. The central distinction is between a composite supply, where the principal supply determines the treatment, and a mixed supply, where the supply carrying the highest applicable rate determines the treatment.

1. Why Section 8 matters

Commercial transactions frequently contain more than one element. A seller may supply goods together with packing, transportation and insurance. A business may sell a festival package containing several independent products for one price. A contractor may provide equipment together with installation and supervision.

The existence of multiple elements does not automatically mean that each element must be taxed independently. Section 8 provides a statutory mechanism for determining the GST treatment of a qualifying composite supply or mixed supply.

The practical sequence is important: identify the individual supplies → determine whether they are naturally bundled → identify the principal supply if composite → otherwise test whether the bundle is a mixed supply → apply the Section 8 tax rule.

2. Statutory text — Section 8

8. The tax liability on a composite or a mixed supply shall be determined in the following manner, namely:—

(a) a composite supply comprising two or more supplies, one of which is a principal supply, shall be treated as a supply of such principal supply; and

(b) a mixed supply comprising two or more supplies shall be treated as a supply of that particular supply which attracts the highest rate of tax.

Key point: Section 8 does not itself define composite supply or mixed supply. Those definitions are contained in Section 2(30) and Section 2(74). Therefore, Section 8 must always be read together with those definitions.

4. Composite supply — Section 2(30)

Section 2(30) defines composite supply as a supply made by a taxable person to a recipient consisting of two or more taxable supplies of goods or services or both, or any combination thereof, which are naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction with each other in the ordinary course of business, one of which is a principal supply.

The definition therefore contains several cumulative concepts:

  1. There must be two or more taxable supplies.
  2. The supplies must be naturally bundled.
  3. They must be supplied in conjunction with one another.
  4. The arrangement should be one ordinarily made in that manner in business.
  5. One component must be identifiable as the principal supply.

4.1 Natural bundling

Natural bundling is the heart of the composite-supply test. The question is not merely whether the supplier has put multiple items on one invoice. The issue is whether the components are commercially and functionally supplied together as a normal package.

Useful indicators include the nature of the customer expectation, the ordinary practice of the industry, whether the components are dependent on one another, the manner in which the package is advertised, and whether one element is clearly the dominant purpose of the transaction.

A single invoice is not enough to establish a composite supply. Likewise, separate prices on an invoice do not automatically prevent a composite supply. The substance and natural commercial arrangement have to be examined.

4.2 Principal supply

“Principal supply” means the supply which constitutes the predominant element of a composite supply and to which any other supply forming part of that composite supply is ancillary.

Once the principal supply is identified, Section 8(a) treats the entire composite supply as a supply of that principal supply for tax treatment.

5. The classic statutory illustration — goods with packing, transport and insurance

The statutory illustration for composite supply is particularly useful. Where goods are packed and transported with insurance, the supply of goods, packing materials, transport and insurance are treated as a composite supply and the supply of goods is the principal supply.

Accordingly, Section 8(a) applies and the bundle is treated as a supply of the principal supply — the goods.

Practical example: A machinery supplier quotes ₹10,00,000 for machinery including packing, transportation to the customer's site and transit insurance. If those elements are naturally bundled in the ordinary course and the machinery is the predominant supply, the transaction is analysed as a composite supply with machinery as the principal supply.

6. Composite supply in real business arrangements

6.1 Goods + installation

Supplying specialised equipment together with installation can be composite in appropriate circumstances. However, the conclusion depends on the contractual and commercial facts. If installation is merely ancillary to the equipment, the equipment may be the principal supply. If the arrangement is fundamentally a service involving substantial design, installation and commissioning, a different classification analysis may be necessary.

6.2 Training + accommodation

Training or coaching provided together with accommodation is not automatically composite. The practitioner must examine whether the accommodation is naturally bundled with the training service in the ordinary course and whether one element is clearly principal. GST authorities have applied this analysis in advance-ruling disputes involving coaching/training and hostel facilities. citeturn0search21

6.3 Goods + freight

Where freight is supplied as part of the ordinary delivery arrangement for goods, it may be ancillary to the principal supply. The contractual terms, invoicing pattern and commercial arrangement should be examined rather than applying a mechanical rule.

7. Mixed supply — Section 2(74)

A mixed supply is fundamentally different. Section 2(74) covers two or more individual supplies of goods or services, or any combination thereof, made in conjunction with each other by a taxable person for a single price where the supply does not constitute a composite supply.

Thus, a mixed supply is not merely “many items together”. The crucial features are:

  • two or more individual supplies;
  • supplied in conjunction with one another;
  • a single price; and
  • the arrangement does not qualify as a composite supply.

The statutory illustration is a package containing items such as canned foods, sweets, chocolates, cakes, dry fruits, aerated drinks and fruit juices sold for a single price. These items can ordinarily be supplied independently and are not dependent on one another. Therefore, where the package is not naturally bundled, it falls within the mixed-supply framework.

8. Section 8(b) — the highest-rate rule

Once an arrangement is established as a mixed supply, Section 8(b) provides a clear rule: the mixed supply is treated as a supply of the particular supply that attracts the highest rate of tax.

This rule is important because it prevents a supplier from reducing the GST treatment of a single-price package merely by combining a higher-tax component with lower-tax components.

Example: A single-price package contains three independently usable taxable products. Product A attracts 5%, Product B attracts 12% and Product C attracts 18%. If the package is a mixed supply, Section 8(b) applies the treatment of the component attracting the highest rate — 18%.
Important: “Highest rate” must be determined after correctly classifying the individual supplies and identifying the applicable rate for the relevant tax period. Do not compare rates before completing classification.

9. What if all components have the same rate?

Section 8(b) says the mixed supply is treated as the supply attracting the highest rate. If every component carries the same applicable GST rate, the highest rate is effectively the same rate across the components.

However, the classification exercise should still be properly documented because the nature of the individual supplies can matter for HSN/SAC reporting, invoicing, exemptions, valuation and future changes in rates.

Illustrative ruling approach: A Gujarat Authority for Advance Ruling matter considered a bundle where the components attracted the same 18% rate and observed that the mixed supply could be treated as a supply of any of those components because the applicable rate was identical. citeturn0search23

11. Important distinction: one contract does not automatically mean composite supply

A common accounting mistake is to look only at the contract or invoice and conclude that all components constitute one supply. The legal test is more nuanced.

A single agreement can contain separate supplies. Conversely, a package can be composite even if its invoice contains component-wise values. The decisive question is the statutory definition: natural bundling and principal supply for composite supply; single-price combination without natural bundling for mixed supply.

Audit point: When an auditor or tax officer challenges a bundled transaction, the taxpayer should be able to demonstrate why the components are naturally bundled or why they are independent supplies sold for a single price. The commercial evidence is often as important as the invoice itself.

12. GST Council background

Section 8 was part of the early GST legislative design. The draft GST framework presented to the GST Council expressly contained the two treatment rules that later became Section 8(a) and 8(b): composite supplies would follow the principal supply, while mixed supplies would follow the supply carrying the highest rate. citeturn0search1

The legislative design reflects a practical objective: where multiple taxable elements are commercially bundled, GST needs a single workable mechanism for determining the tax treatment of the bundle rather than forcing artificial fragmentation in every case.

The GST Council's later materials and advance-ruling decisions continue to apply the same statutory distinction between natural bundling and independent supplies sold together for a single price. citeturn0search20turn0search23

13. Amendment history and stability of Section 8

Unlike Section 7, Section 8 has retained a comparatively stable two-clause structure. The major legal developments affecting Section 8 have generally occurred through the surrounding definitions, rate notifications, classification provisions and judicial/advance-ruling interpretation rather than through a fundamental rewrite of the two operative rules in Section 8.

1 July 2017

Section 8 came into force with the CGST Act's main levy-and-collection provisions. Its basic architecture was composite supply → principal supply and mixed supply → highest-rate supply.

Post-2017 interpretation

Authorities and taxpayers have developed the application of Section 8 through the definitions in Section 2(30) and 2(74), GST rate notifications and case-specific rulings.

Current approach

The central legal test remains the same: establish whether the bundle is naturally bundled and has a principal supply; if not, examine whether it is a mixed supply and apply the highest-rate rule.

14. Practical examples

Example 1 — Machinery + packing + freight + insurance: Naturally bundled in the ordinary course, with machinery as the predominant element → composite supply → machinery treatment under Section 8(a).
Example 2 — Festival hamper: Chocolate, dry fruits, juice and decorative items independently usable, sold together for one price and not naturally bundled → mixed supply → highest applicable rate under Section 8(b).
Example 3 — Restaurant-style meal: Food and incidental services may form a bundled supply, but the specific statutory rate/exemption entry and applicable conditions must also be checked. Section 8 is not a substitute for the rate notification.
Example 4 — Software implementation: Software licence, implementation and support require examination of their contractual and functional relationship. The mere presence of several invoice lines does not decide composite vs separate supply.
Example 5 — Equipment with mandatory installation: If installation is ancillary to the equipment and naturally supplied with it, composite-supply analysis may be appropriate. If the installation/engineering service is the predominant commercial objective, the result may differ.
Example 6 — Gift basket with independent products: If each product can be independently purchased and the basket is merely assembled for a single price without natural bundling, mixed-supply treatment may arise.

15. Professional decision workflow for accounts and tax teams

  1. List every component. Do not start by assuming one supply.
  2. Classify each component separately. Determine whether each is goods, service, exempt, non-taxable or otherwise outside the taxable-supply framework.
  3. Check whether the components are supplied together.
  4. Test natural bundling. Examine ordinary industry practice, customer expectation, dependency and commercial purpose.
  5. Identify the predominant element. If one component is principal and the bundle is naturally bundled, apply composite-supply treatment.
  6. If not composite, test mixed supply. Check the single-price condition and independent nature of the components.
  7. Determine the highest applicable rate. For mixed supply, correctly classify each component before comparing rates.
  8. Check special rate/exemption notifications. Section 8 determines treatment but does not itself provide every GST rate.
  9. Document the conclusion. Keep the contract, quotation, invoice, product/service descriptions and commercial evidence supporting the conclusion.

16. Documentation checklist

  • Agreement / purchase order / work order.
  • Quotation and commercial proposal.
  • Customer-facing package description.
  • Invoice and component-wise values.
  • Industry practice or standard package documentation, where relevant.
  • Evidence showing whether components are optional or dependent.
  • Individual HSN/SAC classification and applicable rates.
  • Rate notification relied upon.
  • Internal tax position paper for material bundled transactions.
  • Relevant advance rulings or judicial decisions relied upon.

17. Common mistakes

  1. “One invoice = composite supply.” Not necessarily.
  2. “Single price = mixed supply.” Not necessarily. Natural bundling must first be tested.
  3. “Any two services together are composite.” Natural bundling and principal supply are required.
  4. “Highest-rate rule applies to every bundle.” It applies to a qualifying mixed supply, not a composite supply.
  5. “Principal supply means the highest-value item.” Not automatically. Principal supply is the predominant element, not simply the item with the highest price.
  6. “Separate invoice lines prevent composite supply.” Invoice presentation is evidence, not the statutory test.
  7. “Section 8 gives the GST rate.” It determines the treatment of the bundle; the actual applicable rate must be checked under the relevant rate/exemption notifications.

20. Research and professional disclaimer

Section 8 should be applied with Sections 2(30), 2(74), 2(90), Section 7, the applicable rate/exemption notifications and the facts of the particular transaction. Advance rulings can illustrate application but should not automatically be treated as universal rules for every taxpayer or every State.
