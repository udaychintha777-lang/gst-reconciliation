CGST Section 121 – Non-Appealable Decisions and Orders

Which GST decisions cannot be appealed? Section 121 of the CGST Act identifies four specific categories of decisions and orders of a Central Tax officer against which an appeal does not lie. This detailed guide explains each category in simple language, with practical examples for businesses, taxpayers, finance teams and GST professionals.

Bare Act

121 Non-appealable decisions and orders

Section 121: Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in any provisions of this Act, no appeal shall lie against any decision taken or order passed by an officer of central tax if such decision taken or order passed relates to any one or more of the following matters, namely:—

(a) an order of the Commissioner or other authority empowered to direct transfer of proceedings from one officer to another officer; or

(b) an order pertaining to the seizure or retention of books of account, register and other documents; or

(c) an order sanctioning prosecution under this Act; or

(d) an order passed under section 80.

Important: Section 121 does not say that every action connected with these subjects is immune from every possible legal remedy. It says that an appeal under the CGST Act does not lie against the specified decision or order. The precise remedy available in a particular case must be examined separately under the Act, Rules and applicable law.
Understand it like a normal person

What does Section 121 mean in simple language?

GST law generally gives a taxpayer a right to challenge certain orders through the GST appellate system. For example, a taxpayer may be able to appeal an adjudication order under Section 107 and proceed through the appellate hierarchy where the law permits.

But the law does not make every single decision made by a GST officer appealable under the GST appeal mechanism.

The simple idea behind Section 121: The CGST Act identifies four types of decisions/orders where a statutory GST appeal does not lie. These are mainly administrative, procedural or preliminary matters where the law has chosen not to create the normal appeal route against the particular order itself.

The four categories are:

ClauseSubjectEasy explanation
121(a) Transfer of proceedings An order transferring GST proceedings from one Central Tax officer to another authorised officer.
121(b) Seizure or retention of records An order concerning seizure or retention of books of account, registers or other documents.
121(c) Sanctioning prosecution An order by which prosecution under the CGST Act is sanctioned.
121(d) Section 80 order An order passed under Section 80, which deals with payment of tax and other amounts in instalments in specified circumstances.
Memory trick: Transfer → Records → Prosecution → Instalments.
Why this provision exists

Why are some GST orders non-appealable?

Imagine that every administrative decision made during a GST proceeding could immediately be taken through the complete appellate hierarchy. A dispute about which officer handles a case, whether seized records should be retained during an investigation, or whether prosecution should be sanctioned could potentially create a separate appellate chain before the underlying GST proceedings are completed.

Section 121 draws a statutory line. For the four specified categories, the CGST Act says that a GST appeal does not lie.

But does “non-appealable” mean “the taxpayer has no rights at all”?

No such broad conclusion should be drawn from Section 121 alone.

There is an important difference between:

GST appeal Statutory appellate route Section 121 says an appeal under the CGST Act does not lie against the specified decisions/orders.
Other legal remedy Separate question Whether another legal remedy, procedural challenge, judicial review or other relief may be available depends on the facts and the applicable law.
Do not read “no appeal” as “no remedy under any law”. Section 121 is specifically about the statutory GST appeal route. A lawyer should examine the exact action, jurisdiction, procedural history and any other remedy that may legally be available.
Four categories

Understanding all four non-appealable decisions

121(a) – Transfer of proceedings

This covers an order of the Commissioner or another authority empowered to direct transfer of proceedings from one officer to another officer.

Layman meaning: Suppose a GST proceeding is currently being handled by Officer A. An authorised higher authority directs that the proceeding should be handled by Officer B. Section 121 says that the transfer order itself is not appealable under the GST appeal mechanism.

121(b) – Seizure or retention of books and documents

This covers an order pertaining to the seizure or retention of books of account, registers and other documents.

Layman meaning: During a GST investigation or proceeding, officers may take custody of or retain records in accordance with the law. Section 121 says the order concerning that seizure or retention is not appealable under the normal GST appeal route.

121(c) – Sanctioning prosecution

This covers an order sanctioning prosecution under the CGST Act.

Layman meaning: Where the competent authority passes an order sanctioning prosecution under GST law, Section 121 says that sanctioning order itself is not appealable through the CGST appellate hierarchy.

121(d) – Order under Section 80

This covers an order passed under Section 80.

Layman meaning: Section 80 deals with allowing payment of tax or other amounts payable under the Act in instalments, subject to the conditions of that section. Section 121 specifically says an order passed under Section 80 is not appealable under the CGST Act.

Clause 121(a)

Transfer of proceedings from one GST officer to another

The first category concerns transfer of proceedings.

GST administration may require a proceeding to be handled by a different officer. The CGST Act therefore recognises powers of the Commissioner or another empowered authority to direct transfer of proceedings from one officer to another.

Simple example

ABC Private Limited has a GST proceeding being handled by Central Tax Officer A. An empowered authority issues an order transferring that proceeding to Officer B.

The company may have preferences about which officer handles the case, but Section 121(a) says that the transfer order itself is not appealable under the CGST appeal mechanism.

Why might proceedings be transferred?

The reason can depend on the administrative circumstances. For example, jurisdictional reorganisation, administrative allocation, specialised handling or other authorised reasons may lead to a proceeding being transferred.

The important point for the taxpayer is that the transfer of the proceeding does not itself decide the underlying GST liability.

Very important distinction: A transfer order and a tax demand order are two different things. Section 121(a) concerns the transfer decision; it does not mean that the taxpayer loses the normal appeal rights that may exist against a separate adjudication order determining tax, interest or penalty.

What should the taxpayer do?

  • Obtain a copy of the transfer order.
  • Update the GST litigation/contact records with the new officer.
  • Check whether any hearing date or submission deadline is approaching.
  • Preserve all submissions already filed in the proceeding.
  • Do not confuse the transfer order with the final tax determination.
Clause 121(b)

Seizure or retention of books, registers and other documents

The second category concerns an order pertaining to the seizure or retention of books of account, registers and other documents.

This provision becomes particularly relevant during GST investigation, inspection, search or related proceedings where business records may be taken into custody or retained in accordance with the statutory framework.

What are “books of account” and “documents” in practical terms?

For a business, records can include accounting records, ledgers, invoices, purchase and sales records, registers, supporting documents, electronic records and other material relevant to the GST proceeding. The exact scope depends on the applicable provision and circumstances.

Example – construction company

Suppose a construction company is undergoing a GST investigation. Officers take custody of specified accounting records and supporting documents relevant to the investigation. An order concerning seizure or retention of those records is passed.

Section 121(b) means the taxpayer cannot use the ordinary CGST appeal mechanism to appeal that seizure/retention order itself.

Do not assume that “non-appealable” means “the department can keep records indefinitely without following the law.” The legality of the underlying action, statutory authority, procedural safeguards and any other available remedy are separate questions.

Clause 121(c)

Order sanctioning prosecution under GST

The third category is an order sanctioning prosecution under the CGST Act.

GST law contains criminal prosecution provisions for specified offences. Section 121(c) says that an order sanctioning prosecution is not appealable through the CGST appellate hierarchy.

What does “sanctioning prosecution” mean?

In simple terms, prosecution is a criminal-law process rather than the ordinary tax-assessment appeal process. A sanctioning order is part of the process that permits prosecution to be initiated in accordance with the applicable law.

Think of the distinction this way:
A GST demand dispute asks questions such as “How much tax is payable?”
A prosecution process can involve a separate question: “Has an offence been committed for which criminal prosecution is being initiated?”

Example

Suppose an investigation alleges conduct falling within an offence provision of the CGST Act. After the applicable statutory process, a competent authority sanctions prosecution.

The sanction order itself is within Section 121(c). Therefore, the ordinary GST appeal route is not available against that sanctioning order.

Why this distinction matters

A company should not mix its GST demand appeal file with its prosecution response file. They can arise from the same factual background but have different legal consequences and procedural tracks.

Tax proceedingProsecution proceeding
May concern tax, interest, penalty and related liabilitiesConcerns alleged offences and criminal prosecution
Uses GST appellate provisions where applicableUses criminal/prosecution procedure applicable to the case
Demand order may be appealable depending on the provisionSanctioning order is non-appealable under Section 121(c)
Clause 121(d)

Order passed under Section 80

The fourth category concerns an order passed under Section 80.

Section 80 is the provision dealing with payment of tax and other amounts payable under the Act in instalments, subject to its statutory conditions.

Why would a taxpayer need instalments?

Imagine a business receives a substantial GST liability and, under the conditions of Section 80, seeks permission to pay the amount in instalments instead of making the entire payment at once.

The authority may pass an order under Section 80 in relation to that request. Section 121(d) specifically makes an order passed under Section 80 non-appealable under the CGST appeal mechanism.

Simple example

A company has a large GST amount payable and applies for an instalment facility under Section 80. The competent authority passes an order dealing with the instalment arrangement. Because the order is an order under Section 80, Section 121(d) says that an appeal does not lie against that order under the CGST Act.

Important: Do not confuse the underlying tax demand with the Section 80 instalment order. A tax demand may have its own statutory appeal route, while the specific Section 80 order is covered by Section 121(d).

Finance-team checklist for Section 80 matters

  • Record total amount payable.
  • Record the instalment schedule specified in the order.
  • Track every due date.
  • Maintain proof of every payment.
  • Check the consequences of missing an instalment.
  • Keep the underlying demand order and Section 80 order in separate but linked files.
Very important distinction

“Non-appealable” does not mean “nothing can be challenged”

This is probably the most important practical lesson from Section 121.

The wording of Section 121 is specific: “no appeal shall lie” against the listed decisions/orders under the CGST appellate framework.

That is different from saying that every action surrounding those decisions is automatically beyond all judicial scrutiny.

QuestionSection 121 answer
Can I file a normal GST appeal against the specified Section 121 order?No, the section says no appeal lies against the specified decision/order.
Does that automatically mean every action connected with it is lawful?No. That conclusion does not follow from Section 121 alone.
Could another legal remedy exist?Possibly, depending on facts, jurisdiction, statutory provisions and applicable procedural law.
Should a taxpayer assume a High Court remedy is automatically available?No. The exact facts and legal basis must be examined by counsel.
For public readers: If you see “no appeal lies” in Section 121, do not interpret it as “you have no legal rights.” It means the ordinary statutory GST appeal route is barred for the specified decision/order. The appropriate next legal step depends on the exact facts.
Detailed practical examples

Section 121 examples for businesses and GST professionals

Example 1 – Officer transfer

A GST audit is being handled by one officer. The Commissioner directs that the proceeding be transferred to another officer. The taxpayer is unhappy because it prefers the original officer.

Section 121(a): The transfer order itself is not appealable under the CGST appeal mechanism.

Practical response: Cooperate with the properly authorised proceeding, update the records and focus the substantive defence on the actual GST issues.

Example 2 – Records retained during investigation

GST officers retain specified books and documents relevant to an investigation. The taxpayer wants to appeal the retention order through the GST appellate hierarchy.

Section 121(b): The seizure/retention order is among the specified non-appealable orders.

Practical response: Obtain documentation of what was taken/retained, preserve copies where possible and obtain legal advice on any other remedy or procedural request that may be available.

Example 3 – Prosecution sanction

A company receives a prosecution sanction order following a GST investigation.

Section 121(c): The sanctioning order is not appealable under the CGST appellate mechanism.

Practical response: Treat the criminal/prosecution matter as a separate legal track and obtain appropriate legal representation.

Example 4 – Instalment order

A taxpayer seeks payment of GST dues in instalments under Section 80 and receives an order dealing with the instalment arrangement.

Section 121(d): The Section 80 order itself is non-appealable under the CGST Act.

Practical response: Track the instalments precisely and separately analyse any issue concerning the underlying tax demand.

Example 5 – Underlying demand versus Section 80 order

A company has a ₹5 crore GST demand. The company challenges the demand through the applicable appeal route and separately obtains an instalment arrangement under Section 80.

The two matters should not be merged. The demand challenge concerns the underlying liability. The Section 80 order concerns the payment arrangement. Section 121(d) applies specifically to the Section 80 order.

Example 6 – Same investigation, different legal tracks

A GST investigation results in a tax demand and a prosecution sanction. The company should not assume that because one order is appealable, the prosecution sanction automatically becomes appealable through the same route.

Section 121(c) specifically excludes the prosecution-sanctioning order from the CGST appeal mechanism.

Example 7 – Transfer followed by adjudication

A proceeding is transferred from Officer A to Officer B. Later, Officer B passes an adjudication order creating a tax demand.

The transfer order and the adjudication order are separate decisions. Section 121(a) concerns the transfer order. The later demand order must be analysed independently to determine the applicable appeal rights.

Business guidance

What should a business do when a Section 121 matter arises?

A non-appealable order should not simply be marked “closed” in the company's tax tracker. The correct approach is to identify exactly what order was passed, what legal consequence it has and what other proceedings may continue.

1. Identify the exact order Record the section, authority, order number, date and subject.
2. Identify why it is non-appealable Map the order to Section 121(a), (b), (c) or (d).
3. Separate the underlying dispute A non-appealable procedural order may exist alongside an appealable tax demand.
4. Obtain legal advice If the business believes the action is unlawful or seriously prejudicial, ask counsel to examine the appropriate legal route.

Documents to preserve

  • Original order and all annexures.
  • Notices and communications leading to the order.
  • Replies and submissions filed by the taxpayer.
  • Relevant inspection/search/investigation records.
  • GST portal acknowledgements and correspondence.
  • Details of the underlying tax period and disputed transactions.
  • Legal opinion or counsel's advice where obtained.
  • Dates of subsequent proceedings.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions on Section 121 of the CGST Act

1. What is Section 121 of the CGST Act?

Section 121 is the provision dealing with non-appealable decisions and orders. It states that no appeal lies against four specified categories of decisions/orders of a Central Tax officer.

2. How many categories are listed in Section 121?

Four categories are listed: transfer of proceedings, seizure or retention of books/registers/documents, sanctioning of prosecution, and an order passed under Section 80.

3. Can a taxpayer appeal an order transferring GST proceedings to another officer?

No. Section 121(a) specifically includes an order directing transfer of proceedings from one officer to another among the non-appealable decisions.

4. Can a taxpayer file a GST appeal against seizure or retention of books?

The order pertaining to seizure or retention of books of account, registers and other documents is covered by Section 121(b), so an appeal under the CGST appellate mechanism does not lie against that order.

5. Can a prosecution sanction order be appealed under GST?

No. Section 121(c) specifically makes an order sanctioning prosecution under the CGST Act non-appealable under the GST appeal mechanism.

6. Can an order under Section 80 be appealed?

Section 121(d) says that an order passed under Section 80 is non-appealable under the CGST Act.

7. What is Section 80 of the CGST Act?

Section 80 deals with payment of tax and other amounts payable under the Act in instalments in specified circumstances. A specific order passed under Section 80 is covered by Section 121(d).

8. Does Section 121 mean the taxpayer has absolutely no legal remedy?

Section 121 means that the specified decision/order cannot be challenged through the ordinary appeal route under the CGST Act. Whether another legal remedy exists is a separate question depending on the facts and applicable law.

9. If a proceeding is transferred, can the later tax demand be appealed?

The transfer order and the later tax demand are separate decisions. Section 121(a) concerns the transfer order. The later demand must be independently examined under the applicable GST appeal provisions.

10. If records are seized, does Section 121 prevent all requests for their return?

Section 121 addresses the availability of a GST appeal against the seizure/retention order. It should not automatically be interpreted as answering every separate question concerning custody, statutory compliance or return of records.

11. Does Section 121 apply to every order passed by a GST officer?

No. It applies to the four categories specifically listed in the section. Other decisions/orders must be examined under their respective provisions.

12. Why is the exact wording of the order important?

Because a single GST investigation can generate several different orders. Section 121 may make one procedural order non-appealable while a separate substantive tax order may have its own appeal route.
