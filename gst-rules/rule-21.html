Rule 21 – Registration to be Cancelled in Certain Cases

A practical guide to the circumstances in which a GST registration becomes liable to cancellation. Understand non-existent business premises, invoices without supply, Rule 10A, wrongful ITC, GSTR-1 versus GSTR-3B differences, Rule 86B and the compliance controls that can prevent cancellation proceedings.

1. What is Rule 21?

Rule 21 identifies circumstances in which a GST registration granted to a person is liable to be cancelled. It is therefore an important enforcement provision and should be read with section 29 and the cancellation procedure under the subsequent rules.

Simple meaning: Rule 21 does not merely describe administrative cancellation. It identifies specific conduct or compliance failures that can expose an existing GST registration to cancellation proceedings.

For management, the rule is especially important because several grounds are connected with actual business operations, invoicing, ITC, return reconciliation and GST payment controls.

3. Rule 21(a) – No business from the declared place

The registration is liable to cancellation where the registered person does not conduct any business from the declared place of business.

What can create risk?

  • Premises does not exist at the declared address.
  • Business has shifted without updating registration.
  • Site inspection finds no business activity and the taxpayer cannot substantiate the registered premises.
  • Address documents and actual occupation are inconsistent.
Practical point: A company may have legitimate reasons for temporary inactivity at a location. The issue under Rule 21(a) is whether business is actually conducted from the declared place, so maintain evidence supporting the actual business arrangement.

4. Rule 21(b) – Invoice without actual supply

Issuing an invoice or bill without a corresponding supply of goods or services, in violation of GST law, is a specific cancellation ground.

This is particularly serious because invoice-only transactions can affect the recipient's ITC and can create a chain of tax exposure across multiple taxpayers.

Examples of high-risk behaviour

  • Invoice issued where no goods were dispatched.
  • Service invoice issued without the underlying service.
  • Accommodation entries created only to facilitate ITC.
  • Billing through a non-operational entity without actual supply.
Do not confuse a genuine advance, credit note, milestone billing or other legitimate transaction with a fake invoice automatically. The actual contractual and supply facts must be examined.

5. Rule 21(c) – Section 171 violation

Rule 21 includes violation of section 171 of the CGST Act or the rules made under it as a ground for cancellation.

Section 171 historically dealt with passing on the benefit of reduction in tax rate or input tax credit to the recipient through commensurate reduction in prices and the related anti-profiteering framework. The legal framework has evolved over time, so any departmental action should be evaluated against the law applicable to the relevant period.

6. Rule 21(d) – Violation of Rule 10A

Rule 10A deals with furnishing bank account details after registration. Rule 21 makes violation of Rule 10A a cancellation ground.

Finance-team control: Maintain a registration master that records whether the bank-account requirement has been completed and preserve the supporting confirmation.

This is a good example of why GST registration compliance does not end when REG-06 is issued. Post-registration obligations can affect the continued validity of the registration.

7. Rule 21(e) – Wrongful ITC

Rule 21 makes availing input tax credit in violation of section 16 or the rules a ground for cancellation.

ITC controls should therefore cover:

  • valid tax invoice/document;
  • actual receipt of goods or services;
  • supplier and invoice verification;
  • 2B/reconciliation controls where relevant;
  • blocked-credit review;
  • business-purpose testing;
  • payment-related conditions; and
  • reversal and correction mechanisms.
For businesses using a GST reconciliation system, the ITC ledger should be linked to invoice-level purchase records and GST portal data so that exceptions are identified before they become a departmental issue.

8. Rule 21(f) – GSTR-1 versus section 39 return

This is one of the most important modern GST cancellation grounds.

If outward supplies reported in GSTR-1 for one or more tax periods exceed the outward supplies declared in the valid return furnished under section 39 for those periods, the registration can become liable to cancellation.

For a normal monthly taxpayer, the section 39 return will generally be reflected through GSTR-3B. Therefore, a material GSTR-1/GSTR-3B mismatch should never be treated as a routine reconciliation difference.

Example

ParticularAmount
Taxable outward supplies in GSTR-1₹1.00 crore
Outward supplies declared in valid section 39 return₹80 lakh
Difference₹20 lakh

The business should immediately identify whether the difference arises from amendments, timing, credit notes, reporting errors, duplicate invoices, classification or an actual under-declaration in the section 39 return.

9. Rule 21(g) – Rule 86B

Rule 86B places restrictions on utilisation of the electronic credit ledger in specified circumstances and generally requires a portion of output tax liability to be paid through the electronic cash ledger, subject to its exceptions.

Rule 21 makes violation of Rule 86B a cancellation ground.

Practical lesson: Businesses that fall within Rule 86B should maintain a monthly control showing whether the rule applies, which exception is relied upon if applicable, and how the payment requirement was satisfied.

10. Rule 21 is a “liable to cancellation” provision

The wording of Rule 21 is important. It identifies circumstances in which registration is liable to be cancelled. It should not be read as saying that every alleged discrepancy automatically means the GSTIN disappears immediately without the prescribed cancellation procedure.

The proper officer follows the applicable notice, reply, hearing and cancellation-order framework under the subsequent rules.

Practical distinction: Rule 21 = grounds/risk. Rule 22 = cancellation procedure. Rule 21A = suspension in specified circumstances.

12. Departmental notice – how management should respond

If a cancellation notice is received, management should not respond with a generic statement such as “there is no default.” The response should address the precise Rule 21 ground identified by the officer.

Recommended response file

  • notice and registration details;
  • reconciliation of the disputed transactions;
  • books and ledgers;
  • invoices and contracts;
  • bank statements where relevant;
  • GSTR-1/GSTR-3B reconciliation;
  • ITC reconciliation and reversal workings;
  • premises evidence for address-related allegations;
  • Rule 10A compliance proof; and
  • Rule 86B computation/payment/exception evidence where relevant.

13. Example – construction company

A construction company has multiple project sites and one GST registration. During physical verification, the department alleges that the declared principal place is not functioning as stated.

The company should establish the facts through:

  • rent/ownership documents;
  • electricity or utility records;
  • employee/site records;
  • books and records maintained at the premises;
  • project contracts;
  • correspondence received at the address; and
  • photographic/site evidence where appropriate.

The company should separately explain project-site locations rather than assuming that activity at another project site disproves the declared principal place.

14. Example – GSTR-1/GSTR-3B difference

A company discovers that GSTR-1 reports ₹12 crore of outward supplies while the corresponding GSTR-3B reporting for the same periods totals ₹11.5 crore.

Before the department identifies the difference, the finance team should reconcile:

  1. invoice-wise GSTR-1;
  2. GSTR-3B outward taxable values;
  3. credit/debit notes;
  4. amendments;
  5. advances where relevant;
  6. zero-rated/exempt/non-GST supplies;
  7. period shifts; and
  8. any duplicate reporting.

If there is an actual under-reporting, the tax and interest consequences should be corrected rather than merely explaining the mismatch as an “ERP difference.”

15. Example – ITC issue

A taxpayer claims ITC based on invoices that appear in its purchase register but the underlying goods/services were not actually received.

Such a case can create a Rule 21(e) risk because the ITC may violate section 16. The finance team should have a three-way control between purchase order/contract, receipt/service evidence and tax invoice, supplemented by GST portal reconciliation.

17. ERP and finance controls

  • Maintain a GSTIN-wise registration compliance dashboard.
  • Perform monthly GSTR-1 versus GSTR-3B reconciliation.
  • Run invoice-level sales exception reports.
  • Maintain ITC eligibility and reversal controls.
  • Track Rule 10A bank-account compliance.
  • Maintain Rule 86B applicability/payment or exception evidence.
  • Keep a place-of-business master linked to each GSTIN.
  • Escalate unusual invoice-only transactions before filing returns.

18. Common mistakes under Rule 21

  1. Ignoring a declared-address mismatch.
  2. Issuing invoices without checking actual supply evidence.
  3. Treating every ITC appearing in books as automatically eligible.
  4. Not reconciling GSTR-1 with GSTR-3B monthly.
  5. Ignoring Rule 10A.
  6. Failing to document Rule 86B exceptions.
  7. Assuming Rule 21 itself cancels registration instantly.
  8. Submitting a generic reply to a cancellation notice.
  9. Not preserving evidence supporting genuine transactions.

20. Preventive compliance checklist

  • ☐ Actual business conducted at declared premises.
  • ☐ GST registration addresses updated when required.
  • ☐ No invoice issued without genuine underlying supply.
  • ☐ Rule 10A bank-account requirement completed.
  • ☐ ITC eligibility reviewed under section 16.
  • ☐ Purchase/2B/ITC reconciliation performed.
  • ☐ GSTR-1 versus GSTR-3B reconciliation performed every period.
  • ☐ Rule 86B applicability reviewed monthly.
  • ☐ Exceptions and supporting documents preserved.
  • ☐ Cancellation notices monitored and responded to promptly.

21. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Rule 21 automatically cancel GST registration?

No. Rule 21 identifies cases where registration is liable to cancellation. The prescribed cancellation procedure must be followed.

Can GST registration be cancelled if business is not operating from the declared address?

Yes. Rule 21(a) specifically identifies not conducting business from the declared place as a cancellation ground.

Can fake invoices lead to GST cancellation?

Yes. Issuing an invoice or bill without an underlying supply in violation of GST law is specifically covered by Rule 21(b).

Can wrongful ITC lead to cancellation?

Yes. Rule 21(e) covers ITC availed in violation of section 16 or the rules.

Can GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B differences cause cancellation risk?

Yes. Rule 21(f) specifically addresses excess outward supplies reported in GSTR-1 compared with the valid section 39 return for the relevant periods.

What is the importance of Rule 86B under Rule 21?

Violation of Rule 86B is expressly included as a cancellation ground under Rule 21(g).

Is Rule 21 the same as Rule 21A?

No. Rule 21 identifies cancellation grounds, while Rule 21A deals with suspension in specified circumstances pending cancellation proceedings.

23. Practical takeaway

Rule 21 is an enforcement warning list for GST registrations. The most important preventive controls are genuine business at declared premises, genuine supplies against invoices, correct ITC, clean GSTR-1/GSTR-3B reconciliation, Rule 10A compliance and Rule 86B compliance. If a cancellation notice is received, respond to the exact Rule 21 ground with evidence and reconciliation rather than a generic explanation.
