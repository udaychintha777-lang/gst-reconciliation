Section 162 of the CGST Act, 2017

Bar on jurisdiction of civil courts. Understand when ordinary civil courts cannot decide questions arising from or relating to actions under the CGST Act, and the limited statutory exceptions preserved by Sections 117 and 118.

1. Section 162 at a Glance

Core ruleOrdinary civil courts generally cannot decide questions arising from or relating to anything done or purported to be done under the CGST Act.
PurposeThe provision channels GST disputes through the specialised statutory mechanisms created by the GST law rather than an ordinary civil suit.
ExceptionsThe bar itself preserves the matters covered by Sections 117 and 118, which concern appeals to the High Court and Supreme Court.
In simple language: If a dispute is fundamentally about an action taken or purportedly taken under the CGST Act, the first question is usually which GST statutory remedy applies—not whether an ordinary civil suit can be filed.

3. Statutory Text of Section 162

162. Bar on jurisdiction of civil courts.— Save as provided in sections 117 and 118, no civil court shall have jurisdiction to deal with or decide any question arising from or relating to anything done or purported to be done under this Act.
Key point: Section 162 is a jurisdictional bar. It does not itself decide whether a taxpayer wins or loses a GST dispute; it identifies the forum in which questions arising from GST action are to be dealt with, subject to the statutory exceptions.

4. Meaning in Simple Language

GST is a self-contained statutory framework with its own officers, adjudication procedures, appellate authorities and higher-court appeal provisions. Section 162 prevents an ordinary civil court from deciding questions arising from or relating to something done or purportedly done under the CGST Act.

The expression “done or purported to be done” is important. The section is not limited only to actions that are ultimately found to be correct. It also refers to acts purportedly done under the Act.

Practical idea: When a GST authority issues an assessment, demand, order, recovery action or other statutory action, first identify the remedy provided by GST law. Do not assume that an ordinary civil suit is the default route.

What Section 162 does not mean

  • It does not mean that GST authorities are immune from every form of judicial review.
  • It does not eliminate statutory appeals or constitutional remedies that may otherwise be available.
  • It does not convert every dispute between two private parties into a GST dispute.
  • It does not itself determine the merits of a tax demand.

5. What Does the Civil-Court Bar Cover?

The statutory language is broad: “any question arising from or relating to anything done or purported to be done under this Act.” In practical GST work, the first task is therefore to identify the source and nature of the disputed action.

SituationInitial questionLikely GST-law focus
Assessment or demandWas the action taken under GST determination provisions?Reply, adjudication and statutory appeal framework.
Penalty orderIs the penalty imposed under the CGST Act?Applicable adjudication and appeal provisions.
Registration-related orderWas the decision made under GST registration provisions?Relevant GST order and appellate mechanism.
Refund disputeIs the dispute about a statutory GST refund decision?Relevant refund procedure and statutory remedies.
Recovery actionIs the action a GST recovery measure?Applicable recovery provisions and statutory challenge.
Pure private contract disputeDoes the dispute arise independently from GST statutory action?The GST civil-court bar should not automatically be assumed to cover a purely private dispute.

6. Why Sections 117 and 118 Are Mentioned

Section 162 begins with the words “Save as provided in sections 117 and 118”. These are important statutory exceptions within the GST appellate architecture.

Section 117Provides for an appeal to the High Court from certain orders of the Appellate Tribunal on a substantial question of law, subject to the conditions of that section.
Section 118Provides for an appeal to the Supreme Court from specified orders of the Appellate Tribunal, subject to the conditions of that section.
Section 162Preserves the statutory higher-court routes while barring ordinary civil-court jurisdiction over questions covered by the section.
Important: Sections 117 and 118 are not general permissions to file ordinary civil suits. They are specialised appellate provisions within the GST Act.

8. Civil Disputes vs GST Disputes

Not every matter involving a GST reference is automatically a “GST dispute” for Section 162 purposes. A company may have a contract, debt, property, service or commercial dispute in which GST is only one component of the background.

ExampleHow to analyse
GST officer passes a tax demand orderThe dispute arises directly from statutory GST action; examine the GST appellate framework.
Two companies dispute payment under a supply contractThe underlying private contractual dispute may have its own civil/commercial remedy, even though GST appears in invoices.
Supplier and recipient disagree over GST amount chargedSeparate the contractual/payment dispute from any challenge to a GST authority's statutory order.
Taxpayer challenges a GST recovery actionIdentify the statutory recovery mechanism and available GST/legal remedies.
Do not classify by label alone: the fact that a dispute mentions GST does not by itself answer the jurisdiction question. The source and legal character of the disputed action must be examined.

9. Practical Examples

Example 1 – GST demand order

A registered person receives an adjudication order creating a tax demand under the CGST Act. The taxpayer should first examine the statutory GST appeal route rather than assuming an ordinary civil suit is available.

Example 2 – Penalty order

A penalty is imposed under a CGST provision. The challenge concerns the statutory action itself, so the relevant GST adjudication and appellate provisions need to be considered.

Example 3 – Recovery action

A taxpayer disputes a recovery step taken under GST law. The finance team should identify the specific recovery provision and the statutory remedies applicable to that action.

Example 4 – Registration cancellation

A GST registration is cancelled by an authority under the GST framework. The taxpayer should examine the applicable GST order and appeal/revocation mechanism rather than treating the matter as an ordinary civil suit.

Example 5 – Refund order

A refund application is rejected under GST law. The taxpayer should identify the statutory GST remedy and limitation period applicable to that order.

Example 6 – Private contract dispute

A contractor and customer dispute whether an invoice amount is payable under their contract. Even though the invoice contains GST, the underlying contractual dispute is not automatically transformed into a dispute over an act done under the CGST Act.

Example 7 – Supplier-recipient ITC disagreement

A buyer and supplier disagree commercially over who should bear a GST-related amount. The parties should distinguish their private contractual rights from any statutory action taken by a GST authority.

Example 8 – High Court appeal

A taxpayer reaches a stage where a statutory appeal to the High Court is available under Section 117 on a substantial question of law. Section 162 expressly preserves the Section 117 route.

Example 9 – Supreme Court route

A matter falls within Section 118's statutory appeal framework. Section 162's opening words preserve that route rather than directing the matter to an ordinary civil suit.

Example 10 – GST mentioned only as background

A commercial dispute includes tax invoices and GST calculations, but no GST authority has passed the disputed action. The jurisdiction analysis should begin with the actual cause of action rather than the presence of GST terminology.

Example 11 – Multiple GSTIN company

A company has several registrations and one GSTIN receives an adjudication order. The case tracker should identify the exact GSTIN, order number, tax period and statutory remedy.

Example 12 – Do not miss limitation

A taxpayer believes a civil suit may be available and delays filing the GST appeal. This can create avoidable procedural risk. Statutory GST limitation should be checked immediately.

11. Litigation & Finance Workflow

  1. Identify the disputed action: obtain the notice, order, recovery communication or other document.
  2. Identify the statutory source: record the CGST section/rule under which the action was taken.
  3. Identify the authority: record the officer, appellate authority or Tribunal involved.
  4. Classify the dispute: distinguish statutory GST action from a private contractual/commercial dispute.
  5. Check the GST remedy: determine whether reply, rectification, appeal, revision or another statutory route applies.
  6. Check limitation immediately: record the last date for the relevant remedy.
  7. Consider higher-court route: where applicable, examine Sections 117 and 118.
  8. Do not rely on a civil suit assumption: Section 162 may bar ordinary civil-court jurisdiction over the GST statutory question.
  9. Preserve the record: maintain notices, orders, acknowledgements, portal downloads, replies, appeal forms and payment/pre-deposit records.
For large companies: maintain a litigation matrix by GSTIN, tax period, disputed provision, order date, forum, limitation date, amount in dispute, pre-deposit and current status.

12. Common Mistakes

  • Assuming every GST-related dispute can be filed as an ordinary civil suit.
  • Ignoring the statutory GST appeal mechanism.
  • Failing to identify the exact statutory action being challenged.
  • Confusing a private contractual dispute with a challenge to a GST authority's order.
  • Missing the GST appeal limitation period while considering another forum.
  • Assuming Section 162 blocks every form of constitutional or higher-court remedy.
  • Failing to distinguish Sections 117 and 118 from ordinary civil-court proceedings.
  • Not maintaining the original GST order and service evidence.
  • Failing to identify the exact GSTIN and tax period in a group-company litigation tracker.

14. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Section 162 of the CGST Act?

It bars civil courts from having jurisdiction to deal with or decide questions arising from or relating to anything done or purported to be done under the CGST Act, subject to Sections 117 and 118.

2. Does Section 162 mean GST officers cannot be challenged?

No. It is a jurisdictional provision concerning ordinary civil-court jurisdiction. GST law itself provides statutory remedies, and other legal remedies may depend on the nature and circumstances of the case.

3. Can I file a civil suit against a GST assessment order?

Section 162 creates a bar where the question arises from or relates to an act done or purportedly done under the CGST Act. The applicable GST appellate route should be examined.

4. Does Section 162 apply to every dispute involving GST?

Not automatically. The nature and source of the disputed action matter. A purely private contractual dispute is not automatically a challenge to an act done under the CGST Act.

5. What are the exceptions mentioned in Section 162?

The provision expressly says “Save as provided in sections 117 and 118”, preserving those specified High Court and Supreme Court appellate routes.

6. What is Section 117?

Section 117 provides a statutory appeal to the High Court from specified Appellate Tribunal orders on a substantial question of law, subject to its conditions.

7. What is Section 118?

Section 118 provides the specified Supreme Court appeal route from Appellate Tribunal orders, subject to its statutory conditions.

8. Is Section 162 the same as Section 160?

No. Section 160 concerns validity of proceedings despite certain mistakes, defects or omissions and specified service challenges. Section 162 concerns civil-court jurisdiction.

9. Is Section 162 the same as Section 161?

No. Section 161 provides rectification of an error apparent on the face of the record. Section 162 deals with civil-court jurisdiction.

10. What if a contract dispute includes GST?

Separate the private contractual cause of action from any statutory GST action. The presence of GST in an invoice does not by itself determine the jurisdiction question.

11. Can a civil court decide a dispute between a supplier and customer about payment?

The answer depends on the actual cause of action and the relief sought. A private commercial dispute is analytically different from a challenge to an act done under the CGST Act.

12. Can Section 162 prevent a civil court from deciding a GST authority's recovery action?

Where the question arises from or relates to the GST statutory action, Section 162 may be relevant. The specific recovery provision and available statutory remedies should be examined.

13. Does Section 162 remove High Court jurisdiction?

Section 162 expressly preserves Section 117. It does not itself erase the High Court appellate route provided by the GST Act.

14. Does Section 162 remove Supreme Court jurisdiction?

Section 162 expressly preserves Section 118, subject to that provision's conditions.

15. Should a taxpayer delay filing a GST appeal while considering a civil suit?

No. Applicable GST limitation periods should be checked immediately. A taxpayer should not assume that exploring another forum extends the statutory GST appeal period.

16. Does Section 162 decide the merits of a GST case?

No. It concerns jurisdiction of civil courts, not whether the underlying GST demand or action is legally correct on its merits.

17. Is Section 162 relevant to GST litigation trackers?

Yes. It helps finance and legal teams identify the statutory GST forum and avoid treating ordinary civil litigation as the default remedy for a GST statutory action.

18. Is this page legal advice?

No. It is an educational explanation. The current Act, Rules, notifications, judicial decisions and precise facts should be reviewed before taking litigation action.

15. Quick Revision – Section 162

  • Section 162 = Bar on jurisdiction of civil courts.
  • Ordinary civil courts cannot deal with or decide questions arising from or relating to anything done or purported to be done under the CGST Act.
  • “Done or purported to be done” is broad statutory language.
  • The opening words preserve Sections 117 and 118.
  • Section 117 relates to specified High Court appeals on substantial questions of law.
  • Section 118 relates to specified Supreme Court appeals.
  • Section 162 does not itself decide the merits of a GST dispute.
  • A private commercial dispute mentioning GST is not automatically the same as a challenge to GST statutory action.
  • Always identify the exact order/action, statutory remedy and limitation period before choosing a forum.
